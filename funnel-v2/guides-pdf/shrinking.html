How to use this guide

If you’re the kind of person who gets taken advantage of, there’s a good chance you talk in a way that makes it easy. Not on purpose. Most of us picked up these habits because they made us seem polite, easygoing, and no trouble to anyone. “Sorry to bother you.” “I just wanted to check.” “Is that okay?”

Each of these phrases is small. Together they send a clear message: my needs come last, and you can talk me out of this. People hear that message, even when they don’t realize they’re hearing it.

This guide has two parts.

Part 1 is 24 common phrases that make you sound smaller than you are, with a plain alternative for each. You don’t have to stop being polite or warm. You only have to stop apologizing for having a point.

Part 2 turns things around. It’s 10 signs that someone may be pushing on you right now, what each one feels like from the inside, and what to do when you notice it.

Don’t try to fix all 24 phrases at once. Read the list, mark the three you say most, and work on one at a time. Most people find that a few small swaps change how they’re treated faster than they expected.

Part 1: 24 phrases that make you sound small

The phrases are grouped into four kinds. For each one you’ll see the phrase, a plainer way to say the same thing, and why the swap helps.

Openers that apologize for being there

The first few words set the tone. These openers tell the other person you don’t think you have the right to ask.

1. “Sorry to bother you”

Instead of: “Sorry to bother you, but could you look at this report?”

Say: “Could you look at this report by Thursday?”

Why: Apologizing for an ordinary request teaches people that your needs are an imposition.

2. “I know you’re busy, but…”

Instead of: “I know you’re busy, but if you have a minute at some point…”

Say: “Do you have ten minutes today? I’d like to talk about the schedule.”

Why: Opening this way ranks you below everything else on their list before you’ve even asked.

3. “Can I ask you something?”

Instead of: “Can I ask you something?”

Say: Just ask the question.

Why: Asking permission to ask makes the other person brace, and it makes you sound unsure you’re allowed to speak.

4. “This is probably a dumb question, but…”

Instead of: “This is probably a dumb question, but when is the deadline?”

Say: “Quick question: when is the deadline?”

Why: If you call your own question dumb, you’ve invited the other person to agree.

5. The long thank-you

Instead of: “Thank you so much for making time, I really appreciate it, I know how much you have going on…”

Say: “Thanks for meeting with me. I want to talk about my workload.”

Why: One thank-you is courteous, but a long one treats their time as a favor you have to keep repaying.

6. “I was hoping we could…”

Instead of: “I was hoping we could maybe talk about the holidays this year.”

Say: “I’d like to talk about the holidays this year.”

Why: “Hoping” sounds like you already expect a no.

Softeners that shrink your point

These are the small words and phrases that quietly take weight off whatever you say next.

7. “I just wanted to…”

Instead of: “I just wanted to follow up on the invoice.”

Say: “I’m following up on the invoice.”

Why: “Just” tells people that what follows isn’t important, so they treat it that way.

8. “This might be wrong, but…”

Instead of: “This might be wrong, but I kind of think we should go with option two?”

Say: “I think we should go with option two.”

Why: If you doubt your own idea out loud, nobody else will take it more seriously than you do.

9. “I don’t mean to be pushy, but…”

Instead of: “I don’t mean to be pushy, but did you get a chance to look at my proposal?”

Say: “Did you get a chance to look at my proposal?”

Why: Asking a fair question isn’t pushy, and calling it that makes it sound like it is.

10. “I hope this isn’t too much to ask…”

Instead of: “I hope this isn’t too much to ask, but could you pick up the kids on Wednesday?”

Say: “Could you pick up the kids on Wednesday?”

Why: Worrying aloud about the size of your request makes the other person notice its size.

11. “I guess I could…”

Instead of: “I guess I could probably do Saturday…”

Say: “I can do Tuesday evening. Saturday doesn’t work for me.”

Why: “I guess” announces that you’re about to give in, which invites a little more pressure to finish the job.

12. “I’m so sorry, but I can’t”

Instead of: “I’m so sorry, I feel terrible, but I can’t make it.”

Say: “I can’t make it, but I hope it goes well.”

Why: Apologizing for a fair no makes it sound like you’ve done something wrong.

Questions that hand your decision away

These turn a clear statement into a request for permission, or ask the other person to approve of you.

13. “Would you mind if I…?”

Instead of: “Would you mind if I left at five today?”

Say: “I’m leaving at five today. Just letting you know.”

Why: When something is yours to decide, asking permission turns it into something they can refuse.

14. “Is that okay?” after a boundary

Instead of: “I can’t lend you money this time. Is that okay?”

Say: “I can’t lend you money this time.” Then stop.

Why: Asking for approval at the end of a boundary tells the other person it’s still open for discussion.

15. “Does that make sense?”

Instead of: “So that’s my plan. Does that make sense?”

Say: “That’s my plan. What questions do you have?”

Why: “Does that make sense?” asks them to grade you, while “What questions do you have?” assumes you were clear.

16. “Would you be open to…?”

Instead of: “Would you be open to maybe splitting the chores differently?”

Say: “I’d like us to split the chores differently. Here’s what I have in mind.”

Why: A wish wrapped in a tentative question is easy to wave away, while a clear request has to be answered.

17. “Don’t you think we should…?”

Instead of: “Don’t you think we should get a second quote?”

Say: “I think we should get a second quote.”

Why: Hiding your opinion inside a question means you never actually say what you think.

18. “Whatever you think is best”

Instead of: “Oh, I don’t mind. Whatever you think is best.”

Say: “I’d prefer the Italian place.”

Why: When you never state a preference, people stop asking for one.

Phrases that blur what you mean

These cover the hard moments: disagreeing, raising a problem, or saying something that matters.

19. “To be honest with you…”

Instead of: “To be honest with you, I’m not happy with how that went.”

Say: “I’m not happy with how that went.”

Why: Announcing honesty adds a runway before the hard part, and it can make the rest of what you say sound less honest.

20. “Can I be real with you for a second?”

Instead of: “Can I be real with you for a second? I feel like I’m doing most of the work.”

Say: “I’m doing most of the work, and I’d like that to change.”

Why: Asking permission to be direct gives the other person a chance to steer you away before you’ve said it.

21. “Wouldn’t it be great if…?”

Instead of: “Wouldn’t it be great if someone else hosted Thanksgiving this year?”

Say: “I’m not hosting Thanksgiving this year. Who would like to take it?”

Why: Hinting leaves it to other people to act on your need, and they usually don’t.

22. “Isn’t it true that…?”

Instead of: “Isn’t it true that I covered the last three weekends?”

Say: “I’ve covered the last three weekends. I need this one off.”

Why: A plain statement of fact is stronger than a question that invites an argument about the facts.

23. “Can you see how…?”

Instead of: “Can you see how that might have upset me?”

Say: “That upset me.”

Why: You don’t need the other person to agree your feelings make sense before you’re allowed to have them.

24. “It’s fine, it’s no big deal”

Instead of: “Oh, it’s fine. It’s no big deal,” when it bothered you.

Say: “That bothered me. Next time, please check with me first.”

Why: Saying it’s fine when it isn’t teaches people it’s fine to do again.

Part 2: 10 signs you’re being worked on

Your body often notices pressure before your mind does. These ten signs don’t always mean someone is using a tactic on you. Sometimes a bad feeling is just a bad feeling. But when one shows up, it’s worth slowing down and looking at what’s happening.

1. You suddenly feel like agreeing with everything

What it feels like: You’ve said yes to a few small things in a row, and now saying no to the next one feels oddly hard, as if it would break the flow.

What to do: Pause. Look at where the requests are heading and ask yourself whether you’d have agreed to the last one if it had come first. See Chapter 15.

2. You feel guilty, but you can’t say why

What it feels like: You haven’t broken a promise or hurt anyone, yet you feel like you owe something. Often it follows a favor you didn’t ask for, or a sigh after you said no.

What to do: Ask yourself one question: did I actually do something wrong? If not, the guilt is pressure, not a signal. You can feel it and still keep your answer. See Chapter 11.

3. They’re unusually warm right before an ask

What it feels like: Compliments, attention, or a sudden sense that you’re special, followed closely by a request.

What to do: Accept the kind words, then treat the request on its own. “Thank you. Let me check and get back to you.” Notice what happens when you say no to something small. See Chapter 14.

4. You feel like you have to decide right now

What it feels like: A rush. Your chest tightens, and waiting feels impossible or rude.

What to do: Ask whether there’s a real outside deadline or only their urgency. If it’s their urgency, give yourself time: “I’ll let you know by Thursday.” See Chapter 14, and Chapter 9 for practice with the pause.

5. You start doubting your own memory

What it feels like: You were sure about what was said or agreed. After talking to them, you’re not sure anymore, and this keeps happening with the same person.

What to do: Write things down after important conversations: the date, what was said, and what was agreed. If the doubt keeps growing, describe what happened to someone you trust. See Chapter 12.

6. They ask lots of questions, but none are about what you want

What it feels like: You’re being questioned closely, and each answer seems to lead you one step closer to a yes. You don’t feel heard.

What to do: Stop answering for a moment and ask one of your own: “What are you hoping I’ll do?” Getting the real request out in the open makes it much easier to answer. See Chapter 3.

7. You’re explaining things nobody asked about

What it feels like: They went quiet, and you rushed to fill the silence with reasons, details, and maybe a small concession.

What to do: Stop, even mid-sentence if you can. You don’t owe a long explanation. Let the pause sit, or say “Let me think about it.” See Chapter 3.

8. You’re told what “everyone” thinks

What it feels like: Suddenly you’re outnumbered. Everyone in the family, the team, or the friend group apparently agrees with them.

What to do: Bring it back to the two of you. “Other people can have their views. I’m talking with you.” See Chapter 12.

9. You’re planning around their mood

What it feels like: You rehearse conversations in advance, watch their face for signs of trouble, and avoid topics that might lead to silence or anger. It’s like walking on eggshells.

What to do: Notice it, and name it to yourself. Then raise one small thing at a calm moment and see what happens. If you feel afraid of someone you live with, or they control your money, phone, or movements, this is more than a communication problem. Please read “When intimidation becomes danger” in Chapter 16 and reach out for support. See also Chapter 13.

10. You feel good, but you’re not sure what you agreed to

What it feels like: The conversation was warm and pleasant. Afterward you realize you’ve taken something on, and you can’t quite say what or how much.

What to do: Go back over it. Write down what you think you agreed to and send a short message to confirm: “Just to check, I said I’d help with the first draft, not the full report.” Then ask yourself whether you’d have agreed if you’d felt less charmed. See Chapter 14 and Chapter 25.

Where to go from here

Neither list is complete. Over the next few weeks, listen to yourself in conversations and emails. You’ll catch other phrases that shrink you, and other moments when something feels off. Add them to your own list.

The goal isn’t to become cold or blunt. You can stay warm, kind, and quietly spoken. You’re just removing the habits that hide what you mean, so people hear it.
