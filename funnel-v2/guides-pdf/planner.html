How to use this planner

You know the feeling. A conversation is coming that you’ve been putting off. Asking for help. Saying you’ve had enough. Turning down something everyone expects you to accept. You know roughly what you want to say. Then the moment arrives, the other person sighs or pushes, and you hear yourself say “it’s fine, don’t worry about it.”

That isn’t a lack of courage. It’s a lack of preparation. When you’re nervous, you fall back on old habits, and for most accommodating people the old habit is giving in.

This planner gives you something to fall back on instead. It has three parts.

  • Before. Twelve short questions that take about ten minutes, plus a short routine to settle yourself just before you start.
  • During. A quick card with four moves to keep in mind while you talk.
  • After. A short debrief, so the next hard conversation is easier than this one.

There’s also a filled-in example near the end, so you can see what good answers look like.

A few tips before you start.

  1. Write your answers down. Thinking “I could answer that” isn’t the same as answering it. Paper works well. Print a fresh copy for each conversation.
  2. Short answers are fine. One honest line beats a paragraph you’ll never read again.
  3. Fill it out a few hours ahead, if you can. The night before or the morning of works well. Read it once more just before the conversation, then put it away. You want the plan in your head, not in your hand.
  4. Use it for small things too. A conversation with a coworker about a shared task is good practice for the big one with your family.

If you’re in a situation where you feel unsafe, this planner isn’t the right tool. Leave, and get support from people you trust or a local helpline.

Before the conversation

Date:

Who I’m talking to:

1. What is this conversation about?

Tip: Name it in one plain sentence, like “Asking my sister to help with Dad’s appointments” or “Telling my manager I can’t take on another project.”

2. What do I want to come out of it?

Tip: Be specific. Not “I want it to go well,” but “I want my brother to take two of Mom’s appointments each month, starting in March.”

3. Why does this matter to me?

Tip: Name the need underneath your request (fairness, respect, being seen, rest). Chapter 19 lists six core needs that come up again and again.

4. What does the other person probably want?

Tip: Try to name at least two things, including the need under their words. If you can’t name anything, you’re not ready yet. Ask yourself what they complain about, and what calms them down.

5. Is there a way to meet their need without giving up mine?

Tip: Often you can’t give someone exactly what they asked for, but you can give them what they actually need. Look for that option before you go in.

6. What’s my bottom line?

Tip: This is the least you’ll accept, or the line you won’t move past. You don’t have to say it out loud. Just know it, so you can tell when you’re being pushed below it.

7. My opening line, word for word.

Tip: Keep it short and calm. No pre-apology, no “this is probably silly, but.” Say what you want to talk about, then stop.

8. What will they probably say back?

Tip: Write the two responses you most expect, then a one-sentence reply to each. Saying your reply out loud once, in an empty room, helps it come out more easily later.

9. What pressure tactic might they use, and what’s my calm reply?

Tip: Think about how this person usually gets their way: guilt, “that’s not what happened,” going quiet, charm, urgency, or anger. Write the line you expect and the calm reply you’ll use. Part Three of the book (Chapters 11 to 18) covers each tactic, and The Calm Reply Scripts has word-for-word answers you can borrow.

10. What’s the one thing I will NOT do?

Tip: Name the trap you know you fall into. “I won’t apologize for bringing it up.” “I won’t offer to do it all myself just to end the silence.” “I won’t say yes on the spot.”

11. My exit line.

Tip: Have two ready. One for a good ending (“Thanks for hearing me out. I’m glad we have a plan.”), and one for when it’s going badly and you need to stop (“I don’t think we’ll settle this today. Let’s pick it up on Sunday.”).

12. How will I lock it in afterward?

Tip: If you reach an agreement, plan a short written follow-up and one small first step, like putting a date in the calendar. Chapter 25 explains why agreements that aren’t written down tend to fade.

Right before you start: a short settling routine

This takes about two minutes. Do it somewhere quiet if you can: your car, a hallway, the bathroom. Nobody needs to know.

Step 1. Breathe out slowly. Breathe in normally. Then breathe out slowly and fully, making the out-breath noticeably longer than the in-breath. Do this three or four times. It’s the same calm-down breath from the 21-day reset. Many people find it helps them feel steadier and slows down their speaking pace.

Step 2. Settle your body. Drop your shoulders. Unclench your jaw. Loosen your hands. If you’re sitting, put both feet flat on the floor. If you’re standing, stand evenly on both feet.

Step 3. Say your opening line once. Quietly, or in your head. Use the exact words from question 7. You want them ready, so you don’t have to search for them when the moment comes.

Step 4. Remind yourself of your exit line. Look at question 11 one more time. Knowing you can end the conversation calmly, at any point, takes a lot of pressure off. You’re not trapped in it.

Step 5. Put the planner away. You’ve done the work. Now go and have the conversation.

If you still feel nervous, that’s normal. You don’t need to feel calm to say what you mean. You just need to be steady enough to say it once and not take it back.

During the conversation

Keep these four moves in mind. If it helps, copy them onto a small card or the notes app on your phone.

1. Pause. After you make your point, stop talking. Count slowly to four in your head. Don’t soften it, don’t add reasons, don’t ask “does that make sense?” When they ask you something, it’s fine to pause before you answer too.

2. Acknowledge, then hold. When they push back, show that you heard them, then say your position again. “I know this is a lot to take on. I still need you to take two appointments a month.” You don’t have to argue about who’s right. You just don’t let the pressure change your answer. If they keep pushing, repeat the same sentence in the same calm tone.

3. Use a buffer. If they ask for something you didn’t expect, you don’t have to answer on the spot. “Let me think about that and get back to you tomorrow.” Your answer can still be yes later. The buffer just makes sure the answer comes from you, not from the pressure of the moment.

4. Use your exit line. If it’s going well, end on a clear, warm note and confirm the next step. If it’s going badly, stop calmly and suggest a time to pick it up again. Leaving a conversation isn’t losing it.

After the conversation: the debrief

Fill this out the same day if you can, while it’s fresh. Even if it went well. Especially if it didn’t. This is the part that makes your next hard conversation easier.

1. What actually happened?

Tip: Compare it with your answer to question 2. Did you get what you wanted? All of it, some of it, or none of it?

2. Did I keep my one promise to myself?

Tip: Look at question 10. Be honest. If you slipped, write down the exact moment it happened and what they said just before.

3. What did they do that I expected, and what surprised me?

Tip: Did the pressure tactic from question 9 show up? Did something else show up instead? Write their exact words if you can.

4. What worked that I didn’t plan?

Tip: Sometimes the best moment was something you said on the fly. Name it, so you can use it again on purpose.

5. What do I need to follow up on?

Tip: Write down the short message you’ll send to confirm what was agreed, and when you’ll send it. If nothing was agreed, write when you’ll raise it again.

6. What would I change about my preparation next time?

Tip: Not “what’s wrong with me.” What would you plan differently? This is how the next planner gets better.

Example: asking a sibling to share care of an aging parent

Here’s what a completed planner might look like. The situation: you’ve been driving your mother to her medical appointments, handling her prescriptions, and checking in on her most evenings for over a year. Your brother lives twenty minutes further away and helps only when you ask, which is rarely. You want to ask him to take on a regular share.

1. What is this conversation about? Asking my brother to share Mom’s care on a regular schedule.

2. What do I want to come out of it? He takes two of Mom’s appointments each month and handles her prescription refills, starting next month.

3. Why does this matter to me? Fairness, and rest. I’m tired, and I’m starting to resent him and feel guilty about resenting him.

4. What does the other person probably want? To not feel blamed. To keep his weekends free, since his work week is long. He probably also wants to feel like a good son, and may feel guilty that he hasn’t done more.

5. Is there a way to meet their need without giving up mine? I can be clear that I’m not blaming him, just asking for a plan. I can let him choose which appointments he takes, so he has some say. Weekday mornings might work if he can shift his hours.

6. What’s my bottom line? At least one appointment a month, plus the prescriptions. Not “I’ll help when I can.”

7. My opening line, word for word. “I want to talk about Mom’s care. I’ve been doing most of it for a while, and I need us to share it on a regular schedule.”

8. What will they probably say back? (a) “I’m really busy at work right now.” My reply: “I know. I’m busy too. That’s why I’d like us to plan it, so it’s not a surprise for either of us.” (b) “You should have said something sooner.” My reply: “You’re right, I should have. I’m saying it now.”

9. What pressure tactic might they use, and what’s my calm reply? Guilt and rewriting the past. He might say “You always wanted to be the one in charge of Mom,” or “It’s not like I never help.” My calm reply: “Maybe it looked that way. Either way, I need us to share it from now on.” If he goes quiet or changes the subject: “I’ll give you some time to think. Can we settle the dates by Sunday?”

10. What’s the one thing I will NOT do? I won’t say “never mind, I’ll just keep doing it” if he sighs or goes quiet.

11. My exit lines. Good ending: “Thanks for hearing me out. This takes a weight off.” Bad ending: “I don’t think we’ll sort this out tonight. Let’s talk again on Sunday.”

12. How will I lock it in afterward? Send a short text the same evening: “Thanks for today. Just confirming: you’ll take Mom’s appointments on the first and third Tuesday, and handle her refills, starting next month.” Ask him to put the first date in his calendar while we’re still talking.

Where to go from here

The more you use this planner, the faster it gets. The first one might take you the full ten minutes. After a few, you’ll find yourself running through the questions in your head on the drive over.

When you want to go deeper, three chapters of the book pair closely with this planner.

  • Chapter 19, What People Really Want, helps you answer questions 3, 4, and 5. It covers the six core needs and how to meet someone’s need without giving up your own.
  • Chapter 21, Being Heard Without a Fight, gives you more ways to open the conversation and keep it calm: the Strategic Pause, the Named Feeling, open “how” and “what” questions, and admitting your own part first.
  • Chapter 25, Making It Stick, covers what happens after a yes: the One-Line Email, small first steps, and what to do when an agreement starts to slip.

And when you know what kind of pressure is coming, turn to Part Three and The Calm Reply Scripts to prepare your answer.

You don’t have to get every hard conversation right. You just have to walk in prepared, say what you mean once, and not take it back.
