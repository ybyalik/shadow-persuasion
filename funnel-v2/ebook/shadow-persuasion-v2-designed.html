How to Use This Book

This book is built in four parts, and the order matters.

Part One explains why you keep getting run over: the rules you were raised with, the signals that show people where to push, the voice in your head that argues for the other side, and the habit of caring so much about everyone else that you disappear.

Part Two is the reset. It starts with a three-day quick start and continues with a 21-day plan of short daily drills. This is where things actually change, so please don’t skip it. The 21-Day Reset Journal that comes with this book has a page for every day.

Part Three names the tactics people use on you, from guilt trips and the silent treatment to scams and group pressure. For each one you’ll find what it looks like, what it’s for, and the calm words that take its power away.

Part Four is quiet power: being heard without a fight, asking for what you’re owed, and making agreements stick. It ends with the line between fair influence and manipulation.

At the back you’ll find playbooks for the people who test you most, a situation finder, and a quick reference of every tactic in the book.

If you’re in the middle of something right now, start with the situation finder or the playbook for that person. Then come back and read from the beginning.

Keep a pen nearby. Every chapter ends with a short exercise. Writing is slower than thinking, and slower is the point.

Part One

Why You Keep Getting Run Over

Chapter 1

The 3 A.M. Replay

It usually starts a little after three.

You are not thinking about anything in particular. Then a sentence surfaces, something someone said to you yesterday, or last week, or eleven years ago at a wedding. And you are back in the room. Same words. Same tone. Same small pause before you answered, and the answer you gave, which was polite and reasonable and wrong.

Now the real work begins. You rewrite it. In this version you stay calm and say the precise thing. They go quiet. Someone else at the table nods. You run it again with a sharper line. Then again. By four o’clock you have a speech that would end the argument for good, and nobody to give it to.

If you have done this, you are the person this book was written for.

Not because you are weak. Most people who replay conversations at night are capable, thoughtful, and good at their jobs. They are the ones who remember birthdays, cover the shift, answer the late email, drive the relative to the airport. They are the ones other people describe as “so easy to work with.” That phrase is a compliment. It is also, very often, a description of how little you cost the people around you.

The yes you didn’t mean

Think back over the last month and count the times you agreed to something you did not want to do.

Not the big things. The small ones. The “sure, no problem” to the coworker who drops a task on your desk at 4:40 on a Friday. The weekend you gave up because your sister needed help moving again. The dinner you went to, the call you took, the favor you said yes to before the other person finished asking. The restaurant you didn’t pick. The opinion you swallowed because it wasn’t worth the friction.

Each one, on its own, was easy to justify. It’s only an hour. It’s family. It’s not a hill to die on. And each justification was true. That is what makes the pattern hard to see. Nobody takes your life from you in one piece. They take it in reasonable, defensible slices, and you hand over most of them yourself, with a smile, because saying no would have felt rude.

Now count something harder. Count the times you meant to say something and didn’t.

The raise you were going to ask for after the project wrapped, and then didn’t, because the timing felt off. The time your partner made a joke at your expense in front of friends and you laughed along. The meeting where your idea was repeated back ten minutes later by someone louder, and the room credited them, and you said nothing because correcting it would have looked petty. The phone call with your mother that ended, as it always does, with you apologizing for something she did.

These are the conversations that come back at three in the morning. Your mind does not replay the times you were treated fairly. It replays the times you left something on the table. It is keeping score, even when you’ve told yourself to let it go.

What the replay is actually doing

Most advice treats the 3 a.m. replay as a problem to quiet down. Breathe. Let it go. Don’t take things so personally.

I think that misreads what’s happening.

The replay is your mind doing an audit you refused to do while you were awake. Some part of you noticed that a line was crossed. It noticed the other person got what they wanted and you paid for it. It noticed that you did the accommodating thing, again, and it wants to know why. The rewriting, the better comeback, the imaginary speech, is that same part of you trying to practice a response it was never allowed to give.

So the replay is a signal, not a malfunction. It says: something here keeps happening, and you keep losing.

The problem is that it shows up at the wrong time and offers the wrong solution. It hands you a perfect line twelve hours after the moment closed. A perfect line is useless in the past. What you need is a reliable response in the present: a way to notice the move while it’s happening, and an answer you can actually say out loud, in your own voice, without your heart pounding.

That is most of what this book is about.

You are easier to read than you think

There is an uncomfortable idea underneath all of this, and I would rather say it now than let you discover it in chapter three.

The people who take advantage of you are not, for the most part, villains. Some are. Most are not. Most of them are ordinary people who have learned, through simple repetition, what works on you.

They learned that if they sound disappointed, you will offer to fix it. They learned that if they ask at the last minute, you will be too flustered to refuse. They learned that if they get loud, you get quiet. They learned that a compliment before a request makes the request land softer. They learned that you will not bring it up later, because you never do.

None of this requires a plan. A child learns which parent to ask. A manager learns which employee will stay late without being told. A friend learns who will always pick up the phone. People follow the path of least resistance, and over time you became that path.

This is not a judgment of you. It is a description of a pattern, and patterns can be read. Once you can read yours, you can see the exact moments where someone presses on it. Then you can stop responding the way they expect.

Some people do press on purpose. They know exactly what they are doing. The partner who goes silent for three days until you apologize. The boss who praises you in private and takes credit in public. The relative who rewrites what happened until you start to wonder whether your memory is the problem. Later chapters deal with these people directly, tactic by tactic, with the words to answer each one. For now it is enough to know that the deliberate ones use the same openings as the accidental ones. They just use them more often, and with more skill.

What it costs

It is easy to treat all of this as a mood problem. You feel a bit resentful, a bit tired, a bit unseen. Then the feeling passes.

It is worth adding up what it costs in plainer terms.

At work, it costs money. The raise you didn’t ask for compounds every year you stay. The credit you didn’t claim becomes someone else’s promotion. The extra work you absorbed without discussion becomes the new baseline, and nobody remembers it was ever extra. Being easy to work with is valuable to everyone except the person doing it.

At home, it costs honesty. Every swallowed complaint goes somewhere. Usually it turns into resentment that leaks out sideways, in a cold tone or a sharp comment about something unrelated. The other person is confused, because as far as they knew, everything was fine. You said it was fine.

In your family, it costs you your adult self. Many people who are confident at work become fifteen again within ten minutes of a holiday dinner. The old roles snap back into place. Someone is the responsible one, someone is the fragile one, someone is always right. If your role was “the one who keeps the peace,” you will find yourself keeping it at your own expense, decades after you should have retired from the job.

With friends, it costs balance. You become the one who listens, helps, and shows up, and the friendship slowly tilts until you are mostly a service. You notice it when you need something and the phone is quiet.

And underneath all four, it costs time and attention. Every hour spent replaying, rehearsing, and recovering from conversations is an hour you didn’t spend on anything you chose. People who are always accommodating others are often surprised, when they finally look, how little of their week belongs to them.

Why being nice was the right strategy, once

If this pattern is so expensive, why does almost everyone who has it keep it?

Because it worked. For a long time, it was the smartest strategy available.

As a child, keeping the adults around you happy was not optional. It was how you stayed safe, got approval, avoided punishment, and kept the household calm. If you grew up with a parent who was volatile, critical, or simply overwhelmed, you learned to read the room before you walked into it. You learned to anticipate what people needed and provide it before they had to ask. You learned that your needs were a risk and other people’s moods were the weather.

School rewarded the same habits. So did most first jobs. Be agreeable, be reliable, don’t make waves, and good things will come. Many of the people reading this were praised their whole lives for exactly the behavior that now drains them.

So the problem is not that you learned the wrong lesson. You learned the right lesson for a situation you are no longer in. You are an adult now. Nobody can send you to your room. You can survive someone being disappointed in you. You just haven’t practiced it, and your body still reacts as if every conflict were a threat.

That is why this book is built around practice, not just ideas. You can read a hundred times that you’re allowed to say no. It won’t change the tight chest and the racing thoughts that show up when you actually try. Practice changes that. Small, deliberate, slightly uncomfortable practice, repeated until the new response feels ordinary.

What this book is, and what it isn’t

You picked up a book called Shadow Persuasion, so it’s fair to wonder what kind of book this is.

It is not a book about becoming cold. You do not need to stop caring about people. Your warmth is not the problem. The problem is warmth with no boundary around it, offered to everyone at any price, including to people who treat it as a resource to be mined.

It is not a book about winning every argument, either. Most of the time the goal is not to win. It is to stop losing by default: to say what you mean, hold your position calmly, and let the other person deal with their own reaction for once.

And it is not a manual for turning into the people who have been using you. There is a whole section on influence, and some of it is powerful. It comes last, on purpose. Influence in the hands of someone who still can’t say no turns into people-pleasing with better technique. Influence in the hands of someone who has done the earlier work is something else. It’s the ability to be heard, to ask for what you’re owed, and to change the direction of a conversation without raising your voice.

The order matters, so here it is.

Part One explains why you keep getting run over: the contract you were raised with, how other people read you, the voice in your head that argues for the other side, and the habit of caring so much about everyone’s feelings that yours disappear.

Part Two is the reset. It starts with a three-day quick start for an early win, then a 21-day plan of short daily exercises and journal prompts. This is where things actually change, so don’t skip it. You will practice noticing, then pausing, then holding your ground, in that order, using small courage drills that build the one skill this whole book depends on: being able to tolerate someone else’s disappointment without rushing to fix it.

Part Three names the tactics people use on you. Guilt, rewriting the past, the silent treatment, charm and pressure, the slow squeeze, intimidation. Then the bigger ones: high-pressure sales, scams, and the way groups pull people in. For each one, you’ll learn what it looks like, what it’s for, and the calm words that take its power away.

Part Four is quiet power. How to read what people really want, how you come across before you say a word, how to be heard without a fight, how to get what you’re owed, how office politics actually works, and how to make agreements stick. It closes with the line you don’t cross.

At the back are quick playbooks for specific people: the boss, the coworker, the partner, the ex, aging parents, adult siblings, in-laws, the friend who always needs something. If you are in the middle of something right now, start there. Then come back and read the book in order.

Start tonight

Before you go further, do one thing. It takes about ten minutes.

Get a notebook, or open the journal that comes with this book. Think of the most recent conversation you’ve replayed, the one that came back last night or this week. Then answer these questions in writing. Not in your head. Writing is slower, and slower is the point.

Who was it, and where were you? Keep it simple. “My manager, Tuesday’s team meeting.” “My mother, on the phone, Sunday.”

What did they say or do? Write their actual words if you remember them. Be specific. “She said, ‘I guess I’ll just do it myself, like always,’ and sighed.” Not “she guilt-tripped me.”

What did you say or do? Again, the actual words. Include what your body did, if you noticed. Did you smile? Go quiet? Apologize?

What do you wish you had said? Write the 3 a.m. version, as sharp as you like. Get it out of your head and onto the page.

What were you afraid would happen if you had said it? This is the most important question. Most people find the answer is some version of: they’ll be upset with me, they’ll think I’m difficult, it will turn into a fight, they’ll leave. Write down exactly what you feared.

What did they get? The last question. Look at the exchange from the outside, as if it happened to a friend. What did the other person walk away with? Your time, your agreement, your silence, your apology, the last word?

That’s all. You don’t have to do anything with it yet. You are simply moving the replay from three in the morning, when it controls you, to the daytime, where you can look at it. Most people find that the conversation looks different on paper. The other person’s move becomes visible. So does yours.

Keep the page. You’ll come back to it in Part Two, and by the end of the reset you’ll write a new answer to that same moment, one you could actually say.

Field note The sharpest line you invent at 3 a.m. is almost never the one that would have worked. What works is usually shorter, calmer, and said a second later than you’re used to. The replay is practice for the wrong moment. This book moves the practice to the right one.
Chapter 2

The Good Person Contract

Nobody hands you a contract when you’re four years old. But you sign one anyway.

It isn’t written down. It’s assembled from a thousand small moments: the look on a parent’s face when you made a scene in a store, the warmth you got when you shared without being asked, the silence at dinner after you said something true that nobody wanted said. You were a fast learner. Most children are. Within a few years you had worked out the terms.

The terms go something like this. Be agreeable and you’ll be safe. Be helpful and you’ll be loved. Don’t make trouble, don’t take up too much room, don’t need too much. If someone is upset, it’s your job to fix it. If there’s a conflict, it’s your job to smooth it over. If you do all of this well, good things will come to you.

That is the Good Person Contract. Most people who replay conversations at three in the morning are still living by it. They never chose it. They have just never read it as an adult, with an adult’s eyes, and asked whether the deal is still worth taking.

This chapter reads it with you. Clause by clause.

Who wrote it

The first thing to understand is that the contract was not written for your benefit.

That sounds harsh, and I don’t mean that the adults who raised you were plotting against you. Most were doing their best with what they had. But look at who gains when a child follows these rules. A compliant child is easier to manage. A child who soothes a parent’s moods makes the parent’s life lighter. A child who never asks for much costs less time, money, and attention. Schools run smoother when students sit still and do what they’re told. Workplaces run smoother when employees absorb extra work without complaint.

None of that is evil. It’s just convenient. And convenience shapes rules the way water shapes rock: slowly, invisibly, and always in the direction of least resistance.

So when you feel the old pull to be agreeable, it helps to ask a plain question. Agreeable for whom? The contract was drafted by people who benefited from your compliance, and it has been enforced ever since by people who still do. That includes some people who love you. Love and convenience are not opposites. They often live in the same house.

Clause one: other people’s feelings are your job

This is the foundation of the whole contract, so it gets the most attention.

Somewhere early, you learned that when someone near you is unhappy, you are responsible for making it stop. Maybe a parent drank, or raged, or went cold for days. Maybe a parent was just sad and you appointed yourself the one who cheered them up. Maybe nothing dramatic happened at all and you were simply a sensitive child in a busy house, who learned that the calmest way through the day was to keep everyone else calm.

Whatever the origin, the clause shows up the same way in adult life.

At work, it’s the knot in your stomach when your manager seems annoyed, and the instant scan of everything you might have done wrong. It’s the way you over-explain a simple request so nobody could possibly take offense. At home, it’s monitoring your partner’s tone from the next room. In your family, it’s the role you still play at gatherings: the one who changes the subject when it gets tense, who refills the drinks and laughs at the uncle’s joke so the silence doesn’t stretch. With friends, it’s the hours spent listening to a problem that is never solved and never reciprocated, because ending the call would feel unkind.

The cost of this clause is enormous, and it’s mostly hidden. If every unhappy person around you is your responsibility, then anyone who wants something from you only has to be unhappy in your direction. Their disappointment becomes your emergency. You’ll do almost anything to make it stop, including give up the very thing you were trying to protect.

Here is the adult revision. Other people’s feelings are real and they matter. They are also theirs. You can care about someone’s disappointment without being in charge of ending it. You can let a person be upset in the next room and keep doing what you were doing. That sentence will feel wrong for a while. That feeling is the old contract objecting. It doesn’t mean the revision is mistaken.

Clause two: your needs are a burden

The second clause follows from the first. If keeping everyone else comfortable is the job, then your own needs get in the way. They’re friction. They create work for other people. The less you need, the better you are at the job.

So you learned to need very little. Or at least to look as if you did.

You see this clause in how hard it is to ask for help, even with something small. You see it in the long, apologetic preamble before a simple request: “I’m so sorry to bother you, I know you’re busy, it’s totally fine if not, but I was wondering…” You see it in the gift you can’t accept without immediately planning how to repay it, and in the way you answer “what do you want for dinner?” with “I don’t mind, whatever you want,” when you do mind and you know exactly what you want.

At work, it looks like eating lunch at your desk because taking a break feels indulgent, or never mentioning that you’re overloaded until you’re about to break. In a relationship, it looks like a partner who genuinely doesn’t know what you like, because you’ve never told them. They aren’t careless. You’ve been hiding it.

The clause has a cruel twist built in. People who never express needs often end up resenting the people around them for not meeting needs they never stated. Then they feel guilty about the resentment, which confirms the original belief that their needs cause trouble. The loop closes and tightens.

The adult revision: Your needs are information. Stating them isn’t selfish. It’s how the people around you learn how to treat you well. A friend who knows you hate surprise visits can stop making them. A boss who knows you’re at capacity can make a different decision. You aren’t burdening anyone by being accurate.

Clause three: conflict means something has gone wrong

The third clause treats every disagreement as a small disaster.

If you grew up in a house where conflict meant shouting, slammed doors, or days of icy silence, this makes complete sense. Conflict was dangerous. Avoiding it was smart. If you grew up in a house where conflict simply never happened in the open, where everyone was always “fine,” you may have absorbed the same lesson by a different route: disagreement is a breakdown, and good relationships don’t have it.

Neither is true for adults.

Healthy relationships have disagreements all the time. Good teams argue about ideas. Good marriages include plenty of “no, I don’t want that.” Good friendships survive a friend saying “that hurt my feelings.” What makes these relationships healthy isn’t the absence of conflict. It’s that conflict is survivable. Both people can say what they think, the other person can push back, and everyone is still there the next day.

When you treat every disagreement as a crisis, three things happen. You give in early to make it stop. You lose the information the disagreement would have given you, because you never find out what the other person actually wanted or why. And you teach the people around you that escalation works, because the moment things get tense, you fold.

That last point matters more than it seems. If raising their voice gets results with you, some people will raise their voice. Not because they’re monsters. Because it works.

The adult revision: Disagreement is normal, and you can survive it. You don’t need to win every one. You need to stop treating them as emergencies. Most of the work in Part Two is practice at exactly this: staying in a mild disagreement for a few seconds longer than feels comfortable, and noticing that nothing terrible happens.

Clause four: if you’re good enough, you’ll be treated fairly

This is the clause that keeps the whole contract going, because it promises a reward.

Be patient, be helpful, be loyal, don’t complain, and eventually people will notice. The raise will come. The partner will appreciate everything you do. The family will finally see you clearly. The friend will show up for you the way you show up for them.

Sometimes this happens. Often it doesn’t, and the reason has nothing to do with whether you deserve it. People notice what’s in front of them, and someone who never asks is easy to overlook. A manager with ten employees and a limited budget gives raises to the people who ask, the people who might leave, and the people whose value is visible. Quietly excellent work, done without complaint, is very easy to take for granted. The same is true at home. If you always handle the bills, the birthdays, and the dentist appointments without comment, the people you live with may genuinely not know it’s happening.

So people living by this clause wait. They do more, and more, assuming the reward is just around the corner. When it doesn’t come, they don’t conclude that the clause is false. They conclude they haven’t been good enough yet. So they do more.

The adult revision: Fair treatment is mostly negotiated, not earned in silence. That doesn’t mean being pushy or demanding. It means making your contribution visible, naming what you want, and asking for it directly. Chapter 22 shows you how to do that at work and at home. For now, just notice how long you’ve been waiting for someone to notice.

Clause five: if you stop being nice, you’ll become one of them

The last clause is the one that keeps people from ever renegotiating the others.

Many accommodating people have been on the receiving end of someone selfish, controlling, or cruel. A critical parent. A bullying boss. A partner who always got their way. And they decided, somewhere deep down, I will never be like that. Being agreeable became part of their identity. It’s how they prove they’re one of the good ones.

So when they imagine saying no more often, or pushing back, or asking for more, they don’t picture a calm adult setting a reasonable limit. They picture the person who hurt them. Saying no feels like the first step toward becoming that person.

This fear deserves respect, because it comes from a real place. But it’s built on a false choice. The options are not “doormat” or “bully.” Between them is an enormous middle ground: people who are kind and also clear, generous and also limited, warm and also willing to disappoint you. You probably know a few. They’re often the people others trust most, because their yes means something. When they agree to help, you know they want to. When they say something is fine, it is.

The adult revision: You can protect yourself without hurting anyone. Saying no to an unfair request doesn’t make you selfish. Asking to be paid what you’re worth doesn’t make you greedy. Telling someone their joke hurt doesn’t make you cruel. These are ordinary acts of self-respect, and the people who react to them as if they were attacks are usually the ones who benefited most from your silence.

Reading the contract out loud

When you put the five clauses side by side, a pattern appears.

Other people’s feelings are your job. Your needs are a burden. Conflict means something went wrong. If you’re good enough, you’ll be treated fairly. If you stop being nice, you’ll become one of them.

Read that list as if a stranger handed it to you as a job offer. No salary, no end date, no right to renegotiate. You’d turn it down in a second. Yet most people reading this have been working under those exact terms for decades, and wondering why they feel tired.

You don’t have to tear the whole thing up at once. That isn’t how change usually works, and trying to do it all in a weekend tends to end with a dramatic confrontation, a wave of guilt, and a quick return to the old terms. What works better is renegotiating one clause at a time, in small, low-stakes situations, until the new terms start to feel normal.

This week’s exercise: find your clauses

Take out your journal. You’ll need about fifteen minutes.

Step one. Write the five clauses at the top of a page, in your own words if you like.

Step two. For each clause, write one recent example of it running your behavior. Be specific. “Clause one: Thursday, my sister texted that she was ‘having a rough week,’ and I offered to watch her kids Saturday even though I had plans.” “Clause four: I’ve done the quarterly report alone for two years and never mentioned it in my review.”

Step three. Circle the clause that shows up most often. For most people one is clearly the strongest. That’s your main clause, and it’s where the reset in Part Two will start.

Step four. Under your main clause, write the adult revision from this chapter. Then write it again in your own words, the way you’d say it to a friend who was stuck in the same pattern. That second version is the one to keep. You’ll come back to it often.

You don’t need to change anything yet. Seeing the contract clearly is the first step. Most people find that once they can name the clause, they start catching it in real time, a split second before they say yes. That split second is where everything else in this book happens.

Field note People who finally read their contract usually expect to feel angry. More often they feel tired, then oddly relieved. The rules were never a description of who they are. They were a strategy, and strategies can be retired.
Chapter 3

How They Read You

Poker players have a word for the small, involuntary signals that give away what someone is holding. They call them tells. A player touches their chip stack when they’re nervous. Another goes very still when they have a strong hand. A good player doesn’t need to see your cards. They just need to watch you long enough.

Accommodating people have tells too. Lots of them. They show up in how you answer a request, how you handle a pause, how fast you apologize, and what happens to your face when someone is disappointed. Most people around you will never consciously notice these signals. But some will notice them perfectly well, and almost everyone responds to them, whether they realize it or not.

This chapter is about learning to see yourself the way others see you. That isn’t about becoming self-conscious. It’s about understanding why the same people keep pressing on the same spots, so you can close the openings you didn’t know you were leaving.

Three kinds of people who take advantage

Before we look at the tells, it helps to sort the people who exploit them. There are roughly three kinds, and they need different responses.

The habit-formers. These are most of them. Ordinary people who learned through repetition that you’re the easy path. Your coworker doesn’t wake up planning to dump work on you. She has a deadline, she has a list of people she could ask, and you’re the one who has never said no. Your brother doesn’t calculate that a sigh will get you to cover the family dinner. He’s just learned, over thirty years, that it does. These people usually change quickly once the pattern stops working. Many barely notice the change. They simply start asking someone else.

The opportunists. These people notice your tells more clearly and use them when it suits them, without much thought for the cost to you. The friend who only calls when she needs something. The manager who knows you won’t push back on a weekend request. The relative who borrows money and is vague about paying it back. They aren’t running a strategy, exactly. They’ve just noticed where the soft ground is and they step on it when it’s convenient. They’ll test a new boundary a few times before accepting it.

The deliberate ones. A smaller group, but the most damaging. These people read others very well and use what they read to control them. They know which compliment will soften you, which tone will make you anxious, and how long a silence has to last before you apologize for something you didn’t do. A controlling partner. A charming boss who takes credit and spreads blame. A parent who has always known exactly how to make you feel twelve years old. They don’t just respond to your tells. They create situations designed to trigger them.

You’ll deal with all three in this book. For the habit-formers, a calm change in your behavior is usually enough. For the opportunists, you’ll need consistency, because they’ll test you. For the deliberate ones, you’ll need the specific responses in Part Three, and sometimes you’ll need distance.

The tells, though, are the same for all three. Close them, and you become much harder to work on for everyone.

The tells

Here are the most common signals accommodating people give off. You won’t have all of them. Most people have four or five that are especially strong.

The instant yes

The clearest tell of all. Someone asks for something and you agree before they finish the sentence, sometimes before you know what they’re asking. “Hey, could you…” “Sure.” The speed is the message. It tells the other person that your agreement doesn’t require thought, which means your time doesn’t require consideration. People learn to bring their requests to whoever answers fastest.

The long explanation

When you do say no, or even when you say yes with a condition, you surround it with reasons. “I’d love to, but I can’t Saturday because my car is in the shop and I promised my neighbor I’d help with something and honestly I’ve been so tired lately and…” Every extra reason is an opening. If you can’t come because your car is in the shop, they can offer you a ride. Over-explaining also signals that you believe you need permission to decline. People who believe that can usually be talked out of it.

The reflex apology

“Sorry, quick question.” “Sorry, I just wanted to check.” “Sorry, that’s my fault,” when it wasn’t. Apologizing for ordinary things, for asking, for taking up space, for someone else bumping into you, tells people you consider yourself the one who owes. Over time, people start treating you as though you do.

Filling the silence

Someone makes a request, or a criticism, or a demand, and then they stop talking. You can’t stand the pause, so you fill it. Often you fill it with a concession. “Well, I guess I could move some things around.” Skilled negotiators and skilled manipulators both use silence on purpose, because they know most people will rush to end it. If you’re one of those people, silence is a lever anyone can pull.

The disappointment flinch

When someone looks disappointed, something visible happens to you. Your face changes. Your voice softens. You start backtracking. “I mean, I could probably…” Disappointment is the most effective pressure there is for accommodating people, because it presses directly on clause one of the contract: other people’s feelings are your job. Anyone who notices that your resistance collapses the moment they look let down has found the most reliable button you have.

The approval hunt

You check, often without words, whether people are pleased with you. You reread emails for tone. You need the meeting to end on a warm note. After a disagreement you send a follow-up message to make sure everything is okay. People who see this learn that your approval-seeking can be traded on. Give you a little warmth, and you’ll give a lot back. Withhold it, and you’ll work hard to win it back.

The no follow-up

You agree to something reasonable, the other person doesn’t do their part, and you let it go. They were supposed to pay you back. They were supposed to take the next shift. They were supposed to apologize. You don’t raise it again, because raising it would be awkward. People learn very quickly whether commitments made to you are enforced. If they aren’t, the commitments stop being made in good faith.

The visible strain

Even when you hold a boundary, everything about you announces how hard it is. Your voice shakes, you won’t make eye contact, you add a nervous laugh, you say “is that okay?” at the end. This signals that the boundary is fragile and that a little more pressure will break it. Often it does.

How people test you

Once you know your tells, you’ll start to notice something else. People test them.

Testing is usually not deliberate. It’s just how people learn what’s possible. A new manager asks you to stay late once and watches your reaction. A new partner cancels plans at the last minute and sees whether you say anything. A relative makes a cutting comment at dinner and checks whether you laugh along. A friend asks to borrow a small amount of money, and if that goes smoothly, a larger amount comes later.

Each test asks the same question: what happens if I push here? Your response is the answer. If the push costs them nothing, the push continues, and usually grows. If the push meets a calm, clear limit, most people adjust and move on. The deliberate ones push again, harder, to see if the limit was real. That second push is often where people give up and fold. Knowing it’s coming makes it much easier to hold.

You can start practicing now, just by noticing. When someone asks something of you this week, ask yourself silently: is this a test? You don’t need to respond any differently yet. Just labeling it moves you from inside the moment to slightly outside it. From there you have more choices.

The openings

Tells are the signals. Openings are the needs underneath them, the places where pressure actually lands. Each of the tells above sits on top of one or more of these.

Guilt. The feeling that you owe something, that you’ve done something wrong, or that you’re being selfish. It’s triggered by sighs, martyrdom, reminders of past favors, and lines like “after everything I’ve done for you.”

The need for approval. The pull to be liked, thanked, and thought well of. It’s triggered by compliments before requests, warmth that comes and goes, and praise that seems to depend on compliance.

Fear of conflict. The physical alarm that goes off when things get tense. It’s triggered by raised voices, cold silences, and any hint that someone might be angry with you.

The need to be seen as good. The part of you that has made being kind and helpful your identity. It’s triggered by framing a request as a test of character: “I thought you were someone I could count on.”

Loneliness or fear of loss. The worry that if you disappoint someone, they’ll leave. It’s triggered by threats to withdraw, real or implied, and by people who make their affection feel conditional.

Urgency. The sense that a decision has to be made right now. It’s triggered by deadlines that appear from nowhere, emotional emergencies, and “I need an answer today.”

Most people who take advantage of you use one or two of these consistently. When you look back at your replay log from Chapter 1, you’ll probably see the same opening again and again. That’s useful. It tells you exactly where to reinforce.

Your tells are not your personality

There’s a risk in a chapter like this, and it’s worth naming.

When people first see their tells laid out, they sometimes conclude that something is fundamentally wrong with them. That they’re weak, or broken, or “too nice.” That isn’t the right conclusion.

Your tells are habits. They were sensible habits once, built to serve the Good Person Contract from Chapter 2. They’re also very common. Almost everyone apologizes too much sometimes, over-explains sometimes, and flinches when someone is disappointed. The difference between you and someone who rarely gets taken advantage of is mostly a difference in how often these habits fire, and how quickly the person recovers when they do.

Habits change with practice. You don’t have to become a different person. You only have to make the tells a little quieter: a two-second pause before saying yes, one fewer reason when you decline, one apology swapped for “thanks for waiting.” Small changes like these alter the signal you send. People respond to the new signal surprisingly fast.

This week’s exercise: the tell audit

Open your journal. This one takes ten minutes now, and a few minutes a day for the rest of the week.

Step one. Go through the eight tells in this chapter and give each one a score from 0 to 3. Zero means you rarely do it. Three means it’s constant. Be honest. Nobody else will see this page.

Step two. Circle your top two. These are the signals people read most clearly in you.

Step three. Look back at your replay log from Chapter 1. Which tell showed up in that conversation? Which opening did the other person press on?

Step four. For the rest of this week, keep a running tally of your top two tells. Just a mark on the page or a note in your phone each time you catch one. Don’t try to stop them yet. Counting is enough. Most people are surprised by the number. Many find the count starts dropping on its own by day four or five, simply because they’re watching.

If you want an outside view, ask someone you trust, someone who is on your side, a simple question: “When do you see me agree to things I don’t want to do?” Their answer is often more accurate than yours. We’re usually the last to see our own tells.

Field note The people who push hardest on your tells are rarely the ones who know you best. They’re the ones who have watched you most carefully. Those aren’t the same thing, and learning the difference saves a great deal of heartache.
Chapter 4

The Voice in Your Head

There’s a conversation that happens before every conversation that matters. You’re the only one in it.

You’re about to ask for the raise, and a voice says, Maybe wait until after the reorg. They’re stressed right now. You don’t want to seem tone-deaf. You’re about to tell a friend that she’s been canceling on you for months, and the voice says, She’s got a lot going on. You’re being petty. Is this really worth it? Your partner says something cutting at dinner and the voice is already there: He didn’t mean it like that. You’re too sensitive. Let it go.

The voice sounds reasonable. It sounds mature, even generous. That’s what makes it so effective. Because if you listen closely to what it’s actually arguing, you’ll notice that it’s almost always arguing for the other side.

This chapter is about that voice. Where it came from, what it says, how it sabotages you before anyone else has to, and how to give it a different job.

The defense attorney you didn’t hire

Picture a courtroom. On one side is you, with a fair complaint: you were overlooked, interrupted, taken for granted, or treated unkindly. On the other side is the person who did it. Now picture who stands up to argue the case.

For most accommodating people, the most persuasive lawyer in the room is working for the other side. And that lawyer is you.

They’re under a lot of pressure. It was probably a misunderstanding. You’ve done the same thing yourself. If you bring it up, it’ll make things worse. Who are you to ask for more, when other people have it so much harder?

Every one of those arguments might be partly true. That’s not the issue. The issue is that the voice only ever makes the case in one direction. It defends the person who hurt you, and it prosecutes you for wanting to say so. It’s not a fair judge. It’s a defense attorney the other side got for free.

This is one of the most important things to understand about being taken advantage of. Much of the work is done before anyone else has to lift a finger. A manipulative person doesn’t need to talk you out of your complaint if your own mind talks you out of it first. A thoughtless boss doesn’t need to refuse your raise if you never ask. The voice does the job for them.

Where the voice came from

Nobody is born with this voice. It’s assembled, like the contract in Chapter 2, from the people around you.

Often it began as a real person’s words. A parent who said “don’t be so dramatic” every time you were upset. A teacher who told you to stop showing off. An older sibling who mocked you for wanting things. A culture, a religion, or a family story that treated wanting as selfish and putting yourself forward as rude. You heard those messages so many times that you eventually didn’t need anyone else to say them. You started saying them to yourself.

There’s a logic to this. When you were young, predicting criticism helped you avoid it. If you could stop yourself before you did the thing that would get you scolded, you’d never be scolded. The voice was an early-warning system. It kept you out of trouble.

The trouble is that the system never updated. It’s still warning you about dangers that ended decades ago, using the voice of people who may no longer be in your life, or alive. And it’s still protecting you the only way it knows how: by keeping you small, quiet, and agreeable.

A useful question when the voice speaks up is simply: Whose voice is that? Not to blame anyone. Just to notice. Many people, when they ask, can name the exact person. Hearing it as their voice, rather than your own clear judgment, takes away a surprising amount of its authority.

What the voice says

The voice has a fairly small vocabulary. It uses the same lines over and over. Here are the most common, sorted by the job each one does.

It shrinks the problem. “It’s not a big deal.” “You’re overreacting.” “Other people have real problems.” “It was just a joke.” The aim is to make your complaint seem too small to be worth raising.

It explains away their behavior. “They’re stressed.” “They didn’t mean it.” “That’s just how they are.” “They had a hard childhood.” The aim is to make the other person’s actions understandable, which the voice then treats as acceptable.

It turns the problem on you. “You’re too sensitive.” “Maybe you misremembered.” “You probably did something to cause it.” “You always make things about you.” The aim is to make you the one at fault.

It predicts disaster. “If you say something, they’ll be furious.” “You’ll lose the job.” “They’ll leave.” “It’ll ruin the holiday.” The aim is to make speaking up seem far more dangerous than staying quiet.

It questions your right to ask. “Who are you to ask for that?” “You should be grateful for what you have.” “Don’t be greedy.” “Good people don’t make a fuss.” The aim is to make wanting anything feel morally suspect.

Read that list again and notice something. Many of those lines are exactly what a manipulative person would say to you, out loud, if you did raise the issue. “You’re overreacting.” “It was a joke.” “You’re too sensitive.” “You should be grateful.” The voice in your head and the person in front of you are often reading from the same script. That’s no accident. Manipulators use these lines because they work. They work because many people already believe them.

How the voice sabotages you

The voice doesn’t just argue. It changes what you do, often in ways you don’t notice until afterward. Watch for these.

Waiting for the perfect moment. The raise conversation waits until after the busy season. Then after the reorg. Then after your review. Then after the holidays. There’s always a reason why now is the wrong time. A year passes. The voice calls this patience. Usually it’s avoidance.

Negotiating against yourself. You decide to ask for a ten percent raise, and by the time you’re in the room you’ve talked yourself down to five, then three, then “whatever you think is fair.” Nobody pushed back. You did it all in your head, before the other person said a word. People do the same thing with boundaries. You meant to say “I can’t help this weekend” and it came out as “I could maybe do Sunday afternoon for a couple of hours.”

Pre-apologizing. “This is probably a stupid idea, but…” “I’m not sure this is right, but…” “You’ve probably already thought of this, but…” The voice gets you to lower the value of what you’re about to say before anyone hears it. Listeners take the hint.

Quitting before asking. You don’t apply for the role because you don’t meet every listed requirement. You don’t ask the question in the meeting because someone else probably knows more. You don’t invite the friend because she’s probably busy. The voice saves you from rejection by making sure you never ask.

Retreating after a win. You finally set a limit, and it goes fine. Then you spend the evening worrying whether you were too harsh. The next morning you send a message softening it. The voice can’t stop you from speaking up, so it gets you to take it back.

You don’t need to silence it

The usual advice about an inner critic is to fight it, ignore it, or drown it out with affirmations. In my experience that rarely works for long. The voice is persistent, and arguing with it in your head tends to turn into another 3 a.m. replay.

A more useful approach is to change the voice’s job. It’s trying to protect you. It’s just using an outdated method. You can keep the protective intention and give it better instructions.

Here are four practical ways to do that.

Name it. Give the voice a name, even a slightly ridiculous one. Some people use the name of the person it sounds like. Others pick something neutral, like “the Lawyer” or “the Manager.” When it starts up, say silently, “That’s the Lawyer again.” This creates a small gap between you and the voice. You’re hearing it instead of being it.

Ask for the other side. When the voice makes the case for the other person, finish the trial. Make the case for yourself with equal care. “They’re under a lot of pressure” can stay. Now add: “And I’ve been under pressure too, and I still met every deadline, and they took credit for my work in front of the director.” You don’t have to decide who wins. You just have to stop letting one side argue unopposed.

Use the friend test. Imagine a close friend described this exact situation to you. Would you tell them they were overreacting? Would you tell them to wait another year before asking for the raise? Most people are far fairer judges for their friends than for themselves. Borrow that judge.

Swap “but” for “and.” The voice loves “but.” “I’m upset, but they didn’t mean it.” “But” erases whatever came before it. Try “and” instead. “I’m upset, and they probably didn’t mean it.” Both things can be true. One doesn’t cancel the other. It’s a small change in wording that makes a real change in what you allow yourself to feel and do.

When the voice is right

To be fair to it, the voice is sometimes right. Sometimes you are overreacting, because you’re tired, or you misread a message, or the situation reminds you of something old and painful. Sometimes waiting really is the better strategy.

The goal isn’t to ignore the voice completely. It’s to stop treating it as the final authority. A good test is to check its predictions against reality. The voice said your boss would be furious if you asked for the raise. Was she? The voice said your friend would end the friendship if you told her she hurt you. Did she? Write down what the voice predicts, then write down what actually happened. Over a few weeks, you’ll see how often it’s wrong. Most people find it’s wrong far more often than it’s right, and almost always in the same direction: it overestimates the danger of speaking up and underestimates the cost of staying quiet.

This week’s exercise: the transcript

Open your journal. This runs all week, a few minutes at a time.

Step one. Give the voice a name and write it at the top of a page.

Step two. Each time you catch the voice talking you out of something this week, write down its exact words. Not a summary. The actual sentence. “Who are you to ask for that?” “She’s got a lot going on.” Aim for at least five by the end of the week.

Step three. Next to each line, note which job it was doing: shrinking the problem, explaining away their behavior, turning it on you, predicting disaster, or questioning your right to ask.

Step four. For each line, write the fair version. The one a friend on your side would say. Not a pep talk. Just an accurate, balanced sentence. “She’s got a lot going on, and she’s canceled four times in two months, and I’m allowed to tell her that bothers me.”

Step five. Whenever the voice made a prediction, add a column for what really happened. Fill it in as you find out.

At the end of the week, look at the page as a whole. You’ll probably see that the voice repeats itself a lot, and that it leans one way. That’s worth seeing on paper. Once you know its script, it gets much harder for the voice, or anyone using the same lines out loud, to talk you out of yourself.

Field note The voice rarely says anything new. It says the same six or seven sentences in a slightly different order. Learn them well enough and you’ll start to recognize them in other people’s mouths, too. That’s usually when things begin to change.
Chapter 5

Caring Too Much

You walk into a room and know within seconds who is upset. You can tell from a single text whether your partner is annoyed. When a coworker gets bad news, you feel it in your own stomach. When a friend is struggling, you lie awake thinking about how to help her.

People call this empathy, and it’s one of the best things about you. It makes you a good friend, a good colleague, a good parent, a good partner. People feel understood around you. They tell you things they don’t tell anyone else.

It’s also the single most exploited quality you have.

This chapter is not about becoming less caring. It’s about the difference between caring for people and carrying them, and how to keep the first without being crushed by the second.

Three ways of responding to someone’s pain

When someone near you is hurting, there are roughly three things you can do with that.

You can feel it with them. Their distress becomes your distress. Your heart rate rises with theirs. You feel their anxiety, their sadness, their anger, almost as if it were happening to you. This is fast, automatic, and powerful. It’s also exhausting, and if it happens all day it can wear you down.

You can care about it. You notice their pain, you feel warmth and concern for them, and you want things to go well for them, but you don’t absorb the feeling as your own. You stay steady. From that steady place, you can actually be useful: listen, ask what they need, offer help you can afford to give.

You can take it over. Their problem becomes your project. You start fixing, solving, rescuing, managing. You make the phone calls they should make. You lend the money. You rearrange your week. Often you care more about solving their problem than they do.

Accommodating people tend to live in the first and third modes. They feel everyone’s emotions at full volume, and then they try to make those emotions go away by taking over. The middle mode, steady care, is the one that actually helps people and the one that’s hardest to exploit. It’s also the one most of us were never taught.

Why this gets exploited

If feeling someone’s distress makes you act to end it, then anyone who wants you to act only has to show distress.

Most of the time that isn’t calculated. People who are upset show it, and you respond. But some people learn, consciously or not, that their visible unhappiness is a remote control for your behavior. A partner who sulks gets apologized to. A parent who sighs on the phone gets an extra visit. A coworker who looks overwhelmed gets her work taken over. A friend in permanent crisis gets your evenings.

The deliberate ones go further. They perform distress, or exaggerate it, because they know you’ll rush in. They tell you how hard their life is right before they ask you for something. They describe their pain in great detail and then let the silence sit, waiting for you to offer. They make it clear that if you don’t help, the consequences for them will be terrible, and it will be your fault.

Here is the uncomfortable part. Your empathy doesn’t tell you the difference between genuine distress and performed distress. It reacts the same way to both. That’s why you can’t rely on feeling alone to decide what to do. You need a second step, a moment of judgment between feeling someone’s pain and acting on it.

Where over-caring shows up

It looks a little different in each part of life.

At work, it’s being the one everyone brings their problems to. You become the unofficial counselor, the person who smooths over team conflicts, who notices when someone is struggling and quietly covers for them. It feels good to be needed. It also takes hours that never appear in your job description, and it’s rarely rewarded at review time. Worse, it can make you the person who absorbs everyone’s stress while the people actually causing it face no consequences.

With a partner, it’s taking responsibility for their moods. If they’re unhappy, you assume you need to fix it. You may find yourself managing their stress, their friendships, their relationship with their family, their career worries, while your own needs wait. Some people in this pattern end up with partners who never really grow up, because they’ve never had to.

In your family, it often goes back the furthest. Many over-carers were the child who looked after a parent emotionally: the one who comforted a depressed mother, mediated between fighting parents, or kept a volatile father calm. As adults they keep doing it, now for aging parents, adult siblings, nieces and nephews. They’re the first call in every crisis. Nobody asks whether they have anything left.

With friends, it’s the lopsided friendship. You listen to the same problems for years. You give the advice, the support, the rides to the airport. When you need something, the response is thinner. You tell yourself that’s fine, that friendship isn’t a transaction. That’s true. It’s also true that a friendship where only one person ever carries the weight is not much of a friendship.

When helping hurts

There’s a harder version of over-caring, and it deserves an honest word.

Sometimes the help you give keeps the other person stuck. You pay the adult child’s rent for the third year running, and they never learn to manage money. You cover for the partner who drinks, call in sick for them, explain away their absences to friends, and they never have to face what’s happening. You fix the coworker’s mistakes so quietly that the manager never sees them, and the coworker never improves.

In each case, your caring takes the consequences of someone else’s behavior and moves them onto you. That feels kind in the moment. Over time, it often makes things worse for everyone. The other person loses the feedback that might have pushed them to change. You lose time, money, and peace. And the relationship slowly becomes one where you’re the caretaker and they’re the patient.

Stopping this kind of help is one of the hardest things accommodating people ever do, because it feels like abandonment. It isn’t. Letting someone face the natural results of their choices, while still caring about them, is often the most respectful thing you can do. If the situation involves addiction, mental illness, or safety, it’s worth talking to a professional who knows the specifics. But in many everyday situations, stepping back is not cruelty. It’s trust.

Caring without carrying

So how do you keep your warmth without being drained by it? A few habits make a large difference.

Ask whose feeling it is. When you notice a strong emotion, pause and ask: is this mine, or did I just pick it up from someone else? You’ll often find that the anxiety you’re feeling started with your partner, or your boss, or the friend you just got off the phone with. Simply naming it, “this is hers,” lets some of it go. You can care about her anxiety without having to carry it around all evening.

Ask whose problem it is. Before you start fixing, ask: whose problem is this to solve? If your sister forgot to book a flight, that’s her problem. If your coworker missed a deadline, that’s his. You can offer help. But offering is a choice, and it should be a deliberate one, not a reflex.

Ask before you fix. When someone tells you about a problem, try asking “What would help most right now?” or “Do you want advice, or do you just want me to listen?” Often they just want to be heard, and you’ve been doing a lot of unnecessary rescuing. When they do want help, they’ll tell you what kind, and you can decide whether you can give it.

Offer help with edges. Instead of open-ended support, offer a specific amount. “I can talk for twenty minutes before I need to start dinner.” “I can help you move on Saturday morning, until noon.” “I can lend you two hundred dollars, and I’ll need it back by the end of the month.” Edges let you be generous without being swallowed. They also make it obvious when someone keeps pushing past them.

Let people have their feelings. This one is subtle. When someone near you is unhappy, you don’t always have to do anything. You can let them be disappointed, frustrated, or sad, and simply stay present. “That sounds really hard” is often enough. Rushing to fix someone’s feelings can actually communicate that their feelings are a problem. Sitting with them communicates that they’re allowed.

Steady is not cold

Some readers worry, at this point, that the advice amounts to caring less. That if they stop absorbing everyone’s pain, they’ll become the kind of hard, distant person they never wanted to be.

That’s not what happens. Most people who learn to care without carrying find that they become more caring, not less. They have more energy for the people who matter. They listen better, because they’re not busy planning how to fix everything. Their help is more useful, because it’s chosen rather than compulsive. And their relationships become more honest, because they stop quietly resenting the people they’re helping.

Think of the calmest person you know in a crisis. The nurse, the teacher, the friend everyone calls when something terrible happens. They’re usually not the ones crying alongside you. They’re steady. They care deeply, and because they’re steady, they can actually help. That’s the goal. Not less warmth. Warmth with a spine.

This week’s exercise: the empathy budget

Open your journal. This takes ten minutes now and a few minutes each evening.

Step one. List the five people who take the most of your emotional energy right now. Next to each name, write roughly how many hours a week go to them: listening, worrying, helping, managing.

Step two. For each person, write which mode you’re usually in with them: feeling it with them, steady care, or taking over.

Step three. Pick one person where you’re mostly in “taking over” mode. Write down one specific thing you’ve been doing for them that is really theirs to do. A phone call, a decision, a payment, a problem.

Step four. This week, do one small experiment with that person. Ask “what would help most?” before jumping in, or offer help with a clear edge, or simply don’t fix one thing you normally would. Then write down what happened. Did they cope? Did they find another way? Did the sky fall? Usually it doesn’t.

Step five. Each evening, note one moment when you caught yourself absorbing someone else’s emotion, and whether asking “whose feeling is this?” changed anything.

Field note The people who lean on you hardest are rarely the ones who would collapse without you. They’re the ones who have never had to find out. Sometimes the kindest thing you can offer is the chance to discover they’re stronger than either of you thought.

The end of Part One

You’ve now seen the whole pattern. The 3 a.m. replay that keeps score. The contract you signed before you could read. The tells that show people where to press. The voice that argues for the other side. And the empathy that turns other people’s feelings into your emergencies.

Understanding all this is necessary. It isn’t enough. Knowing why you say yes doesn’t, on its own, make it easier to say no when your heart is pounding and someone is disappointed in front of you. That takes practice.

Part Two is the practice. It starts with three days designed to get you an early win, then moves into a 21-day plan, about fifteen minutes a day. You’ll keep using your journal. You’ll do small, slightly uncomfortable exercises in low-stakes situations, so that when the high-stakes moments arrive, the new response is already familiar.

Turn the page when you’re ready to start.

The Reset

Chapter 6

The 3-Day Quick Start

Reading about a pattern and changing it are two different activities. You can understand every clause of the Good Person Contract and still say “sure, no problem” the next time someone drops a task on your desk at 4:40 on a Friday. Insight doesn’t reach the part of you that reacts in the moment. Practice does.

So before the full 21-day reset, here are three days designed to get you a quick, concrete win. They’re short and slightly uncomfortable on purpose. By the end of day three you’ll have done three things most accommodating people avoid for years, and you’ll have proof, in your own journal, that nothing terrible happened.

That proof matters more than any argument in this book. The voice in your head from Chapter 4 runs on predictions: if you do this, something bad will happen. The fastest way to weaken it is to do the thing and write down what actually happened. Three days of that is enough to crack the pattern open.

Before you start

A few ground rules.

Pick three ordinary days. Not a holiday, not a day with a huge deadline, not the day of a family wedding. Ordinary days with ordinary interactions give you the most practice with the least risk.

Start small and stay small. The quick start is not the moment to confront your father about your childhood or tell your boss exactly what you think of her. You’ll be working with everyday requests and low-stakes conversations. Big conversations come later, once the new responses feel familiar.

Keep your journal close. Each day has a short morning note and an evening entry. The evening entry is where the real change happens, so don’t skip it.

Expect discomfort. You’ll probably feel a tight chest, a flush of guilt, or an urge to take it back. That’s normal. It’s the old contract objecting. You don’t have to make the feeling go away. You just have to let it be there while you do the exercise anyway.

A word on safety. If someone in your life is abusive, threatening, or dangerous, don’t use these exercises on that person. Practice with everyone else. Chapter 16 and the playbooks at the back deal with difficult people more directly, and some situations need outside support, not just new habits.

Day 1: The pause

Today’s rule: don’t agree to anything instantly.

When someone asks you for something today, anything at all, don’t say yes on the spot. Instead, buy time. Use one of these:

  • “Let me check and get back to you.”
  • “I need to look at my week first.”
  • “Give me an hour and I’ll let you know.”

That’s it. You don’t have to say no. You can come back later and agree to every single request if you still want to. The only thing you’re changing today is the speed.

Why it works. The instant yes is the loudest tell from Chapter 3. It tells people your time doesn’t require thought. Removing it does two things. It gives you a moment to notice what you actually want, separate from the pressure of someone standing in front of you. And it changes the signal you send. People notice, even if they don’t say so, that your agreement now takes a moment. Your time starts to look a little more valuable.

What you’ll probably feel. A strong urge to just say yes and get it over with. A small worry that the other person is annoyed. Maybe a sense that you’re being rude. You aren’t. “Let me check” is one of the most ordinary sentences in adult life. People say it to each other all day long.

Morning note. Write: “Today I don’t agree to anything on the spot.” Then write the name of one person you expect to ask you for something today.

Evening entry. Answer these:

  1. How many requests did you get today?
  2. How many times did you pause instead of agreeing instantly? How many times did the instant yes slip out anyway?
  3. For the requests you paused on, what did you decide once you’d had time to think? Did you agree to all of them, or did some look different with a little distance?
  4. How did people react to “let me check”? Write what actually happened, not what you feared.

Most people find that nobody reacted at all. A few find that one or two requests quietly disappeared once they didn’t get an instant yes. That tells you something useful too.

Day 2: No apologies, and one small ask

Today’s rules: stop the reflex apologies, and ask for one thing you expect to be refused.

Part one: no unneeded apologies

For the whole day, catch every “sorry” that isn’t a real apology. A real apology is for something you actually did wrong. A reflex apology is for existing. “Sorry, quick question.” “Sorry to bother you.” “Sorry, can I just squeeze past?” “Sorry,” when someone else bumps into you.

When you catch one, swap it:

  • “Sorry to bother you” becomes “Do you have a minute?”
  • “Sorry for the late reply” becomes “Thanks for waiting.”
  • “Sorry, I just wanted to check” becomes “Quick check:”
  • “Sorry, that’s probably a dumb idea” becomes “Here’s an idea.”

You’ll miss some. That’s fine. Keep a tally of the ones you catch, whether or not you stopped them in time.

Part two: one small ask

Today, ask for something small that you fully expect to be refused. This is rejection practice. The goal is to hear “no” and notice that you’re fine.

Some examples:

  • Ask for a discount at a shop or café.
  • Ask a restaurant if you can have a table by the window, or a dish that isn’t on the menu.
  • Ask a coworker if they’d swap a task with you.
  • Ask a store if they’ll match a competitor’s price.
  • Ask a friend to pick the restaurant this time, and then let them.

Keep it light and polite. “Is there any chance you could do it for a little less?” If they say no, say “No problem, thanks for checking,” and move on. If they say yes, which happens more often than you’d think, enjoy it.

Why it works. Reflex apologies tell people you consider yourself the one who owes. Removing them changes how you’re perceived and, slowly, how you perceive yourself. The small ask goes after something deeper: the fear of being refused. Most accommodating people avoid asking for anything because hearing “no” feels like rejection of them as a person. Hearing a polite “no” over something trivial, and surviving it easily, starts to separate the two.

Morning note. Write: “No reflex apologies today. One small ask.” Then decide on your small ask and write it down.

Evening entry.

  1. How many reflex apologies did you catch? How many did you stop?
  2. Which swap felt strangest? Which felt surprisingly natural?
  3. What did you ask for, and what happened?
  4. Before you asked, what did the voice in your head predict? How did that compare with what actually happened?

Day 3: One clean no, and one held silence

Today’s rules: say no to one request without an excuse, and let one pause sit without filling it.

Part one: one clean no

Sometime today, decline one request. Pick something low-stakes: a meeting you don’t need to attend, an invitation you don’t want, a favor that would cost you more than you want to give. Say no, briefly and kindly, without explaining why.

Some ways to say it:

  • “Thanks for thinking of me, but I can’t make it.”
  • “I’m going to pass on this one.”
  • “That doesn’t work for me.”
  • “I can’t take that on right now.”

Then stop talking. No list of reasons. No “but maybe next time.” No apology. If they ask why, you can say “It just doesn’t work for me this time,” and leave it there.

If no request comes your way today, find one you’ve been putting off answering, an email, a text, an invitation, and decline it the same way.

Part two: one held silence

In one conversation today, let a pause go on longer than feels comfortable. When the other person stops talking, especially after a request or a criticism, don’t rush in. Count to ten slowly in your head. Breathe out slowly while you count. Then respond.

You don’t have to say anything clever when the silence ends. Often the other person will speak first, and what they say will tell you more than anything you could have said. Sometimes they soften their request. Sometimes they explain what they actually meant. Sometimes they just move on.

Why it works. The clean no goes straight at the over-explanation tell from Chapter 3. Every reason you give is an opening for someone to argue with. A no without reasons is much harder to negotiate away. And it teaches you, physically, that you can decline without justifying yourself. The held silence removes another lever. People who rush to fill pauses often fill them with concessions. When you can sit in a silence calmly, that pressure stops working on you.

Morning note. Write: “One clean no. One held silence.” If you already know what you’ll decline, write it down.

Evening entry.

  1. What did you say no to, and exactly what words did you use?
  2. Did you add any reasons or apologies? Be honest.
  3. How did the other person respond? What did you fear, and what actually happened?
  4. Where did you hold a silence? What happened during and after it?

After the three days

Take ten minutes on the evening of day three, or the next morning, and read back through all three evening entries. Then write a short answer to one final question:

What did I find out that the voice in my head didn’t want me to know?

For most people the answer looks something like this. People didn’t mind as much as I expected. Nobody got angry. One person seemed a little surprised, and then got over it. I felt awful for about ten minutes and then it passed. A couple of requests I would normally have said yes to turned out not to matter.

Write your version. It’s evidence, and you’ll need it. Over the next three weeks, the voice will keep making its predictions, and you’ll be able to look back at this page and remember how often it was wrong.

If the three days went badly, if someone did react strongly, write that down too. A strong reaction to a small change is information. It often tells you which relationships depend most on your old pattern, and which people Part Three is going to be most useful for.

Field note The first clean no is usually the hardest one you’ll ever say. Not because it’s a big no. Because it’s the first time you find out that you’re allowed.
Chapter 7

How the 21 Days Work

The quick start gave you a taste. Three days, three small acts, and probably a few surprises about how little resistance you actually met. Now comes the part that makes those changes stick.

The 21-day reset is built around one idea: you change a reaction by repeating a new one, in small doses, until it stops feeling foreign. Nobody gets comfortable saying no by reading about it. You get comfortable by saying it in low-stakes situations, again and again, until the high-stakes situations stop feeling quite so high.

This chapter explains how the three weeks are put together, what a typical day looks like, and the few rules that make the difference between people who finish and people who drift off by day nine.

The shape of the three weeks

Each week has one theme. They build on each other, so do them in order.

Week 1: Notice. You can’t change a pattern you can’t see in the moment. This week trains you to catch your tells, your clauses, and the pressure being applied to you, in real time, while you’re still in the conversation. You won’t be asked to change much yet. Seeing clearly is the whole job.

Week 2: Pause. Once you can see the pattern, you create space before reacting. This week is about the gap between someone’s request and your response. You’ll practice delaying, checking what you actually want, and setting small limits. The goal is to stop answering on autopilot.

Week 3: Hold. Now you practice staying put. Saying no without explaining. Staying calm when someone sulks, pushes, or gets annoyed. Letting a disagreement exist without rushing to fix it. This is the week where the old contract objects most loudly, and also the week where most people notice the biggest change.

At the end of the three weeks you’ll go back to the replay you wrote in Chapter 1 and write a new answer to it, one you could actually say.

What a day looks like

Each day takes about fifteen minutes in total, split into three small parts.

Morning (3 minutes). Read the day’s focus. Write one line in your journal about where you expect to use it today. “My team meeting at ten.” “Dinner with my sister.” Choosing the moment in advance makes it far more likely you’ll remember when it arrives.

During the day (as it happens). Do the day’s drill when the moment comes. Most drills take seconds: a pause, a different word, a question instead of an answer. You don’t need to stop your life to do them. You just need to remember.

Evening (10 minutes). Answer the day’s journal prompts. This is the most important part. Writing turns a vague experience into something you can look at and learn from. It also builds the record of evidence that weakens the voice in your head. Don’t skip the evening entry, even on days when the drill didn’t go well. Especially on those days.

The courage drills

Throughout the reset, you’ll use a set of short exercises I call courage drills. Each one trains the same underlying skill: tolerating someone else’s mild disappointment without rushing to fix it. That skill is what every boundary, every request, and every calm response in this book depends on.

Here they are, with a short explanation of each. You’ll be told which ones to use on which days.

Rejection practice. Ask for something small that you expect to be refused. A discount, a better table, a favor from someone who will probably say no. The goal is to hear “no” and notice that you’re fine.

One “no” a day. Decline one request, briefly and kindly, without an excuse.

The no-apology day. Catch and swap every reflex “sorry” for something accurate.

Slow replies. For one day, don’t answer any message instantly. Wait at least an hour unless it’s genuinely urgent. This breaks the habit of treating every request as an emergency and teaches the people around you that you have your own schedule.

The ten-second silence. In one conversation, let a pause sit for a slow count of ten before you speak.

Send it back. Return something: a wrong order at a restaurant, a faulty item, a meal that’s cold. Politely, without apologizing, and without guilt.

Eat alone. Go to a café or restaurant by yourself and stay for at least twenty minutes, without your phone. This one sounds unrelated, but it isn’t. Many accommodating people are uncomfortable being seen alone, or being in public without the protection of being busy or helpful. Sitting with that discomfort builds the same muscle as holding a boundary.

The calm-down breath. Before a tense moment, or during one, breathe in normally, then breathe out slowly and fully, making the exhale noticeably longer than the inhale. Do it two or three times. It’s simple, nobody can tell you’re doing it, and many people find it helps them stay steady enough to say what they meant.

Optional: the cold shower. Some people like a physical willpower drill: finish your shower with thirty seconds of cold water. It’s not required, and it isn’t the point of the program. If you have any heart condition or other health concern, check with your doctor first, or simply skip it.

You’ll notice that only a few of these involve the difficult people in your life. That’s deliberate. You’ll practice first with strangers, shop staff, and low-stakes acquaintances, where a strange reaction costs you nothing. The skills carry over. By the time you need them with your boss or your mother, you’ll have used them dozens of times already.

The five rules

People who finish the reset tend to follow these. People who drift off usually broke one of them.

Rule one: low stakes first. Every week starts with easy situations and moves toward harder ones. Don’t jump ahead. If you try to use a week-three skill in a high-stakes conversation during week one, you’ll probably fold, and the voice in your head will use it as proof that this doesn’t work. Build the habit where it’s easy, then bring it to where it’s hard.

Rule two: no big confrontations. The reset is not the time for the dramatic showdown you’ve been rehearsing at 3 a.m. for years. It’s tempting. Resist it. A sudden confrontation after years of accommodating usually shocks the other person, overwhelms you, and ends with an apology and a quick return to the old ways. Small, steady changes are much harder for people to resist, because there’s nothing dramatic to push back against.

Rule three: missed days are fine. Life happens. If you miss a day, don’t start over, and don’t try to double up. Just pick up where you left off. A reset that takes 25 days instead of 21 works perfectly well. A reset you abandon because you missed day eight doesn’t work at all.

Rule four: write it down every evening. Even two lines. Even on bad days. The written record is what lets you see your progress, which is often invisible from inside a single day. Most people who look back over their journal at the end of week two are surprised by how far they’ve come.

Rule five: expect pushback, and don’t treat it as failure. When you change how you respond, some people will notice and push to get the old you back. A coworker might seem irritated that you didn’t immediately agree. A relative might comment that you’ve “changed.” A partner might test a new limit two or three times. This is normal. It’s actually a sign the change is real. Chapter 3 described how people test boundaries. You’re now on the receiving end of those tests. Holding steady through them is the whole point of week three.

A note on difficult relationships

Most people do the reset with a mix of ordinary relationships: coworkers, friends, family members who are sometimes difficult but not dangerous. If that’s you, everything in Part Two applies directly.

If someone in your life is abusive, physically threatening, or controls your money or safety, things are different. Don’t use these exercises to confront that person. Practice with everyone else, and build your confidence where it’s safe to do so. Chapter 16 covers intimidation in more depth, and the playbooks at the back include situations involving controlling partners and family members. But some situations call for support beyond a book: a counselor, a trusted friend, a domestic violence hotline, or a lawyer. Getting that support is not weakness. It’s good strategy.

Tracking your progress

At the start of each week, you’ll fill in a short check-in. At the end of the reset, you’ll compare all three. The check-in asks you to rate, from 0 to 10, how true each of these statements feels right now:

  1. I can say no without over-explaining.
  2. I can let someone be disappointed in me without rushing to fix it.
  3. I can stay calm when someone gets annoyed or upset with me.
  4. I can ask for what I want directly.
  5. I notice pressure while it’s happening, not just afterward.

Write your first set of scores before you begin Week 1. Don’t overthink them. Your gut answer is fine. You won’t see dramatic changes from one day to the next, but over three weeks most people’s scores move noticeably. Seeing that movement on paper is one of the most motivating parts of the whole program.

Field note The people who get the most out of the reset are rarely the most disciplined. They’re the ones who keep going after a bad day. A bad day on day eleven, written down honestly, teaches more than a perfect week.
Chapter 8

Week 1, Notice

This week you won’t be asked to change much. You’ll be asked to see.

That sounds easy. It isn’t. Most accommodating behavior happens on autopilot, in the half second between someone’s request and your answer. By the time you notice what you’ve done, the yes is already out, the apology already said, the extra work already on your desk. This week trains you to catch the pattern while it’s happening. Once you can see it in the moment, changing it becomes possible. Until then, it isn’t.

Before you start, fill in your first check-in from Chapter 7. Rate each of the five statements from 0 to 10. Then begin.

Day 1: The yes count

Focus. Count every time you agree to something today.

Drill. Keep a tally in your journal or your phone. Every yes counts: agreeing to a request, accepting an invitation, nodding along to an opinion you don’t share, saying “that’s fine” when it isn’t. Next to each one, put a mark: M if you meant it, D if you didn’t.

Don’t try to say no today. Just count.

Evening prompts.

  1. How many yeses did you count? How many were D?
  2. Who got the most D answers from you?
  3. Pick one D yes. What were you afraid would happen if you’d said no?

Most people are startled by the number. A common result is somewhere between fifteen and thirty yeses in a day, with a third or more marked D. That’s the pattern, finally visible.

Day 2: Catch your clause

Focus. Watch your main clause from Chapter 2 at work.

Drill. Look at the page where you circled your strongest clause: other people’s feelings are your job, your needs are a burden, conflict means something has gone wrong, if you’re good enough you’ll be treated fairly, or if you stop being nice you’ll become one of them. Today, every time you notice that clause shaping what you do, write a one-line note. “Clause one: offered to call the client back myself because my colleague sounded stressed.”

Evening prompts.

  1. How many times did you catch your clause today?
  2. In which part of your life did it show up most: work, partner, family, or friends?
  3. Write the adult revision of your clause again, in your own words. Did reading it today change anything, even slightly?

Day 3: The tell tally, and no apologies

Focus. Track your top two tells from Chapter 3, and run your first full no-apology day.

Drill. Keep a tally for your two strongest tells. The instant yes, the long explanation, the reflex apology, filling the silence, the disappointment flinch, the approval hunt, the no follow-up, or the visible strain. Just a mark each time.

Run the no-apology day at the same time. Catch every reflex “sorry” and swap it for something accurate. “Thanks for waiting” instead of “sorry for the delay.” “Do you have a minute?” instead of “sorry to bother you.”

Evening prompts.

  1. What were your tallies for each tell?
  2. How many reflex apologies did you catch? How many did you manage to swap?
  3. Did anyone react differently when you didn’t apologize? Most people find nobody noticed at all.

Day 4: Spot the opening

Focus. When someone applies pressure, identify which need they’re pressing on.

Drill. Chapter 3 listed six openings: guilt, the need for approval, fear of conflict, the need to be seen as good, loneliness or fear of loss, and urgency. Today, whenever you feel pulled to do something you didn’t plan to do, ask yourself silently: which one is this?

You’ll often find a specific phrase or gesture behind it. A sigh (guilt). “You’re always so reliable” (approval, and the need to be seen as good). A raised voice (fear of conflict). “I need to know by end of day” (urgency).

Evening prompts.

  1. List each moment of pressure you noticed today, and which opening it pressed on.
  2. Which opening came up most? Is it the same one that shows up in your replay log from Chapter 1?
  3. Who presses on that opening most often?

By now you’re building a picture of exactly where you’re most exposed. That’s what the rest of the reset will reinforce.

Day 5: Hear the voice, and slow replies

Focus. Catch the voice in your head as it talks you out of things, and run your first slow-reply day.

Drill. Keep adding to your transcript from Chapter 4. Every time the voice speaks up, write its exact words. “It’s not worth it.” “They’re stressed.” “Who are you to ask?” Note which job each line is doing.

At the same time, run the slow-reply drill. Don’t answer any message instantly today. Unless something is genuinely urgent, wait at least an hour before replying to texts, emails, and work messages. Notice the urge to respond right away, and notice what happens when you don’t.

Evening prompts.

  1. Write down the voice’s three most frequent lines today.
  2. For each one, write the fair version, the one a friend on your side would say.
  3. What happened when you didn’t reply instantly? Did anyone follow up impatiently? Did anything bad happen? Did you find yourself thinking more clearly about what to say?

Day 6: Whose feeling is it?

Focus. Separate your emotions from the ones you pick up from others.

Drill. Chapter 5 described how easily accommodating people absorb other people’s moods. Today, whenever you notice a strong feeling, ask: is this mine, or did I just pick it up from someone? If it came from someone else, say silently, “This is theirs.” You don’t need to push it away. Just label it.

Also try the “ask before you fix” habit at least once. When someone tells you about a problem, ask, “What would help most right now?” before you offer any advice or help.

Evening prompts.

  1. Describe one moment when you realized a feeling you were carrying had started with someone else. What happened when you labeled it?
  2. Did you ask “What would help most?” What did the person say? Was it what you expected?
  3. Who do you absorb emotions from most often?

Day 7: Review, and rejection practice

Focus. Look back at the week, and practice hearing “no.”

Drill. Do one round of rejection practice today. Ask for something small that you expect to be refused. A discount, a better seat, a favor from someone who will probably say no. Keep it light and polite. When you hear “no,” say “No problem, thanks for checking,” and move on.

Then set aside twenty minutes this evening for the weekly review.

Evening prompts.

  1. What did you ask for, and what happened?
  2. Read back through your journal entries for the whole week. Write down the three most important things you noticed about your pattern.
  3. Which day was hardest? Which was easiest?
  4. Fill in your second check-in, rating the five statements from Chapter 7 again. Compare your scores with the first set. Don’t worry if they’ve barely moved. This week was about seeing, not changing. The bigger movement usually comes in weeks two and three.

What usually happens in Week 1

A few patterns are common enough to mention.

You’ll catch things after the fact at first. On days one and two, most people notice their yes or their apology a few seconds or minutes too late. That’s normal. By day five or six, many people start catching it just before it happens. That shift, from noticing afterward to noticing in the moment, is the main achievement of this week.

You might feel worse before you feel better. Seeing the full size of the pattern can be discouraging. Some people feel embarrassed or angry with themselves when they count their D yeses. Try to treat what you find with curiosity rather than judgment. You’re not discovering that you’re broken. You’re discovering how a strategy you built long ago has been running your days. That’s useful information, not a verdict.

Some things change on their own. Just by watching, many people find they say a few fewer automatic yeses by the end of the week, without trying. Attention alone starts to loosen the pattern.

You’ll see one or two people more clearly. Almost everyone finishes Week 1 with a sharper sense of who presses on their openings most often. Keep those names in mind. They’re the relationships where weeks two and three will matter most.

Field note Noticing feels passive, but it isn’t. The moment you can see a pattern while it’s happening, you’re no longer fully inside it. Part of you is watching, and the part that watches is the part that can choose differently.
Chapter 9

Week 2, Pause

Last week you learned to see the pattern while it’s happening. This week you learn to put space in front of it.

Most accommodating behavior lives in a very small gap: the half second between someone asking and you answering. In that half second, the old contract takes over. You agree, apologize, offer, explain, before your own judgment has a chance to speak. Week 2 is about widening that gap. A few seconds, an hour, a day. Enough time for you to notice what you actually want, and then decide.

You’ll also start setting small limits this week. Not dramatic ones. Small, specific edges around your time and help, mostly with people where little is at stake. By the end of the week, the pause should feel like a normal part of how you answer people.

Day 8: The buffer

Focus. Put a buffer between every request and your answer.

Drill. For the whole day, respond to every request with a buffer instead of an answer. You practiced this on day one of the quick start. Now make it standard.

  • “Let me check and get back to you.”
  • “I’ll look at my week and let you know by this afternoon.”
  • “Let me think about it.”

When you come back with your answer, it can be yes. The buffer isn’t about refusing. It’s about making sure your answer comes from you, not from the pressure of the moment.

Add the calm-down breath from Chapter 7 before any conversation you expect to be tense: breathe in normally, breathe out slowly and fully, two or three times.

Evening prompts.

  1. How many times did you use a buffer today? How many times did an instant answer slip out?
  2. For the requests you buffered, did your final answer differ from what you would have said on the spot?
  3. Did anyone push back on the buffer? What did they say, and how did you respond?

Day 9: The want check

Focus. Before you answer, find out what you actually want.

Drill. Each time you’re asked for something today, use your buffer, then ask yourself one question before you respond:

If nobody would be disappointed either way, what would I choose?

That question strips away the pressure and shows you your real preference. Sometimes the answer is yes. You genuinely want to help, go to the dinner, take the project. Good. Sometimes it’s no. Sometimes it’s “yes, but not like that.”

You don’t have to act on your answer yet. Just find it, and write it down.

Evening prompts.

  1. List each request from today and your honest answer to the want check.
  2. In how many cases did your real preference differ from what you would normally have said?
  3. Which requests were hardest to check honestly? Why do you think that is?

Many people discover on day nine that they’ve lost touch with what they want. The answer to “what would I choose?” comes back blank, or comes back as “whatever’s easiest for everyone.” That’s common, and it changes with practice. The more often you ask, the more clearly the answer starts to come.

Day 10: Help with edges

Focus. When you say yes, put a clear limit on it.

Drill. Chapter 5 introduced the idea of offering help with edges. Today, when you agree to something, define exactly what you’re agreeing to. Turn open-ended yeses into specific ones.

  • “I can help you move, Saturday from nine until noon.”
  • “I can look over the report, but I won’t have time to rewrite it.”
  • “I can talk for twenty minutes, then I need to start dinner.”
  • “I’ll cover this shift, and I’ll need you to take my Thursday.”

Then keep to the edge. When noon comes on Saturday, you leave. When twenty minutes are up, you say, “I need to go now. I hope tomorrow goes well.”

Evening prompts.

  1. Write down every edge you set today, in the exact words you used.
  2. Did you keep to the edge, or did it slip? If it slipped, what happened right before?
  3. How did the other person react to the limit?

Edges are one of the most useful tools in the whole book, because they let you stay generous without being swallowed. They also make patterns visible. If someone keeps pushing past every edge you set, you’ve learned something important about that person.

Day 11: The ten-second silence, and a small boundary

Focus. Let silence work for you, and set one small limit with someone you know.

Drill. Use the ten-second silence at least once today. When someone makes a request or a criticism and stops talking, count slowly to ten while breathing out before you respond. Notice what the other person does with the silence.

Then set one small boundary with someone you know, in a low-stakes situation. Some examples:

  • End a phone call when you planned to, instead of letting it run.
  • Leave work at your normal time, even though others are staying.
  • Tell a friend you’d rather meet somewhere else this time.
  • Ask a family member not to bring up a particular topic at dinner.
  • Tell a coworker you’re not available for messages after 7 p.m.

Say it simply. “I’m going to head off now.” “I’d rather not talk about that tonight.” No long explanations.

Evening prompts.

  1. Where did you use the ten-second silence? What happened during it?
  2. What boundary did you set, and with whom? What exact words did you use?
  3. What did the voice in your head predict would happen? What actually happened?

Day 12: One clean no

Focus. Decline one request from someone you know.

Drill. In the quick start, you said no to something low-stakes. Today, choose a request from someone you have an ongoing relationship with, a coworker, a friend, a relative, and decline it. Still keep the stakes modest. This isn’t the day to turn down your boss’s biggest project. It’s the day to skip the optional meeting, decline the third favor this month, or pass on the weekend plans you’ve been dreading.

Use the buffer first if you need to. Then say no without excuses.

  • “I’m not going to be able to do that.”
  • “I’ll pass this time, but thanks for asking.”
  • “That doesn’t work for me.”

If they ask why, you can say, “It just doesn’t work for me this time,” and stop there. If they push, repeat yourself calmly. You don’t need a new reason. The same sentence, said again in the same tone, is enough.

Evening prompts.

  1. Who did you say no to, and what did you decline?
  2. Did you add reasons, apologies, or “maybe next time”? Write exactly what you said.
  3. How did they respond? How did you feel an hour later? The next morning?

Day 13: Send it back

Focus. Practice asking for what you paid for, without guilt.

Drill. Return something or ask for something to be fixed. A wrong order at a café, a meal that’s cold, a faulty item, an incorrect charge on a bill, a service that wasn’t done properly. If nothing goes wrong today, look for something from the past few weeks you’ve been meaning to return or dispute, and deal with it.

Be polite and direct. “This isn’t what I ordered. Could you bring the one I asked for?” “I was charged twice for this. Could you fix that, please?” No apology, no joke to soften it, no “it’s not a big deal, but.”

Evening prompts.

  1. What did you send back or dispute? What did you say?
  2. Did you feel the urge to apologize or minimize? Did you manage not to?
  3. How was the request received?

This drill can feel trivial. It isn’t. Accommodating people often accept the wrong order, the overcharge, and the shoddy work because complaining feels rude. The same instinct, at a larger scale, keeps them accepting unfair treatment at work and at home. Practicing with a cold coffee is practice for everything else.

Day 14: Eat alone, and review

Focus. Sit with a different kind of discomfort, then look back at the week.

Drill. Go to a café or restaurant by yourself and stay for at least twenty minutes without your phone. Order, eat or drink, look around, think. Notice any urge to look busy, to apologize to the server for taking up a table, or to leave early.

Then take twenty minutes this evening for the weekly review.

Evening prompts.

  1. How did it feel to eat alone without your phone? What did you notice about yourself?
  2. Read back through your journal entries for the week. Which drill made the biggest difference? Which was hardest?
  3. Did anyone push back on your changes this week? How did you handle it?
  4. Fill in your third check-in, rating the five statements from Chapter 7. Compare it with your first two.

What usually happens in Week 2

The buffer becomes a habit faster than you’d expect. By day ten or eleven, many people find that “let me check” comes out almost automatically. It’s one of the quickest changes to stick, and one of the most valuable.

Your preferences start to come back. If the want check came back blank at first, you’ll probably notice it getting clearer by the end of the week. You’ll start to know, sooner, what you’d actually choose.

Some people start testing you. This is the week when habit-formers and opportunists from Chapter 3 usually notice the change. You might get a slightly irritated reaction to a buffer, a second push after a no, or a comment like “you’re being weird lately.” That’s the test. It’s normal, and it usually fades if you stay consistent.

The guilt doesn’t vanish, but it gets shorter. Most people still feel a pang after their clean no on day twelve. The difference is that the pang lasts minutes instead of days. That shortening is the real sign of progress.

Chapter 10

Week 3, Hold

You can see the pattern now, and you can put space in front of it. This week is about the moment after you’ve said what you meant, when the other person doesn’t like it.

That moment is where most accommodating people fold. They say no, and the other person sighs, or pushes, or goes quiet, or says “really?” in a wounded voice. And within seconds the no becomes “well, maybe,” then “I suppose I could,” then “of course, don’t worry about it.” The boundary was set and then given back, usually within the same conversation.

Week 3 trains you to stay put. Calmly, without escalating, without getting defensive, and without taking it back. This is the hardest week of the reset. It’s also where most people feel the biggest shift.

Day 15: The broken record

Focus. When someone pushes back on a no, repeat it calmly instead of adding reasons.

Drill. At some point today, decline something. If the other person pushes, don’t argue, don’t explain more, and don’t offer a compromise you don’t want. Just say the same thing again, in the same calm tone. Then again if needed.

It sounds like this:

“Can you take the Henderson account this week?” “I can’t take that on right now.” “It’s really just a few hours. You’re the only one who knows the system.” “I understand. I can’t take it on right now.” “Could you at least look at it?” “Not this week. I can’t take it on.”

Each repetition is the same sentence, or very close to it. No new reasons for them to argue with. No rising frustration in your voice. Most people stop pushing after the second or third repetition, because there’s nothing left to push against.

Evening prompts.

  1. Where did you use the broken record? Write the exchange as closely as you can remember it.
  2. How many times did you have to repeat yourself?
  3. Did you feel the urge to add reasons or soften the no? What did you do with that urge?

Day 16: Let them be disappointed

Focus. Allow someone to be unhappy with your decision without trying to fix their feelings.

Drill. Today, when someone is disappointed by something you’ve said or decided, don’t rush to make it better. Acknowledge their feeling once, briefly, then let it be.

  • “I know you were hoping I’d say yes.”
  • “I can see you’re disappointed.”
  • “I understand that’s frustrating.”

Then stop. Don’t follow with “but maybe I could…” Don’t offer a consolation prize. Don’t keep checking whether they’re okay. Let them have their reaction. It belongs to them.

This is clause one of the Good Person Contract, renegotiated in real time. Other people’s feelings are real and they matter. They are also theirs.

Evening prompts.

  1. Who was disappointed with you today, and about what?
  2. What did you say? Did you manage to acknowledge once and then stop?
  3. How long did their disappointment last? How long did your discomfort last?

Most people find that the other person’s disappointment passes faster than they expected, often within minutes. Their own guilt tends to outlast it. Write down both. The difference is worth seeing.

Day 17: Answer a guilt trip

Focus. When someone uses guilt to change your mind, respond to the feeling without giving in to the request.

Drill. Guilt is the most common pressure accommodating people face, and Part Three covers it in depth. Today, practice the basic response. When someone uses a guilt line, acknowledge what they said, then restate your position.

“I guess I’ll just do it all myself, like always.” “I know it feels like a lot. I’m not able to help this weekend.”

“After everything I’ve done for you…” “I know you’ve done a lot for me, and I’m grateful. I’m still not going to be able to make it.”

“Fine. I thought I could count on you.” “I understand you’re upset. My answer is still no.”

Notice the shape: acknowledge, then hold. You don’t argue about whether they really do everything themselves. You don’t defend your record as a good person. You just refuse to let the guilt change the answer.

Evening prompts.

  1. Did anyone use guilt on you today? Write down their exact words.
  2. How did you respond? Did you acknowledge and hold, or did the guilt work?
  3. Which kind of guilt line hits you hardest? Note it for Part Three.

Day 18: Follow up on something

Focus. Raise one thing you’d normally let slide.

Drill. Chapter 3 described the no follow-up tell: you agree to something reasonable, the other person doesn’t do their part, and you let it go. Today, follow up on one thing. Someone owes you money. A coworker promised to send something and didn’t. A friend said they’d return a favor. A partner agreed to handle a chore that’s still undone.

Keep it neutral and specific. No accusation, no sarcasm, no long preamble.

  • “Hey, just checking on the two hundred dollars from last month. When can you get that back to me?”
  • “You mentioned you’d send the draft by Tuesday. Can I expect it today?”
  • “We agreed you’d handle the recycling this week. It’s still sitting there.”

Then wait for the answer. Don’t fill the silence with “no rush” or “it’s totally fine if not.”

Evening prompts.

  1. What did you follow up on, and with whom?
  2. What exactly did you say? Did you soften it more than you meant to?
  3. How did they respond?

Day 19: Ask for something you want

Focus. Make one direct request for something that matters to you.

Drill. So far, most of the reset has been about saying no. Today is about saying yes to yourself. Ask someone for something you genuinely want. Choose something with moderate stakes: real enough to matter, not so large that a refusal would be devastating.

Some examples:

  • Ask your manager for a meeting to discuss your role or pay.
  • Ask your partner to take on a specific task you’ve been carrying alone.
  • Ask a friend to visit you this time, instead of you always traveling.
  • Ask a family member to stop a behavior that bothers you.
  • Ask for help with something you’ve been struggling with quietly.

Make the request simple and direct. No pre-apology, no “this is probably silly, but.” State what you want, and why if it helps, then stop.

“I’d like to set up a time to talk about my salary. Could we find thirty minutes next week?” “I’d like you to take over the school pickups on Tuesdays and Thursdays.”

Evening prompts.

  1. What did you ask for, and from whom?
  2. What did the voice in your head say beforehand? Write its exact lines.
  3. What happened? Whatever the answer, how do you feel about having asked?

The answer isn’t the point today. The asking is. Many accommodating people go years without making a single direct request for something they want. If you made one today, that’s a real change, whatever they said.

Day 20: Stay in a disagreement

Focus. Hold an opinion that differs from someone else’s, and don’t take it back.

Drill. Today, when you disagree with someone, say so, and then stay with it. You don’t have to win. You don’t have to convince them. You just have to not retreat.

It might be a small thing: a restaurant choice, a film you didn’t like, an approach to a work problem. Say what you think. If they disagree, you can listen, and you can change your mind if they make a good point. What you shouldn’t do is drop your view just because the disagreement feels uncomfortable.

“I actually see it differently. I think the second option makes more sense.” “I didn’t enjoy it, honestly. The ending didn’t work for me.”

Also run another slow-reply day. No instant answers to messages, unless something is urgent.

Evening prompts.

  1. Where did you disagree today? What did you say?
  2. Did you feel the urge to back down? Did you?
  3. How did the conversation end? Was the relationship damaged, or was it fine?

Day 21: The new answer

Focus. Go back to where you started, and write the answer you can actually give.

Drill. Find the replay log you wrote at the end of Chapter 1. Read it slowly. The conversation, what the other person said, what you said, what you wished you’d said, what you were afraid of, and what they got.

Now write a new answer. Not the 3 a.m. version, with the perfect cutting line. A real one. Something short and calm that you could say, out loud, in your own voice, if that moment happened again tomorrow.

Use what you’ve practiced. A buffer. A clean no. An acknowledgment followed by a hold. A follow-up. A direct request. A pause. Then write down how you think the conversation would go from there.

Then fill in your final check-in, rating the five statements from Chapter 7 one more time.

Evening prompts.

  1. Write your new answer to the Chapter 1 replay.
  2. Compare it with the 3 a.m. version. What’s different?
  3. Lay out all four check-ins side by side. Which statement moved the most?
  4. What’s the single most important thing you learned in the last 21 days?

After the reset

Three weeks isn’t the end of this work. It’s the end of the beginning. The habits you’ve started will fade if you stop using them, and they’ll strengthen if you keep going.

A simple way to keep going: pick one courage drill each week and do it once. Rotate through them. Keep writing a short evening entry two or three times a week. And when a hard conversation comes up, use the buffer to give yourself time, then come back to this book for the specific tools you need.

You’ll also probably hear some version of “you’ve changed” in the coming weeks. From a coworker, a friend, a parent. Sometimes it’s said warmly. Sometimes it’s said with an edge. Either way, you don’t need to apologize for it. A calm “I have, a bit” is enough. The people who benefit from the old version of you will adjust, or they won’t. The people who care about you will usually be glad.

For each one, you’ll learn what it looks like, what it’s for, and exactly what to say.

Part Three

The Tactics They Use on You

You’ve spent the last two parts working on your side of the conversation: the contract you were raised with, the tells that show people where to press, the voice that argues against you, and three weeks of practice changing how you respond.

Now we turn to the other side.

Each chapter in this part covers a family of tactics. For each one, you’ll see what it sounds like in real life, what it’s designed to achieve, and the calm reply that takes away its power. The replies are deliberately short. Long answers give people more to argue with. Short ones don’t.

A reminder before we begin. Most people who use these tactics on you aren’t villains. Many have simply learned that a certain move works, and they repeat it without much thought. The replies in this part work on them as well as on the deliberate ones. In fact they work better on the ordinary ones, who usually just stop once the move stops paying off.

Chapter 11

Guilt and Obligation

Guilt is the most common pressure accommodating people face, and the most effective. It goes straight to clause one of the Good Person Contract: other people’s feelings are your job. If you can be made to feel that you’ve let someone down, or that you owe them, you’ll often do almost anything to make the feeling stop.

The tactics in this chapter all work the same way. They take a request that you could easily turn down on its merits and attach a moral weight to it. Saying no stops being a scheduling decision and becomes evidence that you’re selfish, ungrateful, or unkind.

The reply to every one of them follows the same shape, which you practiced on day 17 of the reset: acknowledge the feeling, then hold the decision. You don’t argue about whether they have a point. You don’t defend your character. You simply refuse to let the feeling change the answer.

The martyr

What it sounds like.

  • “Don’t worry about it. I’ll just do it myself, like always.”
  • “It’s fine. I’m used to handling everything.”
  • “No, you go and enjoy yourself. I’ll stay here and finish up.”

Usually delivered with a sigh, a tired face, or a pointed show of doing the task while you watch.

Where you’ll meet it. The coworker who stays late and makes sure everyone knows. The parent who does all the holiday cooking alone and mentions it for years. The partner who does the dishes loudly. The friend who always ends up “stuck” organizing the group trip.

What it’s for. The martyr doesn’t ask you directly, so you can’t decline directly. Instead, they make their suffering visible and wait for you to rescue them. If you don’t step in, you’re the person who let them struggle alone.

The calm reply. Take their words at face value. Offer something specific if you want to, or simply let them handle it.

“Okay. Let me know if you want help with something specific.”

“I can’t help tonight. I hope it goes quickly.”

If you want to address the pattern itself, do it later, calmly, when the task isn’t happening:

“I’ve noticed you often end up doing things alone and then feeling resentful. If you want my help, ask me directly, and I’ll tell you honestly whether I can.”

What not to do. Don’t rush in to take over, and don’t argue about whether they really do everything. Both keep the pattern going.

The ledger

What it sounds like.

  • “After everything I’ve done for you…”
  • “I was there for you when you needed me.”
  • “I gave up so much to raise you.”
  • “Remember when I covered for you last spring?”

Where you’ll meet it. Parents, especially as you get older and start making choices they don’t like. Friends who keep careful count of favors. Bosses who remind you of the time they went to bat for you.

What it’s for. The ledger turns a relationship into an account. Past kindness becomes a debt, and the request in front of you becomes the repayment. Refusing makes you not just unhelpful but ungrateful.

The calm reply. Acknowledge the past genuinely. Then separate it from the present decision.

“You’ve done a lot for me, and I’m grateful. I’m still not able to do this.”

“I remember, and I appreciated it. This one doesn’t work for me.”

Gratitude and agreement are separate things. You can feel one without owing the other.

What not to do. Don’t start defending your own record (“but I’ve done things for you too”). That accepts the idea that you’re both keeping score, and invites a long argument about who owes whom.

The favor with a hidden price

What it sounds like. There often aren’t any words at first. Someone does something for you that you didn’t ask for: a gift, a favor, a dinner, an introduction. Then, days or weeks later:

  • “I helped you with the move, so I figured you wouldn’t mind…”
  • “I got you those tickets, remember? So can you watch the dog this weekend?”
  • “I put in a good word for you with the director. Now I need you to back my proposal.”

What it’s for. Most people feel a strong pull to return favors. It’s one of the most reliable patterns in human behavior, and in normal relationships it’s healthy. The hidden-price favor exploits it. The giver chooses the gift, chooses the timing, and then chooses the repayment, usually something bigger than the original favor.

The calm reply. You can be grateful for a gift without accepting a bill you never agreed to.

“That was really kind of you, and I appreciated it. I can’t take the dog this weekend.”

If it keeps happening, you can decline gifts or favors from that person in advance:

“Thanks, but I’d rather handle it myself this time.”

What not to do. Don’t let the original favor set the price of the repayment. They chose to give it. You get to choose what, if anything, you give back.

The character test

What it sounds like.

  • “I thought you were someone I could count on.”
  • “I didn’t think you were that kind of person.”
  • “A real friend would do this.”
  • “I guess I know where your priorities are.”
  • “If you really cared about the team, you’d stay.”

What it’s for. This one goes beyond guilt about a single decision. It turns the request into a verdict on who you are. If you say no, you’re not just declining a favor. You’re proving that you’re unreliable, selfish, or disloyal. For anyone whose identity is built on being good, that’s a powerful threat.

The calm reply. Don’t take the bait of defending your character. Name the decision as a decision, not a judgment on who you are.

“I can be someone you count on and still not be able to do this.”

“I’m sorry you see it that way. My answer is still no.”

“I care about the team. I’m still leaving at six.”

What not to do. Don’t start listing all the times you’ve been reliable or caring. The moment you defend your character, you’ve accepted that it’s on trial.

The visible suffering

What it sounds like. Sometimes nothing at all. A long sigh. A heavy silence on the phone. A sad face. A message that says “fine.” A comment like “I’ve just been so tired lately” right after you decline something.

Where you’ll meet it. Almost everywhere. Partners and parents are especially fluent in it, but so are coworkers and friends.

What it’s for. It applies pressure without making a request you can refuse. If you notice the suffering, you feel responsible for it. If you ask what’s wrong, you often get “nothing,” which leaves you guessing and trying harder to fix it.

The calm reply. Name it once, kindly, and offer a door. Then stop.

“You seem upset. If there’s something you want to talk about, I’m happy to.”

If they say “nothing,” take them at their word.

“Okay.”

And carry on with what you were doing. You’ve offered. The rest is up to them.

What not to do. Don’t keep asking, don’t guess at the reason, and don’t start offering things to make it better. That trains the behavior.

“If you loved me…”

What it sounds like.

  • “If you loved me, you’d do this.”
  • “I guess I’m just not that important to you.”
  • “You’d do it for your friends.”
  • “I’d do it for you without even thinking.”

Where you’ll meet it. Partners, parents, and sometimes adult children. It shows up in close relationships, where the stakes feel highest.

What it’s for. It turns any request into a test of love. Agreeing proves you care. Refusing proves you don’t. It’s especially effective because love is hard to measure, so the other person gets to decide what counts as proof.

The calm reply. Separate your feelings from the request.

“I love you. I’m not going to do this.”

“You’re very important to me. My answer is still no.”

If it happens often, address the pattern directly at a calmer time:

“When you say ‘if you loved me,’ it makes it feel like every request is a test. I want to be able to say no to things without it meaning I don’t love you.”

What not to do. Don’t try to prove your love some other way to make up for the no. That confirms the idea that love has to be earned through compliance.

Why guilt works, and why it stops working

Guilt is a useful feeling. It tells you when you’ve genuinely done something wrong, and it pushes you to repair it. The tactics in this chapter hijack that feeling. They create the sensation of having done wrong when you haven’t.

A quick test helps you tell the difference. When you feel guilty, ask: Did I actually break a promise, cause harm, or act unfairly? If yes, the guilt is pointing at something real, and an apology or repair may be the right response. If no, if the guilt only appeared because someone else is unhappy with a fair decision, then it isn’t telling you anything about right and wrong. It’s just pressure.

Once you can tell the two apart, guilt tactics lose most of their force. You’ll still feel the pang. You may feel it for a long time. But you’ll stop treating it as an instruction.

This week’s exercise: your guilt map

Open your journal.

Step one. List the people who use guilt on you most often. For each one, write their most common line, word for word if you can.

Step two. Next to each line, note which tactic it is: the martyr, the ledger, the hidden-price favor, the character test, visible suffering, or “if you loved me.”

Step three. For each line, write your calm reply, using the acknowledge-then-hold shape from this chapter. Say it out loud a few times. It should feel natural in your mouth.

Step four. The next time one of these lines comes up, use the reply. Afterward, write down what happened.

Chapter 12

Rewriting the Past

Some of the most damaging tactics don’t try to change what you’ll do next. They try to change what you believe already happened.

You remember a conversation one way. The other person insists it went differently. You remember a promise. They say they never made it. You’re hurt by a comment. They say it was a joke, and you’re too sensitive. Over time, if this happens often enough, you start to doubt yourself. Maybe you did misremember. Maybe you are overreacting. Maybe the problem really is you.

This family of tactics is often called gaslighting, after an old play and film in which a husband slowly convinces his wife she’s losing her mind. The word gets used loosely now, sometimes for any disagreement about the past. People genuinely do remember things differently, and that alone isn’t manipulation. What turns it into a tactic is the pattern: the other person’s version always happens to favor them, it’s delivered with total certainty, and over time it leaves you doubting your own judgment.

The replies in this chapter rest on one principle. Don’t fight over the facts. State your experience, and make your decision. You’ll rarely win an argument about what happened, especially with someone who is determined to win it. You don’t need to. You only need to stop letting their version decide what you do.

Flat denial

What it sounds like.

  • “That never happened.”
  • “I never said that.”
  • “I didn’t promise anything.”
  • “You’re making things up.”

Where you’ll meet it. A partner denies a hurtful comment from last week. A boss denies agreeing to your time off. A parent denies events from your childhood. A coworker denies having seen the email.

What it’s for. If the event didn’t happen, there’s nothing to answer for. Flat denial ends the conversation about their behavior and starts a new one about your memory.

The calm reply. Don’t argue about the record. State what you remember, once, and move to what matters.

“I remember it differently. Either way, here’s what I need going forward.”

“We see that conversation differently. What I’m asking now is…”

At work, where records exist, use them calmly:

“I have the email from March 3rd where we agreed on this. I’ll forward it to you.”

What not to do. Don’t get pulled into a detailed reconstruction of exactly what was said, in what order, in what tone. That’s a fight you’re set up to lose, because the other person can simply keep denying.

The minimizer

What it sounds like.

  • “You’re too sensitive.”
  • “It was just a joke.”
  • “You’re blowing this out of proportion.”
  • “Why do you always make such a big deal of everything?”
  • “Everyone else thought it was funny.”

Where you’ll meet it. Family members who make cutting remarks and then laugh them off. Partners who tease in front of friends. Coworkers whose “banter” always lands on you.

What it’s for. The minimizer doesn’t deny that something happened. They deny that it matters. Your hurt becomes the problem, not their comment. And because the voice in your head from Chapter 4 often says the same things, this tactic slides in easily.

The calm reply. You don’t need their agreement that it was hurtful. You only need to say what you want.

“Maybe it was meant as a joke. It didn’t land that way for me, and I’d like you to stop.”

“I hear that you think I’m overreacting. I’m still asking you not to say that again.”

What not to do. Don’t try to prove how much it hurt, or argue about whether you’re too sensitive. Both turn the conversation into a debate about your feelings, which the minimizer will always say are wrong.

The blame shift

What it sounds like.

  • “You made me do it.”
  • “If you hadn’t said that, I wouldn’t have reacted.”
  • “This is what happens when you push me.”
  • “I wouldn’t have to check your phone if you didn’t give me reasons.”

What it’s for. It moves responsibility for their behavior onto you. Their anger, their lateness, their broken promise, all become the natural result of something you did. If you accept that, you’ll spend your energy trying to behave better to prevent their reactions.

The calm reply. Separate your actions from theirs.

“I’m responsible for what I said. You’re responsible for how you responded.”

“I’m happy to talk about what I did. I’m not going to take responsibility for what you did.”

What not to do. Don’t immediately apologize just to calm things down. If you did something genuinely wrong, you can own that separately. But an apology offered to end a blame shift usually just confirms that the blame belongs to you.

Fog

What it sounds like. Long, circular explanations that never quite answer the question. Changing the subject mid-sentence. Bringing up something unrelated you did years ago. Answering a question with a question. Getting so detailed about a side issue that the original point disappears.

Where you’ll meet it. In arguments with partners or family members who never seem to land anywhere. In meetings where a colleague talks for ten minutes and somehow never addresses the missed deadline.

What it’s for. Confusion is protective. If the conversation becomes tangled enough, you’ll get tired and drop it. By the end you may not even remember what you were trying to say.

The calm reply. Come back to the original point, simply and repeatedly.

“I want to come back to what I asked. Will you be able to pay me back by Friday?”

“That might be worth talking about another time. Right now I’m asking about the deadline.”

If the fog continues:

“We’re going in circles. Let’s pick this up tomorrow.”

What not to do. Don’t follow every new thread. Write down your main point before the conversation if you need to, and keep returning to it.

The rewrite

What it sounds like.

  • “You agreed to this. Don’t you remember?”
  • “We decided this together.”
  • “You said it was fine at the time.”
  • “You’ve always been like this.”

What it’s for. The rewrite doesn’t deny the past. It edits it. Your hesitant “I guess so” becomes enthusiastic agreement. A conversation where you raised concerns becomes one where you approved the plan. A long history of you being reasonable becomes a long history of you being difficult.

The calm reply. Correct the record once, then focus on the present.

“I don’t think I agreed to that, but either way, I’m not agreeing to it now.”

“I remember having concerns at the time. I still have them.”

What not to do. Don’t let a rewritten history decide the present. Even if you did agree to something in the past, you’re allowed to change your mind now.

“Everyone agrees with me”

What it sounds like.

  • “Everyone thinks you overreacted.”
  • “Your sister agrees with me.”
  • “I asked around, and nobody else has a problem with it.”
  • “Even your friends think you’re being unreasonable.”

What it’s for. It surrounds you with an invisible jury. Your view stops being one of two perspectives and becomes the odd one out. It’s especially effective because you usually can’t check. You don’t know what the other person said to “everyone,” or what everyone actually said back.

The calm reply. Bring it back to the two of you.

“I’m not talking with everyone. I’m talking with you.”

“Other people can have their own views. This is mine.”

What not to do. Don’t start polling people yourself to prove you’re right. That just widens the conflict.

Protecting your memory

When someone repeatedly rewrites the past, the most useful thing you can do is make your memory harder to argue with. A few practical habits help.

Write things down. After an important conversation, note the date, what was said, and what was agreed. It doesn’t have to be formal. A few lines in your journal or a note on your phone is enough. You’re not building a case. You’re giving yourself something solid to stand on when your own memory starts to feel uncertain.

Put agreements in writing at work. After a meeting, send a short email: “Just to confirm, we agreed that I’ll take Thursday off and Sam will cover the client call.” This is ordinary professional practice, and it quietly ends most workplace rewriting.

Talk to someone outside the situation. If you find yourself doubting your memory or judgment regularly with one particular person, describe what happened to a friend, a counselor, or someone else you trust. An outside view is often enough to restore your confidence.

Notice the pattern, not just the incident. One disagreement about the past is normal. A long series of them, always ending with you as the one who was wrong, is something else. If you see that pattern, it’s worth taking seriously. In close relationships, a steady erosion of your trust in your own memory is a recognized warning sign, and getting support from a counselor who understands controlling relationships can make a real difference.

This week’s exercise: the record

Open your journal.

Step one. Think of one person who often disputes your version of events. Write down two or three recent examples, including what they said and which tactic it was: flat denial, the minimizer, the blame shift, fog, the rewrite, or “everyone agrees.”

Step two. For each example, write the calm reply you could have used. Keep it to one or two sentences.

Step three. For the next week, after any significant conversation with this person, write down three things: the date, what was said, and what was agreed. Keep it brief and factual.

Step four. At the end of the week, read back through the notes. Does your written record match the way the conversations were described to you later? Most people find this exercise both calming and clarifying.

If someone sends you a message that leaves you questioning your own memory, paste it into the Decoder. Seeing the tactic named can help you step out of the fog.

Chapter 13

Withdrawal

Some of the most effective pressure involves no words at all.

A partner goes quiet for two days after you disagree. A friend stops replying to your messages after you decline an invitation. A parent answers the phone in a flat voice and says “I’m fine” in a way that makes it clear they aren’t. A manager who used to stop by your desk now walks past without looking up.

Nobody has made a demand. Nobody has said what’s wrong. And yet you can feel the pressure building, and you know exactly what would make it stop: give in, apologize, make it right. For many accommodating people, withdrawal is harder to resist than anger, because there’s nothing to push back against. Just silence, and the growing sense that you’ve done something terrible.

This chapter covers the main forms of withdrawal and how to respond to them without either chasing or retaliating.

Why withdrawal works

To understand why silence is so powerful, go back to the openings from Chapter 3. Withdrawal presses on several at once. It triggers fear of loss: maybe they’re pulling away for good. It triggers guilt: you must have done something wrong. It triggers the need for approval: the warmth you rely on has been switched off, and you want it back.

It also leaves you guessing. When someone tells you directly why they’re upset, you can respond to it. When they withdraw, you have to work out what’s wrong yourself. Many accommodating people fill that gap with the worst possible explanation and then work very hard to fix a problem that may not even exist.

The deliberate version is sometimes called the silent treatment, and it’s a well-recognized form of control in relationships. But plenty of people withdraw without much strategy. They’re hurt, they don’t know how to say so, and they go quiet because it’s the only way they learned to show it. The replies below work for both.

The silent treatment

What it looks like. Hours or days of not speaking, or speaking only in short, cold answers. Ignoring messages. Being physically present but emotionally absent. It often follows a disagreement, a boundary, or a no.

Where you’ll meet it. Most often with partners and family. Sometimes with friends, roommates, and even coworkers.

What it’s for. It makes the cost of displeasing them so uncomfortable that you’ll do almost anything to end it. Often the silence only breaks when you apologize, even if you weren’t the one who did something wrong. Over time, you learn to avoid anything that might trigger it.

The calm reply. Name it once, without accusation. Open a door. Then carry on with your life.

“I can tell you’re upset. When you’re ready to talk about it, I’m here.”

That’s all. You don’t need to say it again in an hour. You don’t need to follow them from room to room, send a stream of messages, or apologize for something you didn’t do.

If the silent treatment is a recurring pattern, raise it at a calm time, when the silence isn’t happening:

“When we disagree, you sometimes stop talking to me for a day or two. I’d rather we talk about what’s wrong, even if it’s hard. Going silent makes it impossible for me to fix anything.”

What not to do. Don’t chase. Every attempt to break through the silence by pleading, over-apologizing, or doing extra favors teaches the other person that silence gets results. Don’t retaliate with your own silence, either. That turns it into a contest of who can hold out longer.

“I’m fine”

What it looks like. You ask what’s wrong. They say “Nothing” or “I’m fine,” in a tone that makes it clear they are not fine, and that you should know why.

What it’s for. It puts the work of figuring out the problem on you. If you guess wrong, you’ve failed. If you don’t guess at all, you’re insensitive. Either way, you end up apologizing and trying harder.

The calm reply. Take them at their word, kindly.

“Okay. If that changes and you want to talk, let me know.”

Then continue as normal. Don’t keep asking, don’t guess, and don’t start apologizing for things they haven’t mentioned.

This can feel cold the first few times you do it. It isn’t. You’re treating the other person as an adult who is able to say what they need. If they want something from you, they’re capable of telling you.

Going cold

What it looks like. It’s less dramatic than full silence. The person is still talking to you, but the warmth is gone. Shorter messages. No “love you” at the end of the call. Fewer questions about your day. A colleague who used to chat now keeps everything strictly professional. A friend who used to share everything now gives one-word answers.

What it’s for. It applies pressure gently and deniably. If you ask about it, they can say nothing has changed. Meanwhile, you feel the loss of warmth and start wondering what you did.

The calm reply. Mention what you’ve noticed, without accusation, and ask directly.

“You seem a bit distant lately. Is something going on between us?”

If they say no, accept that for now. Then watch. If the coldness lifts when you comply with something and returns when you don’t, you’ve learned what it’s for.

What not to do. Don’t increase your own warmth to win theirs back. Extra compliments, favors, and attention in response to coldness teach the same lesson as chasing the silent treatment.

Withholding affection

What it looks like. In close relationships, affection is switched off as a consequence. No physical closeness, no kind words, no intimacy, until you do what they want or apologize for what they’re upset about. Sometimes it’s explicit: “I’m not in the mood to be nice to you right now.” More often it’s simply understood.

What it’s for. It ties affection to compliance. Over time, you learn that love is something you earn by keeping the other person happy, and lose by disagreeing with them.

The calm reply. Name the pattern at a calm moment.

“I’ve noticed that when we disagree, you pull away physically and emotionally until I apologize. I don’t want our closeness to depend on me always agreeing with you.”

If the pattern continues despite a calm conversation, it’s worth taking seriously. Affection used consistently as a reward and punishment is one of the clearer signs of a controlling relationship. Talking with a counselor, either alone or together, can help.

Ghosting and fading

What it looks like. A friend stops replying altogether. A date who seemed interested disappears. A relative stops inviting you to things. A colleague stops including you in meetings. There’s no conversation, no explanation, just absence.

What it’s for. Sometimes ghosting is simply avoidance. The person doesn’t want to have a difficult conversation, so they disappear. Sometimes it’s punishment. They want you to feel the loss and come back on their terms.

The calm reply. Send one clear, warm message. Then let them choose.

“I’ve noticed we haven’t been in touch lately. I value our friendship and I’d like to see you. If something’s bothering you, I’m open to talking about it.”

If there’s no answer, let it go. You don’t need to send a second, third, or fourth message. One clear door is enough.

At work, where absence affects your job, be more direct:

“I noticed I wasn’t included in the planning meetings this month. Can we talk about how I can stay involved?”

The slow reply

What it looks like. A milder version, common in families and friendships, and very common at work. The person who usually replies quickly now takes days. Your questions go unanswered while they reply promptly to everyone else.

What it’s for. Sometimes it’s nothing. People get busy. But when it starts right after a disagreement or a no, and ends when you give in, it’s a quiet form of withdrawal.

The calm reply. Don’t chase. Wait a reasonable time, then follow up once, factually.

“Just checking you saw my message about Saturday.”

If it’s work, set a clear deadline:

“I’ll need your answer by Thursday to keep the project on schedule.”

The deeper pattern

All forms of withdrawal share one feature. They make the relationship conditional. Warmth, contact, and affection are offered when you comply, and removed when you don’t.

Healthy relationships have quiet periods too. People need space after an argument. People get busy. People have bad weeks. The difference is in the pattern. In healthy relationships, space is temporary and eventually followed by talking. In controlling ones, silence is a tool that keeps returning, especially after you set a boundary, and only ends when you give way.

Your job isn’t to make the other person stop withdrawing. That’s not in your control. Your job is to stop letting withdrawal change your decisions. When silence stops working, many people stop using it. When it keeps happening regardless, you’ve learned something important about the relationship, and you can decide what to do with that information.

This week’s exercise: the silence log

Open your journal.

Step one. List the people who withdraw from you most often. Next to each name, write how they usually do it: silent treatment, “I’m fine,” going cold, withholding affection, ghosting, or slow replies.

Step two. For each person, write what usually ends the withdrawal. Do you apologize? Give in? Do something extra? Wait it out?

Step three. Write the calm reply you’ll use next time, from this chapter.

Step four. The next time someone withdraws, use the reply once, then carry on with your normal routine. Each day, write a short note about how you felt and what happened. Pay attention to how long the withdrawal lasts when you don’t chase it.

Many people find that withdrawal lasts much longer when they chase it and much shorter when they don’t. Seeing that on paper is often the moment the pattern loses its grip.

Field note Silence is only pressure if you treat it as a question you’re required to answer. Once you stop rushing to fill it, it goes back to being what it really is: someone else choosing not to talk.
Chapter 14

Charm and Pressure

Not every tactic feels bad while it’s happening. Some feel wonderful.

Someone praises your work right before asking you to take on a weekend project. A new partner showers you with attention, gifts, and plans for the future within weeks of meeting you. A friend tells you a secret, then asks for a favor. A salesperson tells you this price is only available today. A relative says “you’re the only one I can trust with this” before handing you a problem that isn’t yours.

In each case, the pressure arrives wrapped in something pleasant: admiration, closeness, excitement, a sense of being special. That’s what makes these tactics hard to spot. Most accommodating people are on guard against anger and guilt. Very few are on guard against being liked.

This chapter covers the tactics that work through warmth and speed. The replies all share one skill: separate the pleasant feeling from the decision, and slow the decision down.

Flattery before the ask

What it sounds like.

  • “You’re so good at this kind of thing. Would you mind…”
  • “Honestly, you’re the only one on the team I trust with this.”
  • “You always know exactly what to say. Could you call her for me?”
  • “You’re such a generous person. I knew I could ask you.”

Where you’ll meet it. At work, constantly, especially from managers and colleagues who want extra work done. In families, from relatives who need something. With friends who want a favor.

What it’s for. Praise lowers your guard and raises your desire to live up to it. If you’re “the only one I trust,” saying no feels like betraying that trust. If you’re “so generous,” saying no feels like proving you aren’t. The compliment and the request are tied together so you’ll accept both at once.

The calm reply. Accept the compliment, then answer the request on its own merits.

“Thank you, that’s kind of you to say. I can’t take it on this week.”

“I appreciate that. Let me check my schedule and get back to you.”

What not to do. Don’t let the compliment rush your answer. The praise may be completely sincere. It still doesn’t change how much time you have.

Love bombing

What it looks like. An intense flood of attention, affection, and promises early in a relationship. Constant messages. Lavish gifts. Declarations of deep connection within days or weeks. Talk of moving in, marriage, or a shared future very early. Making you feel that nobody has ever understood you the way they do.

Where you’ll meet it. Most often in romantic relationships, especially after a breakup or during a lonely period. A milder version shows up in friendships, in new jobs where a charismatic boss makes you feel like the star, and, as Chapter 18 explains, in groups that want to recruit you.

What it’s for. Healthy affection grows over time as people get to know each other. Love bombing skips that process. It creates a powerful emotional bond before you’ve had time to see the person clearly. Later, when the attention cools or turns into criticism or control, you’re already attached, and you work hard to get back to how it felt at the start.

Not everyone who falls hard and fast is love bombing. Some people are simply enthusiastic. The warning signs are speed combined with pressure: they push for commitment faster than you’re comfortable with, get upset when you want to slow down, or start to criticize or control you once you’re attached.

The calm reply. Slow it down, and see how they respond.

“I really like spending time with you. I’d like to take things a bit slower.”

“That’s a lovely idea, but I’m not ready to talk about moving in yet.”

Their reaction tells you a lot. Someone with genuine affection can handle “slower.” Someone love bombing often reacts with hurt, pressure, or anger.

What not to do. Don’t make major decisions, like moving in together, combining finances, or cutting back time with friends, in the first rush of intensity. Feelings that are real will still be there in six months.

Fake urgency

What it sounds like.

  • “I need an answer by end of day.”
  • “This offer is only good until midnight.”
  • “If you don’t decide now, someone else will take it.”
  • “I’m desperate. I need you to say yes right now.”
  • “There’s no time to think about it. Just trust me.”

Where you’ll meet it. Salespeople, obviously. But also managers who manufacture deadlines, family members whose emergencies always need you immediately, and partners who push for big decisions on the spot.

What it’s for. Urgency shuts down careful thinking. When you feel rushed, you rely on habit, and for accommodating people, habit is yes. Fake urgency also removes the buffer you practiced in Part Two, because there seems to be no time for “let me check.”

The calm reply. Slow it down anyway. Real urgency can usually survive an hour.

“I don’t make decisions like this on the spot. I’ll get back to you tomorrow.”

“If it has to be decided right now, then my answer is no.”

That second reply is worth remembering. It turns the pressure around. If they truly need an instant answer, they get one, just not the one they wanted. Often the deadline suddenly becomes more flexible.

What not to do. Don’t accept someone else’s deadline as your own without checking whether it’s real. Ask: what actually happens if I wait a day?

Borrowed intimacy

What it sounds like.

  • “I’ve never told anyone this before…” followed later by a request.
  • “This is just between us, okay?”
  • “You’re the only one who really understands me.”
  • Oversharing personal problems early in a relationship, then expecting the same closeness in return.

What it’s for. Shared secrets create a feeling of closeness and obligation. If someone has trusted you with something private, refusing them later feels like a betrayal. Some people use this deliberately, offering confidences to make you feel special and indebted. Others do it without much thought because it’s worked before.

The calm reply. You can hold a confidence without accepting the obligation that comes with it.

“Thank you for trusting me with that. I can’t help with this, though.”

If you’re asked to keep a secret that makes you uncomfortable, especially one that could hurt someone else:

“I’d rather you didn’t tell me things I’d have to keep from other people.”

What not to do. Don’t feel that being trusted means you owe someone a yes.

Future faking

What it sounds like.

  • “Once this project’s done, I’ll make sure you get the promotion.”
  • “Next year we’ll finally take that trip, I promise.”
  • “When things settle down, I’ll be able to help you more.”
  • “Just get me through this month, and I’ll pay you back with interest.”

Where you’ll meet it. Bosses who promise rewards that never arrive. Partners who promise change that never comes. Friends and relatives who promise repayment that’s always next month.

What it’s for. Promises about the future get you to accept unfair conditions now. You put up with the extra work, the unequal relationship, or the unpaid loan because something better is coming. The promise is renewed every time the deadline passes.

The calm reply. Ask for specifics, and get them in writing where possible.

“That would be great. Can we agree on a date and put it in writing?”

“I’m happy to help this month. What’s the specific plan for paying it back?”

And watch the track record. If promises have been made and broken before, the next one is probably no different.

“You’ve said that a few times now. I’d like to see it happen before I agree to more.”

What not to do. Don’t keep giving now in exchange for a future that keeps moving.

The foot in the door

What it sounds like. A small, easy request first. “Could you just look at this for five minutes?” Then a slightly bigger one. “While you’re at it, could you fix the formatting?” Then bigger. “Actually, could you rewrite the whole section? You’re so much better at this.”

What it’s for. Saying yes to a small request makes the next one harder to refuse. You’ve already started, you want to be consistent, and stopping feels rude. Chapter 15 covers the long version of this, where it plays out over months. This is the short version, inside a single conversation.

The calm reply. Notice when the request grows, and stop at the edge of what you agreed to.

“I said I’d take a quick look. I can’t take on the rewrite.”

“I’m happy to help with this part. The rest will have to be someone else.”

Why pleasant pressure works

All the tactics in this chapter use the same trick: they attach the request to something you want. Admiration, closeness, excitement, a future reward. Saying no feels like giving up the good thing, not just declining the task.

The defense is simple to describe, though it takes practice. Notice the good feeling, enjoy it if you like, and then ask a separate question: if this compliment or promise didn’t exist, would I still say yes? If the answer is yes, wonderful. If it’s no, the pleasant wrapping is doing the persuading, and you can decline the request while still keeping the compliment.

This week’s exercise: unwrapping the ask

Open your journal.

Step one. Think back over the past month. List three times someone paired a request with something pleasant: praise, a gift, a secret, a promise, excitement, or a sense of being special.

Step two. For each one, separate the wrapping from the request. Write: “The wrapping was . The request was .”

Step three. For each request, ask: without the wrapping, would I have said yes? Write your honest answer.

Step four. This week, whenever someone compliments you right before asking for something, use the reply from this chapter: thank them for the compliment, then buffer the request. Write down what happened.

If you receive a message that feels flattering but leaves you uneasy, paste it into the Decoder. It can show you whether the compliment and the request are doing more work together than they appear.

Chapter 15

The Slow Squeeze

The tactics in this chapter are the hardest to see, because no single moment looks like a tactic at all.

You agree to help a colleague with one report. Six months later, you’re doing all of her reports. You lend your brother a small amount of money. A year later, he owes you several thousand dollars and treats each new request as normal. You agree to host one family holiday. Now you host every holiday, and nobody remembers it was ever a choice. You take on a “temporary” responsibility at work while someone is on leave. They came back eight months ago. You still have it.

None of these began with anything unreasonable. Each step was small, and each step built on the last. That’s the slow squeeze. It doesn’t take much from you at once. It takes a little more, then a little more, until the new normal is something you’d never have agreed to if it had been proposed all at once.

The replies in this chapter share one idea. Go back to the original agreement, name the gap, and reset.

Moving goalposts

What it sounds like.

  • “This is great, but could you also…”
  • “Now that you’ve finished that, the real priority is…”
  • “That’s not quite what I meant. I was hoping for more like…”
  • “If you can hit this number, we’ll talk about the raise.” Then, once you hit it: “Let’s see if you can keep it up for another quarter.”

Where you’ll meet it. At work, constantly, with managers and clients. In families, with parents whose approval always depends on one more achievement. With partners who say they’ll be happy once you change one more thing.

What it’s for. Moving goalposts keep you working while the reward stays out of reach. Each time you meet the standard, a new one appears. You never get to the point where you’ve done enough.

The calm reply. Name the original agreement, and ask whether it’s changed.

“When we talked in March, the goal was ten new accounts, and I’ve brought in twelve. I’d like to talk about the raise we discussed.”

“I’ve done what we agreed. If the scope has changed, let’s talk about what that means for the timeline.”

What not to do. Don’t silently chase the new goal. Every time you do, you teach the other person that the goalposts can move without cost.

The ladder of small yeses

What it looks like. It starts with something tiny. Then each new request is only a little bigger than the last. A friend borrows twenty dollars, then fifty, then two hundred. A coworker asks you to cover one call, then a morning, then a week. A partner asks you to skip one evening with friends, then most evenings, then asks why you need friends at all.

What it’s for. Each yes makes the next one easier, because you want to be consistent and each step is only slightly bigger than the last. By the time the requests are large, you’ve already agreed to so many smaller ones that refusing feels like a sudden change in character.

The calm reply. Look at the whole ladder, not just the next rung. Then stop at a clear point.

“I’ve been happy to help with the last few loans. I’m not going to lend any more until the earlier ones are paid back.”

“I’ve covered several calls for you this month. I can’t keep doing that. Let’s figure out another plan.”

What not to do. Don’t judge each request in isolation. Ask yourself where this is heading, and whether you’d have agreed to the destination on day one.

Scope creep

What it looks like. A task or role that keeps expanding without any formal decision. You were hired to manage social media, and now you also handle customer service, event planning, and the office kitchen. You agreed to drive your mother to her Tuesday appointments, and now you manage her medications, her bills, and her entire calendar. You offered to set up a friend’s website, and now you’re his permanent technical support.

Where you’ll meet it. Work is the classic setting, but it’s just as common with aging parents, adult siblings, and friends.

What it’s for. Usually it isn’t deliberate. Someone notices you’re capable and willing, and hands you more. Nobody stops to ask whether this is fair, because you never say it isn’t.

The calm reply. List what you’re actually doing, and ask for a decision about it.

At work:

“My role has grown quite a bit. I’m now covering customer service and events as well as social media. I’d like to talk about either adjusting my responsibilities or adjusting my title and pay.”

In family:

“I’ve been handling more and more of Mom’s care. I’m happy to keep doing the appointments, but I need you to take over her bills and pharmacy.”

What not to do. Don’t wait until you’re exhausted and resentful to raise it. The longer scope creep goes unaddressed, the more permanent it feels.

Stolen credit

What it looks like. Your idea is repeated in a meeting by someone else, and they get the praise. Your work is presented by your manager as their own. A family member takes the thanks for a holiday you organized. A friend tells everyone how they “helped” with your project, when they barely took part.

What it’s for. Sometimes it’s simple carelessness. Often it’s opportunism: you’re unlikely to object, so your contributions become available for others to claim. Over time, others build reputations on your work while you stay invisible. Clause four of the contract from Chapter 2, “if you’re good enough, you’ll be treated fairly,” makes this especially easy, because you’re waiting to be noticed instead of making sure you are.

The calm reply. Reclaim the credit calmly, in the moment if you can, without accusing anyone.

“Thanks, Sam, I’m glad you picked up on the idea I raised earlier. I’d love to walk everyone through how I’d put it into practice.”

“Just to add some detail, since I put the analysis together: the key finding was…”

Afterward, protect your work by making it visible. Send a short email summarizing your contributions. Present your own work where possible. Keep a running list of your achievements for your next review.

What not to do. Don’t stay silent and seethe. The 3 a.m. replay was built for moments like this. A short, calm comment at the time is worth far more than a perfect speech you never deliver.

“Temporary” that never ends

What it sounds like.

  • “Just until I find a new place.”
  • “Only until we hire someone.”
  • “Just for a few weeks while things are crazy.”
  • “You can stop once I’m back on my feet.”

What it’s for. Temporary arrangements are easy to say yes to, because they have an end. The problem is that the end often never comes, and nobody ever formally extends the arrangement. It simply continues, and asking for it to stop starts to feel like breaking a promise.

The calm reply. Put a date on it at the start. If it’s already gone on too long, set one now.

At the start:

“I’m happy to help for a few weeks. Let’s say until the end of March, and then we’ll check in.”

Later:

“When you moved in, we said it would be a few weeks. It’s been five months. I’d like you to find your own place by the end of next month.”

What not to do. Don’t wait for the other person to notice that “temporary” has expired. They usually won’t.

The unreturned loan

What it looks like. Money, belongings, or favors lent and never returned. The tools borrowed last summer. The money “just until payday.” The favor you did that was supposed to be traded back. Each time you think about raising it, it feels awkward, so you don’t. And the person keeps asking for more.

What it’s for. Sometimes people genuinely forget. Sometimes they’ve learned that you won’t follow up, so the loan quietly becomes a gift. Either way, the pattern drains you while teaching the other person that your resources are always available.

The calm reply. Follow up specifically, without apology, as you practiced on day 18 of the reset.

“I wanted to check on the five hundred dollars from January. When will you be able to pay that back?”

“Can you bring back the ladder this weekend? I need it.”

If new requests come while old ones are outstanding:

“I’m not able to lend more until the earlier loan is sorted out.”

What not to do. Don’t keep lending in the hope that the other person will one day repay you without being asked.

Seeing the squeeze

Because the slow squeeze happens over months or years, it’s hard to see from inside a single day. Each request seems reasonable. The pattern only becomes visible when you step back and look at the whole picture.

A useful habit is a quarterly review. Every few months, look at your work, your family obligations, and your friendships, and ask three questions:

  1. What am I doing now that I wasn’t doing a year ago?
  2. Did I actually agree to it, or did it just accumulate?
  3. If someone proposed this whole arrangement to me today, would I say yes?

If the answer to the third question is no, you’ve found a squeeze. You can then go back to the original agreement, name the gap, and reset.

This week’s exercise: the creep audit

Open your journal.

Step one. Make three columns: work, family, and friends.

Step two. In each column, list the ongoing things you do for other people. Be thorough. Include the small, invisible tasks.

Step three. Next to each item, mark whether you originally agreed to it as it is now (A), whether it grew from something smaller (G), or whether it was meant to be temporary (T).

Step four. Choose one G or T item. Write down the original agreement, what it has become, and the calm reset you’ll propose. Then, this week, have that conversation.

When someone sends you a request that sounds small but feels like part of a larger pattern, paste it into the Decoder. It can help you see whether this is one favor or another rung on the ladder.

Field note Nobody ever agrees to the whole squeeze. They agree to the first small step, then the next one, and then one day they look up and wonder how they got here. The way out is the same way in: one clear step at a time, in the other direction.
Chapter 16

Intimidation

Most of the tactics in this part work quietly. Guilt pulls. Silence waits. Charm flatters. The slow squeeze takes its time.

Intimidation is different. It’s direct and physical. A raised voice. A slammed door. A tone that makes your stomach drop. A comment in front of colleagues that leaves you red-faced. A warning that if you don’t go along, you’ll regret it.

For accommodating people, this is often the hardest pressure to resist, because it presses directly on fear of conflict, the third clause of the contract from Chapter 2. When someone gets angry, the alarm goes off in your body before your mind has a chance to think. Your heart races. Your voice goes thin. And the fastest way to make the alarm stop is to give in.

This chapter covers the common forms of intimidation and how to respond without either collapsing or escalating. It also covers something more serious: how to tell when intimidation has crossed into danger, and what to do then.

A note before we begin. Everything in this chapter assumes you’re physically safe. If someone in your life has hurt you, threatened to hurt you, or makes you afraid for your safety, your priority isn’t a clever reply. It’s getting safe and getting support. There’s more on this at the end of the chapter.

The raised voice

What it sounds like. Shouting, or a voice that rises sharply when you disagree. Sometimes it’s not volume but intensity: speaking fast, interrupting, leaning in, cutting you off before you finish.

Where you’ll meet it. Bosses under pressure, partners and parents in arguments, customers, and anyone who has learned that getting loud gets results.

What it’s for. Anger creates an immediate sense of danger. Most people will back down just to make it stop. For someone who has learned this works, raising their voice is a shortcut that skips the work of persuading you.

The calm reply. Lower your own voice, slow down, and pause the conversation if it doesn’t calm.

“I want to hear what you’re saying. I can’t do that while you’re shouting.”

“Let’s take a break and come back to this when we’re both calmer.”

Then, if it continues, actually leave. Walk out of the room, end the call, or close the meeting.

“I’m going to step out. We can pick this up tomorrow.”

What not to do. Don’t match their volume. Don’t give in just to end it. And don’t stay and absorb it because leaving feels rude. Leaving a shouting match is not rude. It’s sensible.

Public pressure

What it looks like. Being put on the spot in front of others. A manager criticizes you in a team meeting. A relative brings up something private at a family dinner. A friend asks for a favor in front of the group so that saying no would look petty. A partner mocks you in front of friends.

What it’s for. An audience raises the stakes. Saying no in private is hard enough. Saying no in front of others feels like a public conflict. Many people agree to things in public that they’d easily refuse in private.

The calm reply. Decline to have the conversation in public. Move it somewhere private.

“I’d rather talk about that one-on-one. Can we find a time after the meeting?”

“Let’s talk about this later, just the two of us.”

“I’ll get back to you about it tomorrow.”

At a family gathering, when someone brings up a private topic:

“I’m not going to discuss that here.”

Then change the subject, or step away for a moment.

What not to do. Don’t let the audience rush you into a yes. You’re allowed to say “I’ll think about it” in front of anyone.

The implied threat

What it sounds like.

  • “I’d hate for this to affect your review.”
  • “You don’t want to make an enemy of me.”
  • “Think carefully about how this will look.”
  • “I’m just saying, people remember who was a team player.”
  • “If you leave, don’t expect me to be here when you come back.”

What it’s for. The implied threat warns of consequences without stating them. It’s designed to make you fearful while giving the other person deniability. If you object, they can say they were “just being honest” or that you misunderstood.

The calm reply. Ask them to make it explicit. Threats often lose their force when someone has to say them plainly.

“I want to make sure I understand. Are you saying my review will be affected if I don’t do this?”

“What exactly do you mean by that?”

At work, follow up in writing:

“Just to confirm our conversation today, you mentioned my review could be affected if I didn’t take on the weekend project. I want to make sure I understood correctly.”

That kind of note often makes people rethink their words. It also creates a record if you need one later.

What not to do. Don’t comply out of vague fear. Find out what’s actually being threatened, then decide.

The ultimatum

What it sounds like.

  • “Either you do this, or we’re done.”
  • “If you go to that event, don’t bother coming home.”
  • “Agree now, or the deal’s off.”
  • “It’s me or them.”

What it’s for. An ultimatum shrinks your options to two, both chosen by the other person. It tries to force a decision on their terms and their schedule.

The calm reply. Slow down, refuse the false choice, and take time if you need it.

“I’m not going to make a decision like that under pressure. I need some time to think.”

“I don’t accept that those are the only two options.”

Sometimes an ultimatum is a genuine boundary. “If you keep drinking, I’ll leave” can be a fair statement of someone’s limits. The difference is whether it’s about protecting themselves or controlling you. An ultimatum used repeatedly to win ordinary disagreements is a control tactic.

What not to do. Don’t decide on the spot because you’re frightened. Real ultimatums can wait a day. Controlling ones often fade when they don’t get an immediate result.

Looming and physical intimidation

What it looks like. Standing over you. Blocking a doorway. Getting very close when angry. Slamming doors, throwing objects, punching walls. Driving aggressively with you in the car. Nothing directed at you, perhaps, but a clear message about what could happen.

What it’s for. It communicates danger without crossing the line into direct violence. It’s meant to make you afraid enough to comply.

The calm reply. This is no longer a situation for clever words. Your priority is safety.

“I’m leaving now.”

Then leave, if you can do so safely. Go to another room with a door that locks, go outside, go to a neighbor or friend.

What not to do. Don’t treat physical intimidation as ordinary conflict. Breaking objects, blocking exits, and punching walls are warning signs. Take them seriously, even if the person has never touched you.

Staying steady

Intimidation works by triggering a fear response, and fear makes clear thinking hard. A few things help you stay steady enough to use the replies above.

Use the calm-down breath. The slow, long exhale from Chapter 7 is especially useful here. Two or three breaths can be enough to keep your voice from shaking.

Lower and slow your voice. When someone else is loud and fast, speaking quietly and slowly helps you stay calm. It often calms them too.

Have a fixed exit line. Decide in advance what you’ll say when you need to leave: “I’m going to step out, and we’ll talk later.” Having it ready means you don’t have to think of it under pressure.

Remember that you can come back to it. You don’t have to resolve anything while the other person is angry. You can say “I’ll think about it,” leave, and return to the conversation later when you’re calmer.

When intimidation becomes danger

Most of this book is about ordinary relationships with ordinary difficulties. This section is about the ones that aren’t.

Intimidation has crossed into danger if any of these are true:

  • Someone has hit, pushed, grabbed, choked, or otherwise physically hurt you.
  • Someone has threatened to hurt you, your children, your pets, or themselves if you leave or disagree.
  • Someone controls your money, your phone, your movements, or who you can see.
  • You find yourself planning your day around avoiding someone’s anger.
  • You feel afraid of someone you live with.

If any of these describe your situation, the replies in this chapter are not enough, and you shouldn’t try to use them alone. Contact someone who can help: a trusted friend or family member, a counselor, a doctor, or a domestic violence service. In the United States, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is available at any hour by phone (1-800-799-7233) or text (text START to 88788). If you are outside the US, local services exist in most countries. If you are in immediate danger, call emergency services.

Getting help is not a failure of the skills in this book. It’s the most important skill of all: recognizing when a situation needs more than you can handle alone.

This week’s exercise: your steady plan

Open your journal.

Step one. List the people who use intimidation on you, and how: raised voice, public pressure, implied threats, ultimatums, or physical intimidation.

Step two. Look at the list honestly. Do any of the danger signs above apply? If so, put this book down for a moment and think about who you could contact for support. Write down one name or number.

Step three. For the ordinary cases, write your exit line, the sentence you’ll use to pause or leave a conversation that’s getting heated. Practice it out loud until it feels natural.

Step four. The next time someone raises their voice or puts you on the spot, use the calm-down breath, then your exit line. Afterward, write down what happened.

If a message feels threatening but you’re not sure, paste it into the Decoder. It can help you see whether you’re dealing with ordinary frustration or something more serious. If you feel afraid, trust that feeling over any tool, including this one.

Field note Calm is not the same as weak. The person who stays quiet while someone shouts is often the most powerful person in the room. They just don’t need to prove it.
Chapter 17

Manipulation at Scale

So far, this part of the book has focused on people you know: partners, parents, bosses, coworkers, friends. But some of the most skilled manipulation you’ll ever face comes from people you’ve never met.

A salesperson who has been trained to close. A scammer working from a script that has been tested on thousands of people. An online store designed by teams of specialists to get you to spend more than you planned. A news feed built to keep you angry, because anger keeps you scrolling.

These aren’t personal. Nobody is targeting you because of who you are. But they use exactly the same openings you learned about in Chapter 3: guilt, the need for approval, fear of conflict, the need to be seen as good, loneliness, and urgency. And accommodating people are often especially vulnerable, because the habits that make them easy to work on in personal relationships make them easy to work on commercially, too.

The good news is that the defenses are largely the same ones you’ve already practiced. Slow down. Separate the feeling from the decision. Don’t answer on someone else’s schedule.

High-pressure sales

What it sounds like.

  • “This price is only available today.”
  • “I can’t promise it’ll still be here tomorrow.”
  • “Let me see what I can do with my manager.” Then a long wait, and a “special” deal.
  • “Most people in your situation go with the premium package.”
  • “I’ve already put a lot of time into this for you.”

Where you’ll meet it. Car dealerships, timeshare presentations, home improvement sales, gyms, phone plans, extended warranties, and anywhere someone earns a commission.

What it’s for. Sales pressure combines several tactics you’ve already seen: fake urgency (Chapter 14), the hidden-price favor (Chapter 11), and the foot in the door (Chapter 14). The salesperson gives you their time, a free drink, or a “special” discount, so you feel you owe them. They create urgency so you won’t go home and think. They make small agreements along the way so the final yes feels natural.

The calm reply. Take the decision home.

“I don’t buy anything on the same day. I’ll think about it and get back to you.”

“If the price is only good today, I’ll pass.”

“I appreciate your time. I’m not ready to decide.”

You don’t owe a salesperson a purchase because they were friendly, or because they spent an hour with you. Their time is part of their job.

What not to do. Don’t let politeness push you into a purchase. You’re allowed to walk out of any sales conversation at any point, without explaining.

Scams

Scams are high-pressure sales with the honesty removed. They are a large and growing problem, and they target people of every age and background. Scammers are often skilled, well-organized, and use scripts refined over thousands of calls.

The specific stories change constantly. The underlying patterns don’t. Here are the most common ones.

The impersonator. Someone claims to be from your bank, a government agency, the police, a delivery company, or a well-known tech company. There’s a problem: suspicious activity on your account, an unpaid tax, a warrant, a package that can’t be delivered, a virus on your computer. You need to act immediately.

The family emergency. A call or message from someone claiming to be a grandchild, child, or other relative in trouble. They’ve been arrested, hurt, or stranded, and need money urgently. Sometimes a second person comes on claiming to be a lawyer or police officer. Increasingly, scammers use AI tools to imitate the voice of someone you love.

The romance scam. Someone you meet online, often on a dating site or social media, builds a warm, intense relationship over weeks or months. They can never quite meet in person. Eventually there’s a crisis, a medical bill, a business problem, a travel issue, and they need money. This is love bombing (Chapter 14) used for theft.

The investment opportunity. A friend of a friend, or someone you met online, has a guaranteed way to make money, often with cryptocurrency. Early “returns” may even appear on a website. Then you’re asked to invest more.

The prize. You’ve won something, but you need to pay a fee or tax to claim it.

All of these share the same features. Urgency. Secrecy (“don’t tell anyone, this is confidential”). Emotional pressure, either fear or love. And unusual payment methods.

The rules that stop almost every scam.

  1. Hang up and call back on a number you find yourself. If someone says they’re from your bank, end the call and phone the number on the back of your card. If a relative says they’re in trouble, call them directly on the number you already have, or call another family member.
  2. Never pay with gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency, or payment apps to someone who contacted you. Legitimate organizations, including government agencies, do not demand payment in gift cards or cryptocurrency.
  3. Never share codes sent to your phone. A real bank will never call to ask for the code it just sent you.
  4. Take secrecy as a warning sign. “Don’t tell anyone” is almost always a red flag. Tell someone.
  5. Slow down. Scams depend on urgency. A real emergency can survive a ten-minute delay while you check.

Many families find it helpful to agree on a private code word that a real relative in trouble would know, and an AI voice clone would not.

The calm reply.

“I’m going to hang up and call the bank directly.”

“I don’t make payments over the phone to people who call me.”

“I’ll call you back once I’ve checked.”

Then do it. A real organization or a real relative will understand. A scammer will push harder, which tells you everything you need to know.

What not to do. Don’t feel embarrassed if you’ve been targeted, or even caught. Scammers are professionals. Report it to your bank and to the authorities as quickly as possible. In the US, you can report scams to the Federal Trade Commission at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

Advertising and online stores

Advertising isn’t fraud, and most of it is harmless. But modern online stores and apps are often built by teams who study exactly how to get you to buy more, sign up for more, and cancel less. Knowing the common techniques helps you notice them.

Fake scarcity. “Only 2 left in stock!” “12 people are looking at this right now.” Sometimes true, often not. Designed to make you buy before you think.

Countdown timers. “Sale ends in 14:59.” Often the timer resets when you reload the page.

Anchoring. A crossed-out “original” price that makes the current price look like a bargain, even if the item rarely sold at the higher price.

Free trials that become subscriptions. Easy to start, deliberately hard to cancel, often requiring a phone call or a long series of screens.

Guilt buttons. “No thanks, I don’t want to save money.” Wording designed to make declining feel foolish.

Social proof. “Bestseller.” “Join 50,000 happy customers.” “Your friend liked this.” Useful information sometimes, but designed to make you feel that everyone else has already decided.

The calm reply. The 24-hour rule. For any non-essential purchase that feels urgent, wait a day. Put it in your basket, close the page, and come back tomorrow. If you still want it, buy it. Many urges to buy vanish overnight.

For subscriptions, set a reminder to cancel before the trial ends the moment you sign up.

Online outrage

The last form of manipulation at scale is the hardest to see, because it doesn’t ask for your money. It asks for your attention.

Social media feeds, many news sites, and video platforms tend to show you whatever keeps you engaged. And research has repeatedly found that content provoking strong emotions, especially anger and moral outrage, tends to spread more widely than calm content. You don’t need to believe there’s a conspiracy to see the effect. Platforms reward whatever keeps people scrolling, and outrage keeps people scrolling.

What it looks like. Headlines designed to make you furious. Posts that frame every issue as a battle between good people (your side) and bad people (the other side). Videos that show the worst behavior of a group and present it as typical. A steady feeling that the world is getting worse and the other side is to blame.

What it’s for. Your attention, which is sold to advertisers. A side effect is that it can damage your relationships, especially with family and friends who see the world differently. Chapter 18 looks at how groups use the same “us versus them” pressure.

The calm reply. Notice the feeling before you react.

  • When something online makes you angry, pause before you share or comment. Ask: who benefits if I’m angry right now?
  • Check whether a story is true before passing it on, especially if it confirms what you already believe.
  • Limit how much time you spend in feeds designed to provoke you. Choose your news sources deliberately.
  • Remember that the people you see online are rarely typical of the groups they appear to represent.

What not to do. Don’t let a feed decide how you feel about your neighbors, coworkers, or relatives.

The common thread

High-pressure sales, scams, manipulative online design, and outrage feeds all depend on the same thing: getting you to act before you think. They create urgency, attach strong feelings to the decision, and discourage you from checking with anyone else.

Your defenses are the ones you’ve already practiced. The buffer from Part Two: “let me check and get back to you.” The separation of feeling from decision from Chapter 14. The refusal to answer on someone else’s schedule. Used consistently, they protect you from most commercial manipulation just as well as they protect you from personal pressure.

This week’s exercise: the outside-pressure audit

Open your journal.

Step one. List any subscriptions, memberships, or recurring charges you’re paying for. Mark any you didn’t fully mean to keep. Cancel at least one this week.

Step two. Write down the scam rules from this chapter on a card or a note in your phone. Share them with one family member, especially an older relative or a teenager.

Step three. If you live with family, agree on a code word that a real relative in trouble would use.

Step four. For one week, notice every time something online makes you angry. Write down what it was and where you saw it. At the end of the week, look at the list. Who benefited from your anger?

If you receive a message or call that feels urgent and slightly off, paste the message, or a description of the call, into the Decoder before you act. Many scams become obvious once the tactics are named.

Field note Strangers who want something from you have one weakness in common: they can’t afford to wait. Whenever you’re being rushed by someone you don’t know, slowing down is almost always the right answer.
Chapter 18

How Groups Pull You In

Most of this book is about one person pressuring another. This chapter is about something larger: how groups recruit people, keep them loyal, and make leaving feel impossible.

It’s a sensitive subject, because groups are also some of the best things in human life. Churches, clubs, teams, political movements, online communities, workplaces, and families give people meaning, friendship, support, and a sense of belonging. Most groups are healthy. People join, get something valuable, and are free to leave.

But some groups use the same tactics this book has been describing, at a larger scale and with more force. They recruit with warmth, bind people with guilt and secrecy, and punish doubt with withdrawal. The most extreme examples are what people call cults. Milder versions show up in some churches and spiritual groups, in some multi-level marketing companies, in some political and activist communities on every side, in some online movements, and even in some workplaces and families.

This chapter stays neutral on beliefs. It doesn’t matter what a group believes. What matters is how it treats its members. A group can hold views you share completely and still be controlling. A group can hold views you find strange and still be perfectly healthy. The question is never “is this group right?” The question is “can members think for themselves and leave freely?”

The pattern

Researchers and former members of high-control groups have described a remarkably consistent pattern across very different groups. The details vary. The structure repeats.

Warm welcome

What it looks like. A newcomer is greeted with extraordinary warmth. People remember your name, invite you to everything, praise you, and make you feel that you’ve finally found where you belong. You may be told you’re special, that you were meant to find this group.

What it’s for. This is love bombing (Chapter 14) done by a group. It creates a powerful emotional bond before you’ve had time to look closely. For someone who is lonely, grieving, newly moved, recently divorced, or going through a hard time, it can feel like rescue.

What to notice. Warmth isn’t a warning sign by itself. Many healthy groups are welcoming. The warning sign is intensity combined with speed and pressure: being pushed to commit quickly, attend constantly, or give significant money or time before you really understand the group.

Us and them

What it looks like. The world is divided into the group, which is good, wise, or awake, and everyone else, who is ignorant, dangerous, or lost. Outsiders, including your old friends and family, are described as people who “don’t understand,” who are “holding you back,” or who are on the wrong side.

What it’s for. It makes the group the only safe place, and makes outside voices easy to dismiss. If your sister raises concerns, she’s not a person who loves you. She’s one of “them.”

What to notice. Every group has some sense of identity. The warning sign is when outsiders are consistently treated as enemies, and when contact with them is discouraged.

Special knowledge

What it looks like. The group has a truth that others don’t. Members understand how the world really works, what’s really going on, or what really matters. Often there are levels: deeper truths revealed only to those who have proved their commitment.

What it’s for. It makes members feel special and makes leaving feel like losing access to the truth. It also makes outside information easy to dismiss, because outsiders by definition don’t understand.

Gradual commitment

What it looks like. The ladder of small yeses from Chapter 15. First a free event. Then a weekend retreat. Then a course. Then volunteering. Then donating. Then moving, changing jobs, or cutting ties. Each step is only slightly bigger than the last.

What it’s for. By the time the demands are large, you’ve already invested so much time, money, and identity that walking away feels like admitting it was all a mistake.

No questions

What it looks like. Doubts are treated as weakness, disloyalty, or a sign of spiritual or political failure. People who ask hard questions are corrected, shamed, or quietly sidelined. The leader or the core beliefs can’t be criticized.

What it’s for. It turns your own doubts into evidence that something is wrong with you, which is the voice in your head from Chapter 4, installed by a group.

Loaded language

What it looks like. A special vocabulary that only members fully understand. Words that sum up complex ideas in a single phrase, often ones that end a conversation. Outsiders might be “sleepers,” “worldly,” “sheep,” or some other label. Doubts might be called “resistance” or “negativity.”

What it’s for. Shared language creates belonging. It can also make it hard to think about the group in ordinary terms. Once a doubt has a dismissive label, you stop examining it.

The cost of leaving

What it looks like. Leaving means losing everything: friends, community, sometimes family, sometimes income. Former members may be shunned, criticized, or described as traitors. Members are warned that leaving will bring disaster.

What it’s for. It makes staying the only option, even for people who no longer believe.

Where you’ll see it

Because this pattern is about behavior, not belief, you can find it in very different places.

Religious and spiritual groups. Most religious communities are not controlling. Some are. The difference shows in how they treat doubt, outsiders, money, and people who leave.

Multi-level marketing. Some MLM companies recruit with warmth and big promises, encourage members to see doubters as negative people who don’t believe in them, and push ever-larger purchases. People often lose money and friendships before they leave.

Political and activist movements, on every side. Political commitment is healthy. But any movement, left, right, or anywhere else, can slide into “us versus them” thinking, special knowledge only members understand, and harsh treatment of people who question the party line. If you can only see this pattern on the other side, it’s worth looking more carefully at your own.

Online communities. Forums, groups, and channels built around a shared interest or belief can develop the same features: a special vocabulary, scorn for outsiders, punishment for dissent.

Workplaces. Some companies describe themselves as a family, demand total devotion, treat leaving as betrayal, and discourage employees from questioning leadership.

Families. Some families operate like small closed groups, with strict loyalty rules, secrets that can’t be shared with outsiders, and harsh punishment for anyone who steps out of line.

A simple checklist

When you’re trying to judge a group you belong to, or one a loved one has joined, these questions help. A healthy group will usually let you answer yes to most of them.

  1. Can members openly question the leaders or core ideas without being punished or shamed?
  2. Are members encouraged to keep close relationships with people outside the group?
  3. Is information from outside the group welcome, or at least tolerated?
  4. Are money and time requests reasonable and transparent?
  5. Can members leave without losing all their friends or being described as traitors?
  6. Do the leaders follow the same rules they set for everyone else?
  7. Does the group admit when it has been wrong?

If several answers are no, the group may be more controlling than it appears from inside.

If you’re stepping back yourself

Recognizing that a group you belong to has too much pull over you is often painful. You may have real friends there, real memories, and real beliefs you still hold. You don’t have to decide everything at once.

A few steps that help:

  • Reconnect with people outside the group. Call an old friend. Spend time with family. Notice how it feels to be with people who don’t share the group’s language.
  • Give yourself permission to doubt. Write your questions down in your journal. Doubts you can see on paper are easier to think about calmly.
  • Slow your commitment. Attend less often. Give less money. Take a break. Notice the group’s reaction.
  • Watch how they respond. A healthy group respects your choices. A controlling one tends to increase pressure, warmth, or guilt when you pull back.
  • Find support. Former members of many groups have written books and formed support communities. A counselor with experience in high-control groups can be very helpful.

If someone you love is in a group like this

This is one of the hardest situations people face. The natural instinct is to argue, to prove that the group is wrong. That rarely works. Attacking the group usually confirms what the group has told them: outsiders don’t understand and are hostile.

What helps more:

  • Keep the relationship. Stay in touch, stay warm, and avoid ultimatums. You want to be the person they can come back to.
  • Ask, don’t tell. Gentle, curious questions work better than arguments. “What do you like most about it?” “How do they handle it when people disagree?” “What would happen if you wanted to take a break?”
  • Talk about the shared past. Remind them who they were and what you’ve shared, without criticizing who they are now.
  • Don’t argue about beliefs. Focus on their wellbeing, their freedom, and your relationship.
  • Get support for yourself. Watching someone you love in a controlling group is exhausting. Talk to people who understand.

Why this matters for everyday life

You may never encounter a cult. But the pattern in this chapter shows up in smaller ways all the time. The family dinner that turns into a political argument, where each side sees the other as “them.” The friend who has joined something and now treats your questions as attacks. The workplace that makes you feel disloyal for having a life outside it.

The same skills you’ve practiced throughout this book apply. Slow down. Separate the feeling from the decision. Notice when warmth comes with conditions. Keep your own judgment, even when everyone around you seems sure.

This week’s exercise: your groups

Open your journal.

Step one. List the groups that matter most in your life: family, workplace, religious or spiritual community, political or activist groups, clubs, online communities.

Step two. For each group, run through the seven-question checklist. Note any answers that are no.

Step three. Choose one group where you’d like to feel a little freer. Write one small step you could take this month: reconnecting with an outside friend, questioning something you’ve accepted, or giving a bit less time or money.

Step four. If someone you love is in a group that worries you, write down three curious questions you could ask them, and one shared memory you could bring up. Then plan a time to reach out.

Field note The strongest groups don’t need to hold anyone. People stay because they want to, and leave without being punished. Any group, of any belief, that makes leaving feel impossible has already told you most of what you need to know.

Part Four

Quiet Power

For three parts, this book has been about defense. Seeing your own patterns, practicing new responses, and recognizing the tactics people use to push on you.

Defense matters. It’s also not the whole story. Most people who finish the reset don’t just want to stop being used. They want to be heard. They want to ask for the raise and get it, settle a family disagreement without a fight, and walk into a meeting knowing their ideas will land.

That’s quiet power. It isn’t the loud, dominating kind you see in films. It’s the ability to influence what happens around you calmly, honestly, and without becoming someone you don’t respect. The people who have it rarely raise their voices. They don’t need to.

This part comes last for a reason. Influence in the hands of someone who still can’t say no turns into people-pleasing with better technique. Now that you’ve done the earlier work, these skills will serve you instead of serving everyone else.

Chapter 19

What People Really Want

Every request, complaint, and argument has two layers. There’s what the person says, and there’s what they actually need. Most conversations go wrong because people respond to the first layer and ignore the second.

Your manager says, “I need this report by Friday.” What she may actually need is to look prepared in front of her own boss on Monday. Your father says, “You never visit.” What he may need is reassurance that he still matters to you. Your partner says, “You always leave the dishes.” What they may need is to feel that the household is a shared effort, not their job alone.

If you only answer the words, you’ll keep having the same conversation. If you answer the need, things often resolve quickly, sometimes in ways nobody expected.

This chapter is about reading the needs underneath what people say. It’s the foundation of everything else in Part Four. Once you can see what someone really wants, you can be heard by them, negotiate with them, and sometimes give them what they need without giving up what you need.

The six core needs

People want a huge variety of specific things. But underneath, most requests and conflicts trace back to a small number of core needs. Here are six that come up again and again.

Respect. To be taken seriously, valued, and treated as competent. People who feel disrespected often become defensive, combative, or stubborn, even over small things.

Safety. To feel secure: financially, physically, emotionally. People who feel unsafe tend to want control, certainty, and reassurance. They may resist change even when it would help them.

Belonging. To feel included, connected, and wanted. People who fear being left out may become clingy, jealous, or desperate for approval. Or they may withdraw before anyone can reject them.

Control. To have a say in what happens to them. People who feel powerless often push back hard, even against good ideas, simply because they weren’t consulted.

Fairness. To feel that effort and reward are balanced, and that rules apply equally. People who feel cheated may hold grudges for years over amounts that seem tiny to everyone else.

Being seen. To have their effort, pain, or contribution noticed. People who feel invisible often keep repeating themselves, getting louder, until someone acknowledges what they’ve been saying.

Most people have one or two of these that matter most to them. Learning someone’s main need is like learning which language they speak. Once you know it, you can communicate much more effectively.

Reading the need

You can usually spot someone’s core need by paying attention to three things.

What they complain about

Complaints are a direct line to needs. Listen to what people complain about most often.

  • “Nobody ever asks my opinion.” That’s control.
  • “I do everything around here.” That’s fairness, and being seen.
  • “They treat me like I don’t know what I’m doing.” That’s respect.
  • “I never know what’s going on.” That’s safety.
  • “Everyone else was invited.” That’s belonging.

What upsets them out of proportion

When someone reacts far more strongly than a situation seems to call for, a core need has usually been touched. A colleague who becomes furious about a small change to a process they designed may be reacting to a loss of control or respect. A relative who is deeply hurt that you didn’t call on their birthday may be reacting to a fear of not belonging.

What calms them

Notice what settles people down. Some calm down when you explain the plan in detail (safety). Some when you ask their opinion (control). Some when you thank them specifically (being seen). Some when you include them (belonging). Whatever calms them is a clue to what they need.

Asking for the need

You don’t always have to guess. Often you can simply ask, using questions that invite people to tell you what matters to them. Chapter 21 covers this in more detail, but here are a few useful questions to start with.

“What’s most important to you about this?”

“What would a good outcome look like for you?”

“What worries you most?”

“What would make this feel fair?”

These questions do two things. They give you information. And they meet a need in themselves: being asked what matters to you is a form of respect, control, and being seen, all at once. Many difficult conversations soften as soon as you ask one of them.

Using what you learn

Once you understand what someone needs, you have more options than simply giving in or refusing.

Meet the need without meeting the request

Often you can satisfy the underlying need while still saying no to the specific request.

Your manager wants you to work the weekend. Her need is probably safety: she’s worried about a deadline. You could say: “I can’t work Saturday, but I can get the first draft to you by Thursday night, so you’ll have time to review it before Monday.” The request is declined. The need is met.

Your mother wants you to come every Sunday. Her need is probably belonging. You could say: “I can’t come every week, but I’d love to call you every Sunday evening, and visit the first weekend of each month.” Again, the need is met in a way that works for you.

Frame your request around their need

When you want something from someone, connect it to what they care about.

If your boss values safety, frame your raise request around reducing risk: “I want to make sure I can keep giving the team the stability it needs. I’d like to talk about my pay so that I’m not tempted to look elsewhere.” If your partner values fairness, frame a request about chores around balance: “I want us both to feel like we’re carrying the same load. Can we look at who does what?”

You aren’t tricking anyone. You’re simply explaining your request in terms the other person cares about.

Recognize when needs can’t be met

Sometimes, understanding someone’s need shows you that you can’t meet it, at least not without giving up too much of yourself. A partner whose need for control means they want to approve every decision you make. A friend whose need to be seen means they expect hours of your attention every day. A parent whose need for belonging means they want you to live nearby forever.

In those cases, understanding the need still helps. It lets you respond with compassion rather than resentment. “I understand you want to feel involved. I’m still going to make this decision myself.” You can honor the need without surrendering to it.

Your own needs

There’s one more person whose needs this chapter applies to: you.

Accommodating people are often very skilled at reading other people’s needs and almost blind to their own. Clause two of the Good Person Contract taught you that your needs are a burden. So you may have spent years meeting everyone else’s need for safety, belonging, and being seen, while your own went unnoticed.

Go back through the six core needs and ask which ones matter most to you. When you feel resentful, which need isn’t being met? When you feel content, which need is being satisfied?

Knowing your own core needs makes everything else in Part Four easier. You’ll know what to ask for. You’ll know which compromises you can live with and which you can’t. And you’ll stop being surprised by your own resentment, because you’ll understand where it’s coming from.

How You Come Across

People make up their minds about you quickly, often before you’ve said anything that matters. They notice how you walk into a room, how you sit, how your voice sounds, how you handle a pause, and whether you seem comfortable or anxious. From those signals, they decide how seriously to take you.

That might sound unfair, and in some ways it is. But it also means that small changes in how you come across can make a large difference in how you’re treated. You don’t need a new personality. You need to stop sending signals that undercut what you say.

This chapter is about presence: the quiet, steady way of carrying yourself that makes people listen. It isn’t about faking confidence or acting tough. It’s about removing the habits that make you look smaller than you are.

The signals that undercut you

Accommodating people often have habits in how they speak and carry themselves that send a message of uncertainty, even when they know exactly what they’re talking about. Here are the most common.

Rising endings. Ending statements as if they were questions. “I think we should go with option two?” It signals that you’re unsure, or seeking permission.

Qualifiers. “I just think…” “This might be wrong, but…” “Sort of.” “Kind of.” “Maybe.” Each one shaves a little off the weight of what you say.

Speed. Talking fast, especially when you’re nervous, as if you need to get it all out before someone interrupts. It signals that you don’t expect to be given the time.

Filling. “Um.” “Like.” “You know.” Nervous laughter at the end of sentences. These fill space that a pause would do more with.

Shrinking. Hunching, crossing your arms tightly, taking up as little space as possible, sitting at the edge of the room or the back of the table.

Fidgeting. Touching your face, playing with your hair or a pen, shifting your weight. Movement that signals discomfort.

Avoiding eye contact. Looking down or away when you make a point or when someone disagrees with you.

None of these make you a lesser person. Many of them are habits you developed to seem less threatening, which is exactly what the Good Person Contract asked of you. But now they’re working against you.

Your voice

Your voice carries more information than your words. Small changes make a big difference.

Let your statements land. End sentences with your voice going down, not up. “I think we should go with option two.” Say it like a statement, because it is one. If you’re not sure you do this, record yourself reading a few sentences and listen back.

Slow down. Speak about twenty percent slower than feels natural, especially when you’re making an important point. Slower speech sounds more considered and more confident. It also gives you time to think, which helps you avoid nervous filler.

Lower your volume, slightly. Many people get louder when they want to be heard. A calm, slightly lower voice often draws more attention, because people lean in to listen.

Cut the qualifiers. Replace “I just think we might want to consider maybe…” with “I recommend…” Replace “This is probably a dumb question, but…” with “Quick question:” You can still be polite. You just don’t need to apologize for your own ideas.

Use the pause. Stop after you make an important point. Let it sit for a moment before you continue or before you invite a response. A pause signals that you believe what you just said is worth thinking about.

Your body

You don’t need to stand like a superhero or practice dramatic poses. You just need to stop shrinking.

Take your space. Sit fully in your chair, not on the edge. Rest your arms on the table or the armrests rather than hugging yourself. Stand with your weight even on both feet.

Be still. Reduce fidgeting. Let your hands rest when you’re not using them. Stillness reads as calm, and calm reads as confidence.

Look at people. Make comfortable eye contact when you speak and when you listen. You don’t need to stare. Just look at the person, especially at the moment you make your point and when they respond.

Choose your seat. In meetings, sit at the table rather than along the wall, and somewhere you can be seen. Small choices like this affect how much you’re included.

The first thirty seconds

The beginning of any conversation sets its tone. A few of the tactics from the original Shadow Persuasion system are especially useful here.

The 3-Second Read. Before you start talking, take a moment to notice the other person’s state. Are they rushed, tired, irritated, relaxed? Adjust your opening to match. If your boss is clearly overwhelmed, a brief “I’ll keep this short” will land far better than launching into a long explanation.

The Cold Open. Instead of starting with small talk or an apology, open with a specific observation about the other person’s situation. “You’ve had a lot coming in this week with the Henderson launch.” It shows you’ve been paying attention, and it moves the conversation onto real ground immediately.

The Pre-Frame. Set the context for the conversation before you get into it. “I want to talk about my role for a few minutes. I think there’s a way to make things easier for both of us.” When people know what kind of conversation they’re in, and that it’s not a threat, they listen better.

Written presence

More and more of how you come across happens in writing: emails, messages, documents. The same principles apply.

Lead with the point. Put your main request or conclusion in the first sentence. “I’d like to take Friday off.” Not three paragraphs of context first.

Cut the apologies and qualifiers. “Sorry to bother you, I was just wondering if maybe…” becomes “Could you…”

Keep it short. Long, over-explained messages signal anxiety. Short, clear ones signal confidence.

Don’t over-respond. Not every message needs an instant reply, as you practiced in Part Two. Responding thoughtfully, rather than immediately, often makes you seem more considered.

The halo

There’s one more aspect of presence worth knowing about. People tend to assume that someone who is impressive in one area is impressive in others. Psychologists call this the halo effect. Someone who looks calm and capable will often be assumed to be competent, trustworthy, and right, before they’ve proved anything.

You can use this honestly. When you look prepared, speak clearly, and stay calm, people give your ideas more weight. That isn’t manipulation. It’s removing the static that prevents your actual competence from being seen.

The same effect also works against you. If you seem anxious and uncertain, people may doubt ideas that are perfectly good. Much of the unfairness accommodating people experience at work, being overlooked, interrupted, having ideas credited to others, comes partly from this. Changing how you come across won’t fix every unfairness, but it often fixes more than you’d expect.

Don’t fake it

A word of caution. Presence isn’t about pretending to be someone you’re not. People can usually tell when someone is performing confidence, and it often backfires.

The goal is to remove the habits that hide you, not to put on a mask. You can stay warm, kind, and gentle. You can be quietly spoken. You can be someone who listens more than they talk. Many of the most influential people are exactly like that. What they don’t do is apologize for existing, rush through their sentences, or shrink when someone disagrees.

Chapter 21

Being Heard Without a Fight

Most accommodating people have two settings in a difficult conversation. Silence, or explosion. They hold everything in for weeks or years, and then, when they finally speak up, it comes out all at once, louder and harsher than they meant. The other person reacts to the explosion rather than the point, and the accommodating person retreats, convinced that speaking up only makes things worse.

There’s a third setting. You say what you mean, calmly and clearly, in a way the other person can actually hear. You don’t need to win the argument or crush them. You just need to be understood, and to understand them in return.

This chapter brings together several tactics from the original Shadow Persuasion system that do exactly that. They work because of something psychologists have studied for decades: when people feel pushed, they push back. The researcher Jack Brehm called this reactance. The moment someone senses you’re trying to change their mind, part of them resists, even if they’d otherwise agree with you. The tactics here lower that resistance. They help people listen instead of defending.

The Strategic Pause

What it is. A deliberate silence of a few seconds at an important moment. After you make a point. After someone else makes a demand. After a question.

Why it works. You practiced holding silence in Part Two, mostly to stop yourself from conceding. In Quiet Power, silence does something more. It signals confidence. It gives the other person space to think. And it often draws out more information, because people tend to fill silence with what they really mean.

How to use it. After making your key point, stop. Count to four in your head. Don’t add a qualifier, don’t soften it, don’t ask “does that make sense?” Just let it sit.

“I’d like to lead the next project.” (Pause.)

If someone makes a request you’re unsure about, pause before responding. You’ll often find they add something that changes the picture.

The Named Feeling

What it is. Putting a name to what the other person seems to be feeling, before they say it.

Why it works. People who feel understood become calmer and more open. Naming a feeling shows you’re paying attention. If you name it correctly, they feel seen. If you name it wrong, they’ll correct you, and you learn what’s really going on.

How to use it. Start with “It sounds like…” or “It seems like…” and name the feeling or concern you sense.

“It sounds like you’re worried this will land on you if it goes wrong.”

“It seems like you feel I’ve been pulling away lately.”

“It sounds like this deadline has put you in a tough spot.”

Then pause and let them respond. Don’t rush to fix it.

A caution. Use this genuinely. Naming feelings as a technique, without real curiosity, comes across as cold and scripted. The point is to understand, not to sound like a therapist.

The Reflective Echo

What it is. Repeating back what someone said, in your own words, with a little more precision than they used.

Why it works. It shows you’ve listened, which lowers defensiveness. It checks that you understood correctly. And by adding a little more clarity, it often helps the other person understand their own point better.

How to use it.

They say: “I just feel like nobody here values the work I do.” You say: “So it’s not the workload itself. It’s that the effort isn’t being recognized.”

They say: “You’re always on your phone when we’re together.” You say: “It sounds like you feel I’m not really present with you.”

Notice that neither response defends or argues. They just reflect. Once the other person feels heard, they’re much more able to hear you.

The Mirror They Don’t Catch

What it is. Using the other person’s own key words and phrases when you respond.

Why it works. People feel understood when you speak their language. If your manager talks about “bandwidth,” talk about bandwidth. If your partner says they feel “invisible,” use the word “invisible” when you respond. It’s subtle, rarely noticed, and builds a sense of connection.

How to use it. Listen for the words someone uses repeatedly or with emotion, and use them naturally.

They say: “I just need things to be predictable for a while.” You say: “That makes sense. Let’s figure out what would make the next few weeks more predictable.”

The Impossible Answer

What it is. A question that begins with “how” or “what” and can’t be answered with yes or no.

Why it works. Yes-or-no questions invite quick, defensive answers. Open questions invite the other person to think, explain, and sometimes solve the problem themselves. They also give you far more information. Negotiators often call these calibrated questions.

How to use it.

“How would you like me to prioritize this against the Henderson project?”

“What would need to happen for you to feel okay about me going?”

“How can we make this work for both of us?”

These questions are especially useful when someone makes an unreasonable demand. Instead of refusing, you ask them to help solve the problem their demand creates. “How am I supposed to finish both by Friday?” often leads the other person to adjust their expectations themselves.

The Pre-Understood Objection

What it is. Naming the other person’s likely objection before they raise it.

Why it works. When you state someone’s concern accurately, they feel understood, and they no longer need to argue for it. It also shows you’ve thought things through.

How to use it.

“You might be thinking this is bad timing, with the budget review coming up. I’ve thought about that, and here’s why I think it still makes sense.”

“I know you’re probably worried I’ll be less available if I take this course. Here’s how I’m planning to manage it.”

The Confession First

What it is. Opening with something you got wrong, or a weakness in your own position.

Why it works. It disarms people. When you admit a weakness, the other person doesn’t need to point it out, and they tend to trust the rest of what you say more.

How to use it.

“I know I’ve been late with the last two reports, and I’m fixing that. I also want to talk about how the workload has grown.”

“I should have raised this sooner. I didn’t, and I’m sorry about that. Here’s what’s been bothering me.”

Keep it genuine and brief. One honest admission, then your point.

The False Concession

What it is. Agreeing with a point the other person expects you to fight.

Why it works. People often prepare for a disagreement. When you agree with part of what they’re saying, their defenses drop, and the conversation becomes cooperative instead of combative.

How to use it.

“You’re right that I haven’t been pulling my weight with the housework lately.”

“That’s a fair point. The proposal does need more detail on costs.”

Then add your own point. Agreement first makes it much easier for them to hear what comes next.

Putting it together

These tactics work best in combination. Here’s how a difficult conversation might sound using several of them.

You: “I wanted to talk about the weekend shifts. (Pre-Frame.) I know I’ve been flexible about them for a long time, and I should have said something sooner. (Confession First.)”

Manager: “We’re just really short-staffed right now.”

You: “It sounds like you’re under real pressure to keep the schedule covered. (Named Feeling.)”

Manager: “Exactly. I don’t know what else to do.”

You: (Pause.) “How could we cover the weekends without me working all of them? (Impossible Answer.)”

Manager: “I suppose we could rotate them between the team.”

You: “A rotation would work well. (Mirror.) Could we start it next month?”

Notice what’s missing: no raised voices, no accusations, no long defense of your rights. You were heard, the problem was solved, and the relationship came through intact.

Getting What You’re Owed

Clause four of the Good Person Contract promised that if you were good enough, you’d be treated fairly. Work hard, stay patient, don’t complain, and the raise will come. The partner will notice everything you do. The family will finally see how much you carry.

You know by now that it rarely works that way. Fair treatment is mostly negotiated. The people who are paid well, appreciated at home, and treated fairly by their families usually aren’t more deserving than you. They’ve just learned to ask.

This chapter is about asking. Raises and pay. Rates if you work for yourself. Refunds and fair service. And the less obvious things you’re owed at home and in your family: a fair share of the work, recognition, and respect.

The principles

A few principles apply to every kind of asking.

Ask directly. Hints don’t work. “It’s been a while since my last raise” is a hint. “I’d like to discuss a raise” is a request. People can ignore hints. Requests require an answer.

Know your number. Decide what you want, and what you’d accept, before the conversation. People who go in without a number usually end up with whatever they’re offered.

Make your value visible. Don’t assume others know what you’ve done. Spell it out, with specifics.

Don’t negotiate against yourself. The voice in your head from Chapter 4 loves to talk you down before anyone else does. State your number and stop. Let them respond.

Be willing to hear no. You may not get what you ask for, at least not the first time. That’s fine. A no is information. It tells you where you stand, and it opens the next conversation.

Asking for a raise

Prepare

Know your market value. Look up what people in similar roles, in your area and industry, are paid. Salary websites, job listings, professional associations, and people in your network can all help.

List your contributions. Write down what you’ve achieved since your last pay change. Be specific. Projects delivered, money saved or earned, problems solved, responsibilities added, people trained. Numbers help.

Choose your number. Decide what you’ll ask for. A reasonable approach is to aim a little above what you’d be happy with, so you have room to negotiate.

Choose your timing. After a strong result, during review season, or when your responsibilities have clearly grown are all good moments. Searches for “how to ask for a raise” spike in the autumn and again in spring, around review periods, for a reason.

Ask

Request a dedicated meeting. Don’t raise it in passing.

“I’d like to set up thirty minutes to talk about my compensation. Would next Tuesday work?”

In the meeting, use the Pre-Frame from Chapter 20 to set the tone:

“I really value working here, and I want to keep growing with the team. I’d like to talk about my pay.”

Then make your case:

“Over the past year, I’ve taken on the client accounts from Sam’s team, led the system migration, and reduced our processing time by about thirty percent. Based on that, and on what similar roles pay in this area, I’m asking for an increase to eighty-five thousand.”

Then stop. Use the Strategic Pause. Let them respond.

Handle the response

If they say yes: thank them, and confirm it in writing.

If they say “not right now”: ask what it would take.

“I understand. What would I need to show for this to happen at my next review? Can we put those goals in writing?”

This turns a vague no into a specific plan, and protects you against the moving goalposts from Chapter 15.

If they say there’s no budget: ask about other options.

“I understand budgets are tight. Are there other ways we could recognize the added responsibility? A title change, extra time off, a training budget, or a review date in six months?”

If they make an offer below your number: don’t accept immediately.

“I appreciate that. It’s lower than I was hoping for. Could we get closer to eighty-five?”

Then pause again.

Use what you already have

The 48 Salary Negotiation Scripts bonus that comes with this book has word-for-word scripts for dozens of specific situations: new job offers, counteroffers, promotions without pay, remote work, and more. Use them alongside this chapter.

Setting your rates

If you work for yourself, as a freelancer, consultant, or small business owner, the same principles apply, with a few additions.

Raise your rates for new clients first. It’s easier to quote a higher rate to someone new than to change an existing arrangement.

State your rate, don’t ask about it. “My rate for this kind of project is four thousand dollars.” Not “Would four thousand be okay?”

Don’t discount before you’re asked. Many people offer a discount the moment they sense hesitation. Wait. Let the client respond.

When clients push back, use the Hidden Cost. This is a tactic from the original system: point out the cost of the alternative they haven’t considered.

“I understand it’s more than you planned. It’s worth comparing with the cost of hiring someone who takes twice as long, or having to redo the work later.”

Give existing clients notice. “Starting in March, my rate will be ninety dollars an hour. I wanted to let you know well in advance.”

Refunds and fair service

Chapter 9’s “send it back” drill was practice for this. When you pay for something and don’t get what you were promised, you’re owed a fix.

Be specific and calm. “I was charged twice for this order. Could you refund the second charge?”

Ask for what you want. A refund, a replacement, a repair. Don’t make them guess.

Use the Two Doors. Another tactic from the original system: offer two acceptable options.

“I’d be happy with either a full refund or a replacement shipped this week. Which works better for you?”

Both options work for you. Offering a choice makes it easier for the other person to say yes.

Escalate if needed. “I understand you can’t do it. Could I speak with someone who can?”

What you’re owed at home

Fairness at home is harder to negotiate than a raise, because nobody hands you a pay slip. But it matters just as much.

A fair share of the work

If you carry most of the household, childcare, or family logistics, start by making it visible. Many partners genuinely don’t realize how much is being done.

“I’d like us to sit down and list everything it takes to run the house: the cooking, cleaning, bills, school forms, appointments, birthdays, all of it. Then let’s look at who does what.”

Seeing it all on paper often changes things on its own. Then ask for a specific change:

“I’d like you to take over the school forms, the grocery shopping, and the Thursday pickups.”

Recognition

You’re allowed to want your effort noticed, and to say so.

“I’d love it if you’d notice when I handle things. It would mean a lot to hear a thank you sometimes.”

That’s not needy. It’s honest, and it’s what clause two of the contract taught you never to say.

Respect in your family

With family, what you’re owed is often respect for your adult choices. Use the Reverse Priority, a tactic from the original system, to find out what the real issue is: when someone raises several concerns, the one they mention last is often the most important.

“You’ve mentioned the job, the move, and that I don’t visit enough. If you had to pick, which matters most to you?”

Then address that one directly, and hold your position on the others.

Changing the Frame

Every conversation has a frame: an unspoken agreement about what the conversation is about, what’s at stake, and what the options are. Most of the time, nobody notices the frame. But it shapes everything that happens inside it.

When your manager says, “Can you stay late tonight or not?”, the frame is a yes-or-no question about tonight. When your sister says, “Either you host Christmas or Mom will be devastated,” the frame is two options, both of which involve you sacrificing something. When a colleague says, “This is about whether you’re a team player,” the frame is your character.

People who get stuck in difficult conversations are often fighting inside a frame that’s already rigged against them. Quiet power means noticing the frame and, when it doesn’t serve you, gently changing it.

This chapter brings together tactics from the original Shadow Persuasion system that do exactly that. None of them involve raising your voice or winning an argument. They change what the argument is about.

The Stakes Shift

What it is. Changing what’s actually at risk in the conversation.

Why it works. People argue hardest about whatever they think is at stake. If the stakes are framed as your loyalty, your commitment, or your character, every disagreement becomes a test you have to pass. Shifting the stakes moves the conversation to ground where you can actually talk.

How to use it. Name what you think really matters, and move the conversation there.

They say: “If you don’t stay late, it shows you’re not committed.” You say: “I’m very committed to getting this right. The question is how we get it done without burning people out, so the quality stays high.”

They say: “If you don’t come to Christmas, it means family isn’t important to you.” You say: “Family is important to me. What I want to figure out is how we spend time together in a way that works for everyone, not just this one week.”

The Category Move

What it is. Redefining what kind of conversation you’re having.

Why it works. People behave differently in different kinds of conversations. A negotiation feels adversarial. A problem-solving session feels cooperative. A complaint feels like an attack. Choosing the category changes how both of you behave.

How to use it.

“I don’t want this to be an argument. I’d like us to solve the problem together.”

“This isn’t a complaint about you. I’m trying to figure out how to make the workload work for both of us.”

“I’m not asking for a favor. I’m proposing a trade that helps us both.”

The Third Option

What it is. When someone presents two choices, introducing a third.

Why it works. Ultimatums and either-or questions often hide other possibilities. Pointing them out frees you from a rigged choice and makes you the person solving the problem rather than the person being cornered.

How to use it.

They say: “Either you take on the project or we’ll have to give it to someone more junior.” You say: “There might be another way. What if I lead the planning and someone more junior handles the day-to-day work?”

They say: “You either come every Sunday or you obviously don’t care.” You say: “What about this: I call every Sunday, and we see each other in person once a month.”

The Exposed Assumption

What it is. Naming an unspoken rule that the other person is operating under.

Why it works. Many conflicts rest on assumptions nobody has said out loud: that the person who’s best at something should always do it, that the oldest sibling handles the parents, that whoever is home more does the housework. Once an assumption is spoken, it can be questioned. Before that, it just controls things.

How to use it.

“It sounds like we’re assuming that because I’m good with spreadsheets, I should do all the reporting. Is that how we want to divide it?”

“I think there’s an unspoken rule in our family that because I live closest, I’m in charge of Mom’s care. I’d like to talk about whether that’s fair.”

The Misclassification

What it is. Quietly correcting a label someone has put on you or the situation.

Why it works. Labels stick. If you’re labeled “the reliable one,” you’ll keep getting extra work. If your boundary is labeled “being difficult,” every future boundary will be read the same way. Correcting the label, calmly, changes how people see the situation.

How to use it.

They say: “You’re being difficult.” You say: “I don’t think I’m being difficult. I’m being clear about what I can take on.”

They say: “You’re the one who always handles this.” You say: “I’ve been handling it, but that was never really decided. I’d like to change it.”

They say: “This is just you being sensitive again.” You say: “This isn’t sensitivity. I’m telling you something that bothers me.”

The Volcano

What it is. Letting someone’s strong emotion run its course without reacting, then responding calmly once it’s spent.

Why it works. When someone is angry or upset, anything you say tends to escalate things. If you stay calm and quiet, the emotion usually burns out on its own. When it does, the person is often more open to listening, and the calm person has more influence than the angry one.

How to use it. Stay still. Breathe slowly. Don’t interrupt, argue, or defend. Nod if appropriate. When they’ve finished, wait a moment, then speak calmly.

“I can see how frustrated you are. I’d like to understand what’s behind it.”

When not to use it. The Volcano is for ordinary frustration, not abuse. If someone is threatening, shouting at you for a long time, or making you feel unsafe, leave, as described in Chapter 16. Staying calm is not the same as staying put.

The Mutual Enemy

What it is. Recasting a conflict between two people as a shared problem you’re both facing.

Why it works. When you’re on opposite sides, every conversation is a fight. When you’re on the same side against a common problem, you start working together.

How to use it. Name the problem as something outside both of you.

“We’re not really fighting each other. We’re both fighting this deadline.”

“The real problem isn’t you or me. It’s that there’s too much to do and not enough time. How do we handle that together?”

“I think we’re both frustrated with how disorganized the holidays always get. What if we fix that part?”

A caution. Use this for real shared problems, not to invent a scapegoat. Turning people against a third person to win an argument is a manipulation tactic, and Chapter 26 addresses that line directly.

The Paradox Reveal

What it is. Pointing out, without accusation, a contradiction in someone’s position.

Why it works. People generally want to be consistent. When they notice a contradiction in what they’re asking, they often adjust their position themselves. The key is doing it gently, so they don’t feel caught out.

How to use it.

“I’m a little confused. You’ve said you want me to take more initiative, but the last two times I did, I was told to check with you first. Can you help me understand which you’d prefer?”

“You’ve said you want us to spend more time together, and you’ve also asked me to take on more of the household work. I’m trying to figure out how to do both.”

The Disqualification Move

What it is. Saying clearly what the conversation is not about.

Why it works. People come into conversations with a script. If they think you’re about to criticize them, they prepare a defense. If you remove that script at the start, they can listen.

How to use it.

“This isn’t about blame. I just want to figure out what went wrong so it doesn’t happen again.”

“I’m not asking you to change your whole approach. There’s just one thing I’d like to adjust.”

“I’m not upset with you. I want to talk about how we plan the holidays this year.”

Choosing the right move

You don’t need to use all of these. In most situations, one well-chosen move is enough. A simple guide:

  • When you’re being pushed into an unfair choice, try the Third Option.
  • When your character is being questioned, try the Stakes Shift or the Misclassification.
  • When a hidden rule is running things, try the Exposed Assumption.
  • When someone is very upset, try the Volcano, then the Category Move.
  • When you’re stuck on opposite sides, try the Mutual Enemy or the Disqualification Move.

Chapter 24

Office Politics and Alliances

Many accommodating people hate the idea of office politics. It sounds manipulative: flattery, backstabbing, people who are good at schmoozing getting ahead of people who are good at their jobs. So they opt out. They keep their heads down, do excellent work, and assume it will speak for itself.

It usually doesn’t. And opting out of politics doesn’t mean politics stops affecting you. It means it affects you without your input.

Office politics, at its core, is simply how decisions get made when people are involved. Who gets the promotion, whose idea gets funded, who gets blamed when something goes wrong. Those decisions are shaped by relationships, reputation, and information, not just by the quality of the work. The same is true in families, friend groups, community organizations, and any other group of people.

This chapter isn’t about becoming a schemer. It’s about understanding how influence actually flows, so you can take part honestly and stop being the person everyone else’s politics happens to.

Formal power and real power

Every group has two maps of power.

The formal map is the organization chart: who reports to whom, who has which title, who signs off on what. It’s important, but it’s only part of the picture.

The real map shows who actually influences decisions. The assistant who controls the director’s calendar. The long-serving engineer everyone goes to before making a change. The colleague whose opinion the boss always asks for. The aunt who decides where the family holiday happens, even though nobody ever voted.

People who navigate groups well understand both maps. People who only see the formal one are often surprised when decisions go against them.

How to see the real map. Pay attention to a few things.

  • Whose opinion does the decision-maker ask for before deciding?
  • Who do people go to for information or help, regardless of title?
  • Whose objections stop things from happening?
  • Who knows what’s happening before everyone else?

Write down what you notice. Over a few weeks, the real map becomes clear.

Favors and goodwill

In every group, people keep an informal record of who has helped whom. Not in a calculating way, usually, but the record exists. People who have helped others build goodwill. When they need support, they’re more likely to get it.

Accommodating people often give a great deal of help. The problem is that they give it to everyone, constantly, without anyone noticing, and without ever drawing on the goodwill it creates. They’re running a bank account full of deposits and never making a withdrawal.

Quiet power means being more deliberate.

The Unrepayable Favor. A tactic from the original system: offer something small and specific that’s genuinely useful to someone, without asking for anything in return. Forward an article relevant to their project. Introduce them to someone who can help. Mention their good work to their manager. These small, thoughtful acts build goodwill far more effectively than taking on huge amounts of extra work.

Help strategically, not constantly. Instead of saying yes to everyone, focus your help on people who matter to your work and who would help you in return. That isn’t cold. It’s sustainable.

Let your help be seen. Not by boasting, but by not hiding it. When you’ve helped another team succeed, it’s fine for that to be known.

Make the occasional withdrawal. When you need support, ask the people you’ve helped. “I’ve got a proposal going to the leadership team next week. Would you be willing to back it if you think it’s a good idea?”

Building alliances

An alliance is simply a relationship of mutual support. In every group, people with strong alliances have more influence than people working alone, regardless of their skill.

Get to know people beyond your immediate circle. Have coffee with someone from another team. Ask about their work. Find out what they’re struggling with. Most of your future opportunities will come through people who aren’t on your direct team.

Find out what others need. Use the six core needs from Chapter 19. A colleague who needs recognition will value someone who notices their work. One who needs safety will value someone who keeps them informed.

Share information generously. People who share useful information are valued and trusted. People who hoard it are avoided.

Support others publicly. Back good ideas in meetings, even when they’re not yours. Give credit openly. People remember who stood with them.

Making your work visible

Chapter 15 covered stolen credit. The broader point is that good work doesn’t speak for itself. Someone has to speak for it, and if it’s not you, it may be someone else.

The Public Witness. A tactic from the original system: involve others in your commitments and achievements so they’re part of the record. Copy relevant people on progress updates. Present your own work. Mention results in team meetings.

Keep a running list of achievements. Update it monthly. Use it in reviews, raise conversations, and when you’re preparing for new roles.

Talk about outcomes, not effort. “I worked really hard on the migration” tells people how tired you are. “The migration cut processing time by thirty percent” tells them what you achieved.

Brief your manager regularly. A short weekly or monthly update on what you’ve done and what you’re working on keeps your contributions visible to the person who most influences your career.

Handling difficult players

Every group has a few people who play politics in ways that hurt others: the credit-taker, the gossip, the blame-shifter, the one who agrees in meetings and undermines afterward. The tactics in Part Three apply, but a few additions help at work.

Put things in writing. After any important conversation with someone you don’t trust, send a short email confirming what was agreed. This protects you against rewritten history (Chapter 12).

Don’t join the gossip. When someone shares gossip with you, stay neutral. “I hadn’t heard that.” Then change the subject. People who gossip to you will gossip about you.

Address undermining directly, but privately. “I noticed in the meeting you seemed to support the plan, but I’ve heard you raised concerns with the director afterward. If you have concerns, I’d like to hear them directly.”

Build allies who can speak for you. Difficult players are much less effective against someone with strong relationships across the organization.

Politics at home

The same principles apply in families and friend groups.

Every family has a real map of power that differs from the formal one. Who decides where holidays happen. Whose approval matters most. Who everyone calls first when there’s a problem. Who carries the information about what’s going on.

If you’re always the one who does the work and never the one who shapes decisions, the advice in this chapter applies directly. Notice the real map. Build alliances with the siblings or relatives who share your concerns. Make your contributions visible. And when you need support for a decision, ask for it directly, from the people whose goodwill you’ve built.

“I’ve been handling most of Mom’s appointments. I’d like your support when we talk to the others about sharing them.”

Politics without becoming political

The fear that holds many accommodating people back is that playing politics will turn them into the schemers they dislike. It doesn’t have to.

The difference is honesty. Building relationships, sharing information, making your work visible, and asking for support are all honest. Lying, spreading rumors, taking credit you didn’t earn, and setting people against each other are not. You can do all of the first list and none of the second. In fact, people who do the first list well rarely need the second.

Making It Stick

You finally have the conversation. You ask for the raise, set the boundary, or agree a new arrangement with your partner. They say yes. You walk away relieved.

A week later, nothing has changed. The raise was “being processed” and then forgotten. The boundary is being tested again. Your partner did the chores twice and then slipped back into the old pattern. The agreement evaporated.

This happens constantly, and it’s one of the main reasons accommodating people stop asking for things. It feels as though even when they win, they lose. But agreements falling apart usually isn’t a sign that the other person was lying. It’s a sign that the agreement wasn’t anchored. People forget, get busy, have second thoughts, or quietly drift back toward what’s easiest.

This chapter covers how to make agreements stick: tactics from the original Shadow Persuasion system that protect a yes after you get it.

The Unclosing

What it is. When someone agrees, don’t make a big deal of it.

Why it works. When people agree to something, especially something they were unsure about, a big reaction can trigger second thoughts. Excessive thanks, celebration, or relief signals that they’ve conceded something significant, and they may start to wonder whether they should have.

How to use it. When you get a yes, acknowledge it simply and move on naturally.

“Great, thank you. So, about the Henderson timeline…”

Not: “Oh, thank you so much, I really appreciate it, this means so much, I know it’s a big ask…”

The One-Line Email

What it is. After a verbal agreement, send a short written confirmation.

Why it works. Memories fade and get rewritten, as Chapter 12 explained. A brief written note creates a shared record. It’s too short to start a new debate, and too clear to misunderstand.

How to use it. Within a day, send something like:

“Thanks for today. Just confirming: my salary will move to eighty-five thousand from the March pay cycle.”

“Good to talk. As agreed, you’ll take the Tuesday and Thursday pickups starting next week.”

Keep it short. No long recap, no new requests, no apologies.

The Minimum Viable Commitment

What it is. Ask for the smallest concrete step that confirms the agreement.

Why it works. Big agreements are easy to make and easy to forget. A small, specific action locks them in. Once someone has taken the first step, they’re much more likely to follow through on the rest.

How to use it.

“Great. Could we put the review date in the calendar now?”

“Sounds good. Could you send the updated job description by Friday?”

“Let’s start this week. Can you do Thursday’s pickup?”

The Waiting Period

What it is. After an agreement, give it a moment to settle before rushing into details.

Why it works. Rushing into logistics straight away can make people feel pressured, and pressure creates second thoughts. A brief pause lets the agreement settle as a decision, not a concession.

How to use it. After a yes, let a short silence sit, or move to a lighter topic for a moment, before getting into specifics.

The Future Echo

What it is. Describe a specific moment after the agreement has been put into practice.

Why it works. When people picture the agreement working, it becomes more real to them, and they become more invested in making it happen.

How to use it.

“I think it’ll make a big difference. Next month, when the new rota’s in place, the team won’t be scrambling every weekend.”

“I’m looking forward to Thursday evenings being a bit calmer for both of us.”

The Permanent Status

What it is. Talk about the agreement as a settled fact, not a trial.

Why it works. Language shapes how people think. “If we try this” sounds temporary and easy to drop. “Now that we’re doing this” sounds settled.

How to use it.

“Now that you’re handling the pickups, I’ll take the grocery shopping.”

“Since I’m leading the planning for the next project, I’d like to set up a kickoff meeting.”

The Earlier Agreement

What it is. When an agreement starts to slip, refer back to what was agreed.

Why it works. People generally want to be consistent with their own commitments. A calm reminder of what they agreed to is usually enough to bring things back on track. This is where the One-Line Email pays off.

How to use it.

“When we spoke in January, we agreed I’d move to eighty-five thousand from March. I noticed it hasn’t come through yet. Can you check on it?”

“We agreed you’d take Tuesdays and Thursdays. It’s been my turn the last two weeks. Can we get back to the plan?”

Keep it factual. No accusation, no sarcasm. Just the agreement and the gap.

The Last-Word Exit

What it is. End important conversations on a deliberate note.

Why it works. People remember the end of a conversation more than the middle. The last thing said shapes how they feel about the whole exchange, and what they think about until the next conversation.

How to use it. Close with something that reinforces the agreement and the relationship.

“Thanks for hearing me out. I’m glad we’ve got a plan.”

“I appreciate you taking this seriously. It means a lot.”

Avoid ending with an apology (“Sorry for taking up so much of your time”) or a new worry (“I hope this doesn’t cause problems”). Those undo the work you just did.

When agreements still slip

Sometimes, even with all of this, agreements fall apart. A few guidelines for handling that.

Raise it early. The longer you wait, the more permanent the slip becomes. Address it the first or second time it happens, not the tenth.

Raise it once, clearly. Use the Earlier Agreement. Don’t hint, sigh, or wait for them to notice.

Look for a pattern. One slip is normal. A pattern of agreements that never hold is information about the other person, or about whether they really agreed in the first place.

Ask what’s getting in the way. Sometimes agreements slip for genuine reasons. “I noticed the pickups have slipped. Is something making Thursdays hard?” This opens a conversation instead of an argument.

Decide what you’ll do if it keeps happening. If a boss keeps forgetting promised raises, or a partner keeps dropping agreed responsibilities, you may need to change your own response. Look for a new job, stop covering the dropped tasks, or have a more serious conversation. You can’t force someone to keep their word. You can decide what you’ll do when they don’t.

Chapter 26

The Line You Don’t Cross

This book is called Shadow Persuasion. You’ve learned how people pressure, guilt, flatter, rush, and intimidate others. You’ve learned to read what people really want, how to come across with quiet authority, how to change the frame of a conversation, and how to make agreements stick.

That’s a lot of power. And power has a way of changing people.

Some readers will finish this book and use what they’ve learned to protect themselves, ask for what they’re owed, and influence the people around them fairly. Others, without meaning to, will start using the same tactics that were once used on them. The guilt trip that worked on you. The silence that wore you down. The flattery before the ask. Once you understand how these work, it’s tempting to use them.

This final chapter is about the line between influence and manipulation, and how to stay on the right side of it.

The difference

Influence and manipulation can look similar from the outside. Both involve trying to change what someone thinks or does. The difference lies in three things.

Honesty. Influence uses true information and real reasons. Manipulation uses deception, half-truths, or invented urgency.

Respect for choice. Influence leaves the other person genuinely free to say no. Manipulation tries to remove that freedom, by making refusal too painful, too frightening, or too confusing.

Whose interests it serves. Influence aims for an outcome that’s fair to both people, or at least doesn’t harm the other person. Manipulation serves one person at the other’s expense.

A raise conversation where you present your real achievements and real market data, and your boss can say no, is influence. A raise conversation where you invent a competing offer to pressure your boss is manipulation, even if you get the same result.

Four tests

When you’re not sure which side of the line you’re on, these four questions help.

The disclosure test. Would I be comfortable if the other person knew exactly what I was doing and why? If you’re using the Named Feeling to genuinely understand someone, you’d be fine with them knowing. If you’re faking concern to get something, you wouldn’t.

The reversal test. Would I think this was fair if someone did it to me? You know exactly how guilt trips, silent treatments, and fake urgency feel. If you’d resent it being done to you, don’t do it to someone else.

The free-choice test. Can the other person genuinely say no without being punished? If refusing would cost them your warmth, your approval, or their peace, you’re applying pressure, not influence.

The outcome test. Is the outcome fair to both of us? Or am I getting what I want at their expense?

If you pass all four, you’re almost certainly on the right side of the line.

Tactics to leave alone

Some techniques in the influence world are inherently manipulative. A few were part of the original Shadow Persuasion system. In this book they appear only so you can recognize them when they’re used on you.

Creating false scarcity. Pretending something is in shorter supply than it is, or pulling back an offer you never intended to withdraw, to make the other person want it more.

Manufacturing urgency. Inventing deadlines to stop someone from thinking carefully.

The hidden escalation ladder. Planning a series of requests, each slightly bigger than the last, while hiding where it’s heading.

Inventing enemies. Uniting people by turning them against a third person or group who isn’t really the problem.

Exploiting someone’s openings. Deliberately pressing on someone’s guilt, loneliness, fear, or need for approval because you know they can’t resist it.

Withdrawal as punishment. Going silent or cold to get your way.

You know how these feel from the receiving end. That knowledge is the best protection against using them yourself.

The trap of becoming them

There’s a specific risk for people who have spent a long time being used. When they finally find their power, some swing to the other extreme. They become rigid, cold, or controlling, determined never to be taken advantage of again.

It’s understandable. But it isn’t freedom. It’s the same pattern turned inside out. The person who once couldn’t say no now can’t say yes. The person who once absorbed everyone’s feelings now refuses to care about anyone’s. Their fear of being used still runs their life. It just looks different.

Chapter 2 introduced clause five of the Good Person Contract: if you stop being nice, you’ll become one of them. The goal of this book has never been to stop being nice. It has been to stop being nice by default, to everyone, at any cost. You can be warm and firm. Generous and limited. Kind and clear.

A few signs that you might be swinging too far:

  • You’re saying no to things you’d actually enjoy, just to prove you can.
  • You’ve stopped caring how your decisions affect others.
  • You treat every request as an attempt to use you.
  • You’re using guilt, silence, or pressure on others in the way it was once used on you.
  • People who used to feel close to you now seem wary.

If you notice these, it doesn’t mean you’ve failed. It means you’re adjusting. Come back to the middle. Use the four tests. Let yourself say yes, generously, when you genuinely want to.

Power that makes things better

Used well, the skills in this book don’t just protect you. They make your relationships better.

When you can say no clearly, your yes means something. People trust it. When you can disagree calmly, conflicts get resolved instead of buried. When you ask for what you need, the people who care about you finally know how to help. When you understand what others really want, you can meet their needs without giving up yours. When you can be heard without a fight, difficult conversations stop being so difficult.

That’s the real goal of quiet power. Not winning. Not control. Relationships that are honest and fair in both directions, where you’re neither the person being used nor the person doing the using.

Where to go from here

You’ve reached the end of the main book. Here’s how to keep the work going.

Keep practicing. Choose one courage drill each week. Rotate through them. Keep writing short evening entries two or three times a week.

Use the playbooks. The back of this book has quick playbooks for the specific people in your life: the boss, the partner, the ex, aging parents, siblings, in-laws, the friend who always needs something. Turn to them when a situation comes up.

Revisit Part Three when you need it. When someone uses a tactic on you, look it up. The calm replies work best when you’ve read them recently.

Use the Decoder. When a message leaves you confused, guilty, or pressured, paste it in. Seeing the tactic named often takes away most of its power.

Be patient with yourself. Old patterns don’t disappear in a few weeks. You’ll still sometimes say yes when you mean no, apologize when you don’t need to, or replay a conversation at 3 a.m. That’s not failure. It’s being human. What matters is that it happens less often, and that you recover faster.

One last exercise: your new contract

Open your journal to a fresh page.

In Chapter 2, you read the Good Person Contract, the deal you signed as a child without knowing it. Now write a new one. Your own terms, as an adult, written with an adult’s judgment.

It might include clauses like these:

  • Other people’s feelings matter, and they belong to them.
  • My needs are information, not a burden.
  • Disagreement is normal and survivable.
  • I ask for what I’m owed.
  • I can be kind and clear at the same time.

Write yours in your own words. Keep it short enough to remember. Then put it somewhere you’ll see it: the first page of your journal, a note on your phone, a card in your wallet.

This is the contract you’ll live by now. And unlike the first one, you get to renegotiate it whenever you choose.

What to watch for. Withdrawal (Chapter 13). “If you loved me” (Chapter 11). The minimizer (Chapter 12). If you see the danger signs in Chapter 16, get support.

Go deeper. Chapter 21 for being heard. Chapter 19 for understanding what they really need.

The ex

The usual pattern. Reopening old arguments. Using children, shared friends, or shared finances as bargaining chips. Guilt about the breakup. Rewriting what happened. Messages designed to provoke a reaction.

What to say. Keep communication brief, factual, and focused on logistics.

“I’m happy to discuss the pickup schedule. I’m not going to discuss the past.”

“I’ll respond to messages about the kids. I won’t respond to the rest.”

When you write, keep it short, polite, and to the point. Leave out emotion, blame, and long explanations. Assume anything you write could be read by someone else later.

What to watch for. Rewriting the past (Chapter 12). Guilt (Chapter 11). Ultimatums and implied threats (Chapter 16).

Go deeper. Chapter 12 for protecting your memory. Chapter 16 if you feel unsafe. If you share children or are involved in legal proceedings, take advice specific to your situation.

Dating again after a manipulative relationship

The usual pattern. Being drawn to the same type of person. Moving too fast because the attention feels wonderful. Ignoring early warning signs. Over-explaining and over-accommodating from the first date.

What to do.

“I really like spending time with you. I’d like to take things a bit slower.”

Keep your own friendships, routines, and plans. Don’t make major decisions, like moving in or combining money, in the first few months. Watch how they respond to small nos early on.

What to watch for. Love bombing (Chapter 14). Anger or sulking when you say no to small things. Pressure to commit quickly. Criticism that begins once you’re attached.

Go deeper. Chapter 14 for charm and pressure. Chapter 3 for your own tells.

Aging parents

The usual pattern. Increasing demands on your time. Guilt about not visiting enough. The expectation that you, especially if you live nearby or are the “responsible one,” will handle everything. Old family roles snapping back into place.

What to say.

“I can’t come every weekend, but I’d love to call every Sunday and visit the first weekend of each month.”

“I’m happy to keep doing the appointments. I need someone else to handle the bills and pharmacy.”

“I know you’ve done a lot for me, and I’m grateful. I’m still not able to do this.”

What to watch for. The ledger (Chapter 11). Scope creep (Chapter 15). Caring too much (Chapter 5).

Go deeper. Chapter 5 for caring without carrying. Chapter 23 for the Exposed Assumption, especially “whoever lives closest does everything.”

Adult siblings

The usual pattern. Unequal sharing of family responsibilities. Old rivalries and childhood roles. One sibling who always needs rescuing. Disagreements about parents’ care or money.

What to say.

“I think there’s an unspoken rule that I handle Mom’s care because I live closest. I’d like to talk about whether that’s fair.”

“I’ve lent you money a few times now. I’m not going to lend more until the earlier loans are paid back.”

“I’m not going to get involved in this argument between you and Dad.”

What to watch for. “Everyone agrees with me” (Chapter 12). The unreturned loan (Chapter 15). The martyr (Chapter 11).

Go deeper. Chapter 24 for alliances within the family. Chapter 23 for changing the frame.

In-laws

The usual pattern. Unsolicited advice. Criticism of your parenting, home, or choices. Expectations about holidays and visits. Your partner caught in the middle.

What to say. Agree with your partner on the main boundaries first. Then:

“Thanks for the suggestion. We’ve decided to do it differently.”

“We’ll be spending Christmas with my family this year. We’d love to see you on the weekend after.”

“I’m not going to discuss our parenting decisions.”

What to watch for. Public pressure at gatherings (Chapter 16). The character test (Chapter 11). Visible suffering (Chapter 11).

Go deeper. Chapter 21 for being heard. Chapter 24 for working as a team with your partner.

The friend who always needs something

The usual pattern. Long calls about problems that never get solved. Favors that flow in one direction. Showing up when they need you, disappearing when you need them. A permanent crisis.

What to say.

“I can talk for twenty minutes before I need to start dinner.”

“Do you want advice, or do you just want me to listen?”

“I can’t help this time. I hope it works out.”

What to watch for. Borrowed intimacy (Chapter 14). Visible suffering (Chapter 11). The ladder of small yeses (Chapter 15).

Go deeper. Chapter 5 for caring without carrying. Chapter 9 for help with edges.

The salesperson or contractor

The usual pattern. Pressure to decide today. Extras added to the bill. Work not done as agreed. Deadlines missed. Feeling too awkward to complain.

What to say.

“I don’t buy anything on the same day. I’ll think about it and get back to you.”

“This isn’t what we agreed. Please fix it before I pay the final invoice.”

“I’d be happy with either a refund or the work redone this week. Which works better for you?”

What to watch for. Fake urgency (Chapter 14). High-pressure sales (Chapter 17). The hidden-price favor (Chapter 11).

Go deeper. Chapter 17 for manipulation at scale. Chapter 22 for refunds and fair service.

The Tactic Quick Reference

Every pressure tactic in this book, in one place. For each one: how much harm it can do when used often, who it works on most, and the core of the calm reply.

How much harm is a rough guide. Low means it’s mostly irritating and usually stops when it stops working. Medium means it can wear you down over time. High means it can seriously damage your confidence, finances, or safety, especially when used often by someone close to you.

Who it works on refers to the openings from Chapter 3: guilt, need for approval, fear of conflict, need to be seen as good, loneliness or fear of loss, and urgency.

Where to Get Help

This book can help with everyday pressure. Some situations need more than a book. If any of these apply to you, please reach out.

If you feel unsafe with someone you live with or are close to. The National Domestic Violence Hotline (United States) is available at any hour: call 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788. You can also chat online at thehotline.org.

If you’re in crisis or thinking about harming yourself. Call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (United States), available at any hour.

If you’ve been targeted by a scam. Contact your bank right away, then report it to the Federal Trade Commission at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

If you’re in immediate danger, call 911 or your local emergency number.

Outside the United States, most countries have similar free services. Your local emergency number, doctor, or community health service can point you to them.

Talking to a licensed counselor or therapist can also help, especially if a relationship has left you doubting your own memory or judgment. Getting support is not a failure of anything in this book. It’s one of the most important skills in it.
