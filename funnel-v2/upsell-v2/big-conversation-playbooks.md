# The Big Conversation Playbooks

## Step-by-step plans for the 15 conversations that decide how you're treated

*A companion to Shadow Persuasion: How to Stop Being Used and Take Your Power Back, by Nate Harlan*

---

## How to use these playbooks

Some conversations matter more than others. Most days you need a quick, calm reply to a guilt trip or a pushy request. But a few times a year, a single conversation sets the terms for months to come. The talk with the boss who keeps leaning on you. The day you say no to lending your brother money. The evening you end a relationship. How that one conversation goes often decides how you're treated afterward.

This book is for those moments. It isn't meant to be read in order. When one of these conversations is coming up, turn to that playbook, read it through once or twice, and walk in with a plan.

Every playbook follows the same pattern:

- **When to use it** tells you which situation it covers.
- **What's really at stake** names what you stand to gain or lose.
- **Before you start** gives you one or two things to prepare.
- **Your opening line** is a first sentence you can use as written or put in your own words.
- **Three moves** carry you through the middle of the conversation.
- **If they push back** covers the responses you're most likely to hear, with a calm reply to each and a pointer to the chapter in Shadow Persuasion that explains what's going on.
- **How to close it** gives you a way to end the conversation and make the result last.
- **What not to do** names the mistakes that undo the rest.

A few things to keep in mind.

**These plans complement the short Playbooks at the back of the main book.** Those cover the people who test you most often: the boss, the partner, the aging parent, the friend who always needs something. These cover single events, the one big conversation. If you're dealing with a pattern, start there. If you're facing a specific moment, start here. For the dozens of everyday lines people throw at you, use The Calm Reply Scripts. For conversations about pay, use the salary guide.

**The words are a starting point, not a script to recite.** Change them until they sound like you. What matters is the shape: say the main thing early, listen properly, hold your position calmly, and confirm what was agreed.

**Everything here is meant to be honest.** These plans only work if what you say is true. Don't say "I'm not leaving" if you are, or "I'd like to help another way" if you wouldn't. Every reply in this book passes the four tests in Chapter 26: you'd be comfortable if the other person knew exactly what you were doing, you'd think it was fair if it were done to you, the other person stays free to say no, and the outcome is fair to both of you.

**Some situations need more than a good conversation.** If someone has hurt you, threatened you, controls your money or your phone, or you feel afraid of them, don't try to handle it with a script. Step back and get support from someone you trust, a counselor, or a domestic violence service. In the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is available at any hour: call 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788. If you're in immediate danger, call 911. Chapter 16 explains the warning signs.

## Work

### 1. The hostile or difficult boss

**When to use it.** Your manager regularly snaps at you, criticizes you in front of others, piles on work with no warning, or treats you with a coldness they don't show the rest of the team. You've been absorbing it, and you've decided to have one calm, direct conversation about how you work together.

**What's really at stake.** Your peace of mind, your reputation, and possibly your job. If you stay silent, the pattern usually continues, and you start to believe you deserve it. If you explode, you hand them a reason to see you as the problem. One clear conversation can reset how they treat you. Even when it doesn't, you'll know where you stand and can decide what to do next.

**Before you start.** Write down two or three specific moments, with dates, and what was said. Specifics are harder to brush aside than "you're always hard on me." Then decide on the one change you most want.

**Your opening line.**

> "I'd like thirty minutes with you this week to talk about how we work together. I want to do my best work for you, and I think a few things are getting in the way."

**Three moves.**

1. **Describe one specific behavior and its effect, without labeling them.** Talk about what happened, not what kind of person they are.

   > "In Tuesday's meeting, the report was criticized in front of the whole team. I'm happy to hear criticism. I'd rather hear it one-on-one, so I can actually fix the problem."

2. **Ask what they need from you, and listen properly.** Hostile managers are often under pressure you can't see. Understanding what they're worried about (Chapter 19) doesn't excuse how they behave, but it shows you what they actually want.

   > "What would doing this job well look like to you over the next couple of months?"

3. **Propose a specific way of working together.** Make it concrete and easy to agree to.

   > "Could we do a fifteen-minute check-in on Monday mornings? You can tell me what's most important that week, and anything that isn't working, before it becomes a problem."

**If they push back.**

- **"I don't have time for this."** This is a brush-off, not a no (Chapter 21 covers keeping the conversation going without a fight).

  > "I understand you're stretched. Then let me ask just one thing: what's the one change that would matter most to you?"

- **"You're being too sensitive."** This is the minimizer (Chapter 12). It turns your concern into a flaw in you.

  > "Maybe. I'm not asking you to go easy on my work. I'm asking for feedback in private rather than in front of the team."

- **"If you can't handle the pressure, maybe this isn't the right job for you."** This is an implied threat (Chapter 16). Ask them to say plainly what they mean.

  > "Can you say more about what you mean? Is my job at risk, or are you frustrated with how things are going right now?"

**How to close it.**

> "Thanks for making the time. I'll send you a short note with what we agreed, and let's see how the Monday check-ins go over the next month."

Send that note within a day. Keep it to two or three lines: the change you agreed and when it starts (the one-line confirmation from Chapter 25). If things slip back, refer to what was agreed, calmly and early.

**What not to do.**

- Don't apologize for things you didn't do just to calm them down. It teaches them that pressure works.
- Don't have this conversation in public, by group chat, or right after a blow-up. Wait for a calm moment.
- If the behavior includes threats, harassment, or discrimination, don't rely on a conversation alone. Keep your notes and take it to HR or a manager above them.

### 2. Someone took credit for your work

**When to use it.** A coworker presented your idea as theirs in a meeting. Your manager passed your analysis up the line with only their name on it. Someone accepted praise for a project you carried. You want the record corrected and want it not to happen again.

**What's really at stake.** Your reputation and your future. Promotions, raises, and interesting projects go to the people decision-makers think did the work. If you let it slide once, it becomes easier for it to happen again, and you stay invisible while others build careers on your effort (Chapter 15).

**Before you start.** Gather the record: the email where you first shared the idea, the draft with your name on it, the notes from the meeting. Decide whether to speak up in the moment, in a private conversation, or both.

**Your opening line.** In the moment, if you can:

> "Thanks for picking that up. Since I put the original analysis together, let me add the detail on how we'd make it work."

Then, in a private conversation afterward:

> "I wanted to talk about the meeting this morning. The proposal that was presented came from the work I shared with you last week, and my name wasn't on it."

**Three moves.**

1. **State the facts and leave out the accusation.** You don't need to prove they meant to do it. You only need the facts on the table.

   > "I sent you the first draft on the 12th. What was presented today was mostly that draft."

2. **Say what you want going forward.** Be specific about the fix.

   > "Going forward, when we present something I worked on, I'd like my name on it, and I'd like to present my part myself."

3. **Make your work visible from now on.** Don't rely on this one conversation. Copy the right people on progress updates, present your own work, and keep a running list of what you've done (Chapter 24).

   > "I'll send a short update to the team on where the project stands, so everyone has the full picture."

**If they push back.**

- **"We all worked on it. It's a team effort."** This is fog (Chapter 12). It blurs who did what until nobody can say.

  > "I'm glad it's a team effort. The analysis itself was mine, and I'd like that to be clear."

- **"I don't remember it that way."** This is a rewrite of the past (Chapter 12). Let the record speak.

  > "I understand. I've got the email from the 12th with the draft attached. Happy to forward it."

- **"Aren't you being a bit petty about this?"** This is the minimizer (Chapter 12), and also a misleading label (Chapter 23).

  > "I don't think it's petty. Credit decides who gets the next project. I'm just asking for it to be accurate."

**How to close it.**

> "Thanks for hearing me out. I think we'll work better together if we're both clear on who did what."

Afterward, send a short email to the relevant people summarizing your contribution to the project. Keep it factual and friendly. That email is your record if it happens again (Chapter 25).

**What not to do.**

- Don't stay silent and replay it at 3 a.m. A short, calm comment at the time is worth more than a perfect speech you never give.
- Don't complain about the person to the rest of the team. It makes you look like the problem.
- Don't wait for your manager to notice on their own. They usually won't.

### 3. Telling your manager you're at capacity

**When to use it.** Your workload has grown steadily, with new projects and extra responsibilities, and nothing has come off your list. You're working evenings, quality is starting to slip, and another request just landed. You want one honest conversation about what's realistic.

**What's really at stake.** Your health, your evenings, and your reputation for good work. People who never say "I'm full" get handed more, because they've shown they'll absorb it. Eventually something gives, and it's usually the quality of the work, which hurts you more than an honest conversation ever would.

**Before you start.** Write down everything currently on your plate, including the small recurring tasks nobody sees. Seeing the whole list on paper makes the problem obvious without any argument. Then decide what you'd most like to drop, hand over, or delay.

**Your opening line.**

> "I'd like to talk about my workload before it starts affecting the quality of what I deliver. Can we look at my current projects together?"

**Three moves.**

1. **Show the full list.** Let the list do the talking.

   > "Here's everything I'm responsible for right now. The last three items were added in the past two months, and nothing came off."

2. **Ask them to set the priorities.** It's their job to decide what matters most. A how or what question (Chapter 21) hands that decision back to them.

   > "If the new project is the top priority, which of these would you like me to pause or hand to someone else?"

3. **Offer a realistic plan.** Show that you're solving the problem, not just raising it.

   > "I can take on the new project if the monthly reporting moves to someone else. Or I can keep the reporting and start the new project in March. Which works better for you?"

**If they push back.**

- **"Everyone's stretched right now. We all need to pitch in."** This is the character test (Chapter 11). It turns a workload question into a question about whether you're a team player.

  > "I'm happy to pitch in. That's why I want to be clear about what I can do well. If I take everything, some of it won't get done properly."

- **"It's only for a few weeks, until things calm down."** This is "temporary" that never ends (Chapter 15). Put a date on it.

  > "I can stretch for a few weeks. Let's say until the end of next month, and then we look at it again together."

- **"You're so good at this. That's why I gave it to you."** This is flattery before the ask (Chapter 14). Accept the compliment, then answer the question on its merits.

  > "Thank you, I appreciate that. I still can't do all of it well at once."

**How to close it.**

> "Great. So the new project is the priority, and the reporting moves to Jordan from next week. I'll confirm that by email."

Send the confirmation that day. Then put a date in the calendar to check how it's going (Chapter 25). If the old work quietly comes back, raise it the first time it happens, not the fifth.

**What not to do.**

- Don't apologize for having limits. "I'm so sorry, I know everyone's busy" invites them to push.
- Don't say yes in the hallway and complain later. Ask for time: "Let me look at my week and come back to you this afternoon."
- Don't wait until you're exhausted. The calmer you are, the better this goes.

### 4. Quitting your job

**When to use it.** You've decided to leave. You have a plan, a new job, or simply a date, and now you need to tell your manager.

**What's really at stake.** Your reputation and your relationships. Industries are smaller than they look, and the way you leave is often what people remember. A clean, warm exit keeps doors open. A drawn-out, apologetic one invites them to talk you out of a decision you've already made.

**Before you start.** Be sure of your decision and your last day before you walk in. Draft a simple handover plan: what you're working on, who could take it, and what you'll finish before you go.

**Your opening line.**

> "I wanted you to hear this from me first. I've decided to resign. My last day will be [date]. I want to use the time between now and then to make the handover as smooth as I can."

**Three moves.**

1. **Say the decision in the first sentence or two.** Don't bury it under small talk. The waiting is harder on both of you than the news.

2. **Thank them for one real thing.** Make it specific and true.

   > "I want to thank you for putting me on the Henderson account last year. I learned more from that than from anything else here."

3. **Offer the handover plan.** It shows respect, and it moves the conversation from "why" to "how."

   > "I've put together a list of my current projects and who I think could pick each one up. I'd like to go through it with you this week."

**If they push back.**

- **"What would it take to keep you?"** This is a fair question, not a tactic. Answer it honestly and briefly.

  > "I appreciate that. I've thought about this carefully, and I'm not looking for a counteroffer. I'd like to focus on the handover."

- **"After everything we've invested in you, you're leaving us in the lurch?"** This is the ledger (Chapter 11). It turns past support into a debt.

  > "You've invested a lot in me, and I'm grateful. That's why I'm giving proper notice and a full handover plan."

- **"Can you at least stay another two months, until we hire someone?"** This can be the start of scope creep (Chapter 15). Decide in advance what you're willing to offer, and stick to it.

  > "I can't extend my last day. Before I go, I can write up everything the new person needs and spend time with whoever covers in the meantime."

**How to close it.**

> "Thank you for taking it so well. I'll send you my resignation in writing today, along with the handover plan."

Send a short written resignation the same day with your last day clearly stated. Agree on the handover in writing too (Chapter 25), so nothing gets added later.

**What not to do.**

- Don't over-explain or apologize. A short, warm message is kinder than a long, guilty one.
- You don't have to say where you're going or why. "I'm not sharing the details yet" is a complete answer.
- Don't use your exit to air every complaint you've collected. If there's useful feedback, give it calmly, briefly, and only if asked.

## Family

### 5. A parent who guilt-trips you

**When to use it.** Whenever you say no, set a limit, or make a choice your parent doesn't like, they reach for guilt: the sigh, the "after everything I did for you," the silence, the call to your sibling about how hurt they are. You've decided to talk about the pattern itself, once, calmly, instead of fighting the same battle every time it comes up.

**What's really at stake.** Your relationship with your parent, and your right to make your own adult choices without paying for them in guilt. If nothing changes, you'll keep saying yes to things you don't want, and resentment will slowly replace the closeness you're trying to protect.

**Before you start.** Pick a calm moment, not right after an argument or on a holiday. Choose one or two recent examples. Decide what you're asking for. Not "stop making me feel guilty," which they can't do on command, but something specific, like "accept a no without bringing up the past."

**Your opening line.**

> "Mom, I love you, and I want us to be close. There's something that keeps getting in the way, and I'd like to talk about it while we're both calm."

**Three moves.**

1. **Name the pattern, with one example.** Stick to what happened.

   > "When I said I couldn't host Thanksgiving, you said you'd sacrificed everything for me and I couldn't even do that. I felt terrible for days."

2. **Acknowledge what's true for them.** Guilt trips often come from fear of being left out, of losing you, of not mattering (Chapter 19). Naming that honestly lowers the temperature.

   > "I think when I say no, it might feel like I'm pulling away from you. I'm not. I just can't say yes to everything."

3. **Ask for the specific change.** Make it clear and small.

   > "When I say no to something, I'd like you to tell me you're disappointed if you are, and leave it there. I'd rather hear that than hear about everything you've done for me."

**If they push back.**

- **"After everything I gave up for you."** This is the ledger (Chapter 11). Separate gratitude from the decision.

  > "I know how much you gave, and I'm grateful every day. Being grateful doesn't mean I can say yes to everything."

- **"I never try to make you feel guilty. You're too sensitive."** This is flat denial combined with the minimizer (Chapter 12). You don't need to win the argument about what happened.

  > "Maybe you don't mean to. It still lands that way for me, and I'm asking for something different from here on."

- **Tears, a long silence, or "Fine. I won't bother you anymore."** This is visible suffering (Chapter 11), and sometimes the start of withdrawal (Chapter 13). Acknowledge the feeling, and don't take back the request.

  > "I can see this is upsetting. I'm not going anywhere. I'll call you on Sunday, like always."

**How to close it.**

> "Thank you for listening, even though it wasn't easy. I love you. I'll talk to you Sunday."

The real test comes the next time you say no. When the old pattern returns, refer back to this conversation briefly and calmly (Chapter 25): "Remember what we talked about? I'd like to just hear that you're disappointed." Then hold your answer.

**What not to do.**

- Don't list every guilt trip from the past twenty years. One example is enough.
- Don't start defending your own record ("I do plenty for you"). That turns the talk into a contest over who owes whom.
- Don't expect it to change after one conversation. Expect to repeat it calmly a few times.

### 6. Sharing care of an aging parent with siblings

**When to use it.** Your parent needs more help: appointments, bills, groceries, rides, visits. Most of it has landed on you, often because you live closest, are seen as "the responsible one," or simply started doing it first. You want a family conversation that shares the load more fairly.

**What's really at stake.** Your health, your time, your own family, and your relationship with your siblings. Carrying a parent's care alone wears people down, and resentment between siblings can last long after the parent is gone. A clear, fair plan protects everyone, including your parent.

**Before you start.** Write down every task involved, big and small: appointments, medications, bills, insurance calls, groceries, house repairs, visits, emergency calls. Note who does each one now. If there's a sibling likely to be sympathetic, talk to them first and ask for their support in the bigger conversation (Chapter 24).

**Your opening line.**

> "I'd like us to talk about how we're all helping Dad. Right now most of it is falling to me, and I don't think that's working for any of us in the long run."

**Three moves.**

1. **Show the full list.** Let everyone see the whole picture.

   > "Here's everything that goes into looking after Dad right now. I've marked who's doing what. Most of it is in my column."

2. **Say the unspoken rule out loud.** Often the arrangement rests on an assumption nobody ever agreed to (Chapter 23).

   > "I think we've all assumed that because I live twenty minutes away, I'm in charge. I don't think we ever decided that together."

3. **Ask each person what they'll take.** Let them choose, but make sure the outcome is specific. People who live far away can still handle a lot: bills, insurance calls, online orders, research, regular phone calls, and paying for services.

   > "What could each of you take on? Even from a distance, someone could take over the bills and the insurance calls."

**If they push back.**

- **"But you live closest."** This is the exposed assumption in action (Chapter 23). Agree with the fact and question the conclusion.

  > "I do, and I'll keep doing the in-person things. Living closest doesn't mean I should do everything."

- **"Dad prefers you. You're the only one he trusts with this."** This is flattery before the ask (Chapter 14).

  > "That means a lot. It also means he'd be fine with help from the rest of you if we set it up together."

- **"Everyone thinks you're handling it fine."** This is "everyone agrees with me" (Chapter 12). It borrows the weight of a group to close the question.

  > "I'm glad it looks that way from the outside. I'm telling you it isn't sustainable, and I'd like us to change it."

**How to close it.**

> "Thank you, all of you. So Sam has the bills and insurance, Alex calls Dad twice a week and handles the pharmacy orders, and I keep the appointments and the Saturday visits. I'll put it in the family chat so we all have it."

Write the plan down and share it the same day (Chapter 25). Agree on a date to review it, perhaps in three months, because care needs change. If tasks start drifting back to you, raise it early and refer to the written plan.

**What not to do.**

- Don't let the conversation turn into old childhood grievances. Keep coming back to the list.
- Don't do the tasks you handed over when someone forgets. Remind them once. Covering for them quietly puts you back where you started.
- Don't wait for a crisis. The best time for this conversation is before you burn out.

### 7. Setting a boundary with your in-laws

**When to use it.** Your partner's parents regularly cross a line: dropping in unannounced, criticizing your parenting, making holiday plans for you, commenting on your home or your choices. You and your partner have agreed something needs to change, and it's time for the conversation.

**What's really at stake.** Your peace at home, your marriage or partnership, and a workable long-term relationship with your in-laws. Left alone, the friction usually grows, and your partner ends up caught in the middle. Handled well, one conversation can set terms everyone can live with for years.

**Before you start.** Agree with your partner on exactly what you're asking for and who will say it. Often it's best if your partner leads with their own parents and you support. Decide on one or two boundaries, not a list of ten.

**Your opening line.** From your partner, with you present:

> "Mom, Dad, we love having you in our lives and in the kids' lives. We've talked about a couple of things that would make visits easier for all of us, and we wanted to share them together."

**Three moves.**

1. **Make it clear you're a united front.** Use "we," not "she" or "he."

   > "This is something we've decided together."

2. **State the boundary clearly, with what you do want.** Give them something to say yes to.

   > "We'd like you to call before coming over, instead of dropping in. We'd love to see you most Sundays. Let's plan those ahead."

3. **Say what the conversation is not about.** It helps people listen when they don't feel accused (Chapter 23).

   > "This isn't about not wanting you around. We do. It's about having a bit more predictability with the kids' schedules."

**If they push back.**

- **"We're just trying to help."** This is usually true, and it's not really a tactic. Take the good intent and keep the boundary.

  > "We know, and we're grateful. This is how help would work best for us."

- **"So we're not welcome anymore? We hardly see the grandchildren as it is."** This is visible suffering (Chapter 11), sometimes mixed with the ledger.

  > "You're very welcome. We want you to see the kids. We're asking to plan it, not to stop it."

- **"This must be your idea. He never minded before."** This is the blame shift (Chapter 12). It tries to split you and your partner.

  > Your partner: "It's our idea, together. I should have said something sooner."

**How to close it.**

> "Thank you for hearing us. Let's put this Sunday's lunch in the calendar now."

Agreeing on the next visit right away turns the boundary into a plan (Chapter 25). The first time the boundary is tested, your partner restates it calmly and briefly. Consistency matters more than the first conversation.

**What not to do.**

- Don't raise it in front of other relatives or at a holiday meal. Public settings make people defensive (Chapter 16).
- Don't let your partner leave you to deliver it alone if they're not on board. Have that conversation with your partner first.
- Don't argue about every past incident. Focus on what happens from now on.

### 8. Reconnecting with estranged family

**When to use it.** You've been out of contact with a parent, sibling, or adult child for months or years. Something has shifted: time, a loss, a life change, or simply missing them. You'd like to reopen the door, carefully.

**What's really at stake.** A relationship that matters to you, and your own peace either way. A gentle, low-pressure first step can begin to rebuild trust. A heavy, rushed one can close the door harder. Either way, reaching out honestly lets you know you tried.

**Before you start.** Be honest with yourself about why the estrangement happened. If it was because of abuse, threats, or behavior that made you unsafe, you don't owe anyone reconnection, and it's worth talking it through with a counselor first. If it was a falling-out, a drift, or a fight that hardened over time, decide what you're hoping for. Usually it's just to be in touch again, not to settle the past in one go.

**Your opening line.** Often a short letter, message, or a brief word at a family event:

> "I've been thinking about you, and I miss you. I'd like to be back in touch, at whatever pace feels right for you. There's no pressure to reply."

**Three moves.**

1. **Keep the first contact short and light.** Don't open with the history. The point of the first conversation is to show that ordinary talk between you is possible again.

   > "How have you been? I heard you moved. How are you finding it?"

2. **Take responsibility for your part, briefly, if there is one.** An honest admission (Chapter 21) can lower defenses. Keep it short and don't demand one in return.

   > "I know I said some things back then that I regret. I'm sorry for that."

3. **Suggest one small next step.** Make it specific and easy to say no to.

   > "Would you be open to a phone call sometime next month? Just to catch up."

**If they push back.**

- **"What do you want from me?"** This is a fair question, not a tactic. Answer it honestly.

  > "Nothing complicated. I just don't want us to be strangers."

- **"You're the one who walked away. Don't act like this is my fault."** This may be a rewrite of the past (Chapter 12), or it may be how they truly remember it. Don't argue the history now.

  > "We probably remember it differently. I'm not here to argue about it. I'd just like to start talking again."

- **No reply, or "I'm not ready."** Silence can be withdrawal (Chapter 13), or it can simply be someone who needs time. Respect it either way.

  > "That's okay. The door's open whenever you'd like. Take care of yourself."

**How to close it.**

> "It was good to hear your voice. Let's talk again next month, if that works for you."

If they agree to a next step, confirm it gently (Chapter 25): "Looking forward to talking on the 14th." Then let the relationship grow at its own pace. Small, steady contact rebuilds more trust than one big emotional conversation.

**What not to do.**

- Don't try to resolve the whole history in the first conversation. Earn a second conversation first.
- Don't keep reaching out if they've clearly said no. Say the door is open, once, and then respect their answer.
- Don't reconnect at the cost of your own safety. If old patterns of harm return, you can step back again.

## Relationships and Friendships

### 9. The apology

**When to use it.** You hurt someone you care about, whether a partner, friend, or family member. Maybe you already said sorry, but it came out as a defense. Now you want to make a real apology.

**What's really at stake.** Trust, and the relationship itself. Most failed apologies are defenses in disguise: "I'm sorry you felt that way," or "I'm sorry, but you have to understand..." A real apology costs you your pride for a moment, and in return it gives the relationship a chance to heal.

**Before you start.** Get clear on exactly what you did and how it affected them. Not what you meant, but what happened from their side. Decide on one specific thing you'll do differently. And check yourself: are you apologizing because you were wrong, or just to end the discomfort? Only the first one works.

**Your opening line.**

> "I owe you a proper apology. Not the one I gave you the other day. Can we sit down for a few minutes?"

**Three moves.**

1. **Name what you did, specifically.** No "if," no "but," no explaining.

   > "When you told me work was overwhelming you, I said you were overreacting. You weren't, and I shouldn't have said it."

2. **Name how it may have landed for them, and check.** Show you understand the effect, not just the act. Then let them correct you (Chapter 21).

   > "I think you felt alone with it, like I didn't take you seriously. Is that close?"

3. **Say what you'll do differently.** One specific, realistic thing. No grand promises.

   > "When you tell me something's hard, I'm going to listen first and ask what you need, before I say anything else."

**If they push back.**

- **"You always say this, and nothing changes."** This is a fair reaction, not a tactic. Agree with what's true (Chapter 21).

  > "You're right, and I understand why you'd say that. That's why I'm only promising one specific thing. Let me show you I can do it."

- **"I don't know if I can trust that."** Also a fair reaction. Don't push for forgiveness.

  > "You don't have to decide now. I'd rather show you over the next few weeks than try to convince you tonight."

- **"And while we're at it, what about last year, when you..."** This can become a running ledger of past wrongs (Chapter 11). It may also be real hurt that needs its own conversation.

  > "That matters too, and I'm willing to talk about it. Tonight I'd like to get this one right."

**How to close it.**

> "Thank you for hearing me out. I meant what I said, and I'd like you to see it in what I do."

Then do the thing you promised, starting this week. That's what makes an apology stick (Chapter 25). If you slip, own it quickly: "I did it again. I'm sorry. Let me try that again."

**What not to do.**

- Don't use the word "but." Everything before it disappears.
- Don't ask them to reassure you, or expect forgiveness on your schedule.
- Don't apologize for things you didn't do just to keep the peace. A real apology is for real wrongs.

### 10. Ending a recurring fight

**When to use it.** You and your partner, a close friend, or a family member keep having the same argument. The topic changes (dishes, money, being late, his mother), but the fight feels the same every time. You want to talk about the pattern instead of having round twelve.

**What's really at stake.** Every unresolved fight teaches both of you that some problems can't be fixed in this relationship, and over time, people stop raising things at all. Getting to the real issue underneath can end a fight that the surface topic never will.

**Before you start.** Pick a calm time, not during or right after the fight. Think about what the argument might really be about for you: feeling unappreciated, feeling controlled, feeling that your effort doesn't count. Be ready to share that honestly.

**Your opening line.**

> "I've been thinking about the argument we keep having. I don't want to have it again. Could we try talking about it differently, for fifteen minutes?"

**Three moves.**

1. **Name the pattern without blaming anyone.** Make it a problem you share, not something they do (Chapter 23).

   > "I think we've had this argument several times now, about different things. I wonder if we're both upset about something underneath."

2. **Go first with your real issue.** Be honest about what it touches in you. It invites them to do the same.

   > "For me, I think it's that when the dishes pile up, I feel like the work I do around the house doesn't count. Does that make sense?"

3. **Listen to theirs, and reflect it back before responding.** Show you heard them (Chapter 21).

   > "So for you, it's less about the dishes and more about feeling criticized as soon as you walk in the door. I didn't see that."

**If they push back.**

- **"Don't try to analyze me."** This may be fair. Some people find this kind of talk uncomfortable. Drop the method and keep the goal.

  > "Fair enough. I'm not trying to. I'm just tired of this fight, and if you've got a better way to end it, I'm in."

- **"You started it. You're the one who always gets upset."** This is the blame shift (Chapter 12). Don't defend yourself. Return to the shared problem.

  > "Maybe I do. I'm not trying to figure out whose fault it is. I'd like us both to feel better about this."

- **They go quiet and leave the room.** This may be withdrawal (Chapter 13), or they may simply need a break.

  > "Okay. I can tell this is hard. When you're ready, I'd still like to talk about it."

**How to close it.**

> "So I'll try saying thank you when you handle things, and you'll try giving me a few minutes before bringing up the house. Let's check in on Sunday and see if it felt different."

The check-in is what makes it last (Chapter 25). Keep it light: "How's it been this week?" If the old fight starts again, name it gently: "I think we're in the old argument again. Can we pause?"

**What not to do.**

- Don't bring up past examples ("last Tuesday you..."). That restarts the fight you're trying to end.
- Don't try to win. The goal is to understand each other, not to be proved right.
- Don't expect one conversation to fix years of habit. Expect to repeat it a few times.

### 11. Asking for space

**When to use it.** You need some room in a relationship: less time together, fewer daily calls, a short break to think, recover, or deal with something of your own. You're not ending the relationship. You want to ask for space without it sounding like rejection.

**What's really at stake.** The relationship, and your own wellbeing. Asked badly, "I need space" can sound like the start of a breakup and set off a panic that makes things worse. Asked clearly and honestly, it can protect a relationship you care about.

**Before you start.** Be honest with yourself: is this truly a pause, or a slow way of leaving? If it's a way of leaving, use the next playbook instead. Asking for space when you've already decided to go isn't fair to them. If it's genuinely a pause, decide what space means: how long, how much contact, and when you'll talk again.

**Your opening line.**

> "I want to ask you for something, and I'll tell you up front: I'm not ending things. I'd like to explain what I need, and then I really want to hear how it sounds to you."

**Three moves.**

1. **Be specific about what you're asking for.** Vague requests create fear.

   > "I'd like two weeks where we don't see each other every day. I'd still like to text and talk on the phone a few times a week."

2. **Name the fear they may be feeling, honestly.** Then explain why it's not what this is (Chapter 21).

   > "You might be hearing this as me pulling away. It isn't. I've been running on empty, and I want to come back to us with more to give."

3. **Set a clear date to reconnect.** An end date is what makes it a pause instead of an ending.

   > "Two weeks from Saturday, let's have dinner and talk about how it went for both of us."

**If they push back.**

- **"If you need space from me, maybe we should just break up."** This may come from hurt, or it may be used as an ultimatum (Chapter 16). Either way, don't accept the false choice.

  > "I don't want that. I'm asking for two weeks, not an ending. If you need to think about what it means for you, I understand."

- **"Is there someone else?"** This is a fair fear, not a tactic. Answer honestly and directly.

  > "No. This is about me needing to rest and think, not about anyone else."

- **"Fine. Do whatever you want."** Then coldness. This may be going cold (Chapter 13).

  > "I can tell this hurts, and I'm sorry it does. I'll text you tomorrow, and I'll see you on the 20th."

**How to close it.**

> "Thank you for listening. Let's put dinner on the 20th in the calendar now, so we both know when we'll talk."

Then keep your word on contact during the break, and show up on the date you agreed (Chapter 25). Keeping small promises during the pause is what rebuilds trust.

**What not to do.**

- Don't leave it open-ended. "I need some time" with no end date feels like being left.
- Don't promise more than you're sure of. If you're not certain what you'll feel after, say so honestly.
- Don't use space as a quiet punishment for a fight. That's withdrawal, and Chapter 26 explains why it crosses the line.

### 12. Ending a relationship

**When to use it.** You've decided a relationship needs to end. It might be a partner, a marriage, or a long friendship that has run its course. You've thought about it, maybe for a long time, and you're sure.

**What's really at stake.** How the other person heals, and how you'll feel about yourself afterward. Ending something kindly and clearly is one of the hardest things a people-pleaser can do, because every instinct says to soften it, delay it, or make it their idea. But a clear ending delivered with care is kinder than a slow, confusing one.

**Before you start.** Make sure you've decided, not just that you're angry today. Plan where and when: somewhere private where they can react, with time afterward, and not on a birthday or before a big event. Plan the practical next step, such as where you'll stay tonight. **If you have any reason to fear how they'll react, don't do this alone in private.** Tell someone you trust, choose a safer setting, and contact the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788) for help planning it.

**Your opening line.**

> "I need to talk to you about us. This is hard to say. I've thought about it for a long time, and I don't think we should stay together."

**Three moves.**

1. **Say the decision in the first minute.** Don't build up to it. Long preambles make it worse.

2. **Give one honest reason, not a list.** A list invites them to argue each point.

   > "For me, we want different lives. I've hoped that would change, and I don't think it will."

3. **Stay kind and stay clear.** When they argue, you don't need new reasons. Repeat your decision calmly.

   > "I hear you. I'm not going to change my mind. I'm sorry."

**If they push back.**

- **"Let's try counseling first."** This is a fair request, not a tactic. Answer honestly.

  > "I've thought about that. I'm past the point where I think it would help, and I don't want to put us both through months of trying when I already know."

- **"Tell me what I did. I'll change."** Also fair, and painful to hear. Don't offer a list.

  > "This isn't about one thing you did wrong. It's that I know this isn't the right life for me. I'm sorry."

- **"I can't live without you." / "You'll regret this."** This can be visible suffering (Chapter 11) or an ultimatum (Chapter 16). Stay kind and don't take the decision back. If they threaten to hurt themselves, take it seriously: in the US, call or text 988 (the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline), or 911 if they're in immediate danger. Their safety matters. It still doesn't mean you have to stay.

  > "I know this hurts terribly. I care about you, and I'm still not going to change my mind. Is there someone I can call to be with you tonight?"

**How to close it.**

> "I'm going to stay at my sister's tonight. Let's talk about the practical things in a few days, when we've both had some time. I'm not going to disappear on you."

Afterward, keep contact about practical matters short and clear, and confirm any arrangements in writing (Chapter 25). If you share children, a home, or finances, get advice specific to your situation.

**What not to do.**

- Don't soften it into something it isn't ("maybe we just need a break") if you've decided.
- Don't do it by text unless there's a safety reason. In person, or at least by phone, shows respect.
- Don't comfort them by hinting you might change your mind. It feels kind in the moment and is cruel later.

### 13. Being broken up with

**When to use it.** Someone you love is ending the relationship. It could be happening right now, or you know the conversation is coming. You can't control their decision. You can control how you carry yourself through it.

**What's really at stake.** Your dignity, and how quickly you heal. Begging, bargaining, or arguing rarely changes someone's mind, and it often leaves you with memories you wish you didn't have. Getting through the conversation with your self-respect intact gives you solid ground to rebuild on.

**Before you start.** If you can see it coming, decide now what you won't do: plead, promise to become someone else, or agree to things you'll regret. Arrange to have a friend you can call afterward.

**Your opening line.** Once they've told you:

> "Okay. I hear you. Can I ask you one or two questions before we finish?"

**Three moves.**

1. **Pause before responding.** Your first impulse may be to argue or plead. Give yourself a few seconds of silence (Chapter 21).

2. **Ask the questions that matter, once.**

   > "Is this definite?"

   > "Is there anything you want me to understand about why?"

   Listen to the answers. Don't debate them.

3. **Say what you need now.** You're allowed to ask for space and time.

   > "I don't agree with this, but I respect that it's your decision. I'm going to need some time and distance to process it."

**If they push back.**

- **"Can we still be friends?"** This is usually sincere, not a tactic. You don't have to decide tonight.

  > "Maybe someday. Not now. I need distance first."

- **"You never really cared about this relationship anyway."** This is a rewrite of the past (Chapter 12), sometimes used to ease the other person's guilt. You don't need to win this argument.

  > "I don't see it that way. I don't need us to agree on it tonight."

- **They keep explaining, wanting you to say they're doing the right thing.** You don't have to give them that. Acknowledging their decision is enough.

  > "I've heard you. I don't have to agree with your reasons to accept your decision."

**How to close it.**

> "I'm going to go now. I need some time without contact. I hope things go well for you."

Then leave, and call the friend you lined up. If there are practical matters to settle (belongings, a lease, shared bills), deal with them later, briefly and in writing (Chapter 25), once you're steadier.

**What not to do.**

- Don't beg, bargain, or promise to change who you are.
- Don't ask to "talk more tomorrow" as a way of keeping the door open. It extends the pain.
- Don't check their social media or reach out late at night in the weeks afterward. Give yourself the distance you asked for.

## Money and Services

### 14. Saying no to lending money to family or a friend

**When to use it.** A relative or friend has asked you to lend money, or has asked again, perhaps while an earlier loan is still unpaid. You've thought it through, and the answer is no. You want to say it clearly without wrecking the relationship.

**What's really at stake.** Your savings, your security, and the relationship itself. Money lent with doubts often turns into resentment on both sides: you feel taken for granted, and they feel watched. Saying no clearly and kindly now usually protects the relationship better than a reluctant yes.

**Before you start.** Decide your answer before the conversation. If you're open to helping in another way, decide what that is, and only offer what you truly mean. If there's an earlier loan still outstanding, decide whether you'll mention it.

**Your opening line.**

> "I've thought about what you asked, and I want to give you a straight answer. I'm not going to lend the money."

**Three moves.**

1. **Give the answer without a long explanation.** Reasons give people something to argue with. "It doesn't work for me" is enough.

   > "It's just not something I can do right now."

2. **Acknowledge what they're going through.** You can care about their problem without taking it on (Chapter 21).

   > "It sounds like things are really stressful at the moment. I'm sorry you're dealing with this."

3. **Offer another kind of help, only if you mean it.** A third option (Chapter 23) can keep the relationship warm without the money.

   > "I can't lend money, but I'm happy to sit down with you this weekend and go through your budget, or help you call the bank about a payment plan."

**If they push back.**

- **"After everything I've done for you."** This is the ledger (Chapter 11). Past kindness is turned into a debt.

  > "I haven't forgotten any of it, and I'm thankful. It doesn't change my answer on the money."

- **"Family helps family." / "I thought you were a better friend than this."** This is the character test (Chapter 11). It turns a money decision into a judgment of who you are.

  > "I care about you a lot. Saying no to a loan isn't the same as not caring."

- **"It's just until next month. I'll pay you back right away."** This can be future faking (Chapter 14), especially if an earlier loan is still outstanding (the unreturned loan, Chapter 15).

  > "I understand. I'm still not able to, especially while the money from last spring is still open."

**How to close it.**

> "I know this isn't what you hoped to hear. I'm still here for you, and my offer to help with the budget stands."

If you offered other help, follow through on it, and put a time on it now (Chapter 25): "Saturday at ten?" If they ask again later, give the same answer in the same calm tone. You don't need a new reason.

**What not to do.**

- Don't invent an excuse ("all my money is tied up"). Excuses invite them to solve the excuse, and they aren't honest.
- Don't lend money you can't afford to lose, just to end the discomfort.
- Don't agree to "think about it" if you've already decided. It only delays the same conversation.

### 15. A dispute with a contractor or service provider

**When to use it.** A contractor, repair service, or other provider has billed far more than the estimate, did work you didn't agree to, didn't finish the job, or did it badly. You want it put right calmly, without either giving in or blowing up.

**What's really at stake.** Your money, and your sense that you can stand up for yourself with people who seem to know more than you. Some providers count on customers paying to avoid an awkward conversation. A calm, specific, documented approach usually gets a fairer result than either silence or anger.

**Before you start.** Gather everything: the original estimate, any written agreements, texts or emails about changes, photos of the work, and the invoice. Mark the specific items you're questioning. Decide what outcome would be fair to both of you, whether that's an adjusted bill, a repair, or a partial refund.

**Your opening line.**

> "Thanks for sending the invoice. I've gone through it, and there's a gap between the estimate and the final bill that I'd like to understand. The electrical work was quoted at $3,200 and it's billed at $5,800. Can you walk me through what changed?"

**Three moves.**

1. **Ask them to explain, then listen fully.** Don't interrupt or argue while they explain. A pause after they finish often brings out more detail, and sometimes a better offer (Chapter 21).

2. **Say what you'll pay and why.** Be fair, specific, and calm.

   > "I want to pay you properly for the work we agreed on. I'm not comfortable paying for extra work that nobody discussed with me before it was done."

3. **Offer two acceptable options.** Giving a choice makes it easier to say yes (Chapter 22).

   > "I'd be happy either to pay the original estimate plus the parts you can show me receipts for, or to talk through a revised figure today. Which works better for you?"

**If they push back.**

- **"That's just what it costs."** This is fog (Chapter 12). It's vague on purpose. Ask for specifics.

  > "I understand costs change. Can you send me a breakdown of the extra hours and materials, so I can see what happened?"

- **"Pay the full amount today, or I'm not finishing the job."** This is an ultimatum (Chapter 16), sometimes paired with fake urgency (Chapter 14).

  > "I won't settle a disputed bill on the spot. I'll pay promptly for the work we agreed on. Send me the breakdown for the rest, and I'll get back to you by Thursday."

- **"None of my other customers have a problem with this."** This is "everyone agrees with me" (Chapter 12).

  > "That may be. I'm only talking about this bill, and I'd like to understand it."

**How to close it.**

> "Thanks for going through it with me. Please email me the revised invoice with the breakdown, and I'll pay once we've both agreed on it."

Get every agreement in writing, even a short email or text confirming the final amount (Chapter 25). Pay only what's been agreed. If extra work is still underway, ask them to pause it until the bill is settled.

**What not to do.**

- Don't get angry or make threats. It shifts attention from the bill to your behavior.
- Don't pay a disputed amount on the spot just to end the discomfort.
- If the amount is large or the provider becomes hostile, don't handle it alone. Get advice from someone qualified, such as your state or local consumer protection office.

---

*When a conversation doesn't match any of these exactly, use the closest one. The shape stays the same: say the main thing early, listen properly, hold your position calmly, and put what you agreed in writing. If you're not sure which tactic you're facing, describe the situation in the Decoder and ask for help planning your opening and your replies.*
