# The Raise and Pay Scripts

*Word-for-word scripts for asking for a raise, negotiating an offer, and getting paid what you're worth*

## How to use these scripts

This is a set of word-for-word scripts for the pay conversations most of us put off for far too long. Asking for a raise. Answering "what are you looking for?" Responding to "it's not in the budget." Negotiating a job offer. Following up when nobody follows up with you.

The scripts are grouped into six parts: opening the talk, naming your number, handling objections, negotiating the offer, follow-up emails, and special cases. You don't need to read them in order. Find the one that fits your situation and read it twenty minutes before the conversation.

Each script has a short note on why it works. Read that part too. If you understand why a line works, you can adjust it when the conversation goes somewhere unexpected. Change the words so they sound like you. Then say it out loud once before you use it.

They work in any kind of job: an office, a hospital, a store, a job site, a school, a government agency, a nonprofit, or a small family business. Where a script needs adjusting for your kind of work, the note says so.

One rule sits under all of them. Everything here is honest. You're not bluffing, inventing offers, or cornering anyone. You're making a clear, fair case and leaving the other person free to say yes or no. That's what makes these scripts safe to use with people you'll keep working alongside.

## Before you ask

Most of a pay conversation is won before it starts. Do these four things first.

**Know your market value.** Find out what people in your role, in your area, are actually paid. Good free sources include government wage data (in the US, the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes pay by job and by region), current job listings that show a pay range, salary surveys from your professional association or trade group, and published pay scales if you work in the public sector or under a union contract. Talking to people in similar jobs helps too. Write down the range you find and where each number came from.

**List your contributions.** Write down what you've done since your last pay change. Be specific. Responsibilities you've added, people you've trained, problems you've fixed, money you've saved or brought in, shifts you've covered, certifications you've earned, compliments from customers or patients. If you've quietly taken on extra work (the "temporary" duty that never went away), put that on the list. Chapter 15 explains how that kind of slow creep happens.

**Pick your number.** Choose the figure you'll ask for, based on your research and your contributions. It should be a number you can explain. Also decide, privately, the lowest number you'd accept. Knowing both keeps you from talking yourself down in the moment, which is the voice from Chapter 4 at work.

**Pick your moment.** Good times to ask: after a strong result, before budgets are set for the next year, during review season, or when your job has clearly grown. Bad times: in passing, in the hallway, or in the middle of a crisis.

## Part 1: Opening the talk

How you start sets the tone for everything after it.

### 1. Asking your boss for a dedicated meeting

**When to use:** You want a real conversation about pay, not a few minutes squeezed into another meeting.

> "I'd like to set up thirty minutes this week to talk about my pay. I'd rather give it its own time than squeeze it into our regular check-in. Does Thursday at two work, or is later in the week better?"

**Why it works:** Asking for a dedicated meeting shows you take the topic seriously. Naming the topic in advance is also fair to your boss, who won't feel ambushed.

### 2. Going to the next level up

**When to use:** Your direct boss has been stalling for months, and you need to talk to their manager, the owner, or HR.

> "I've been trying to move a conversation about my pay forward with [manager] for the past few months, and it hasn't gone anywhere yet. I wanted to come to you directly, and I wanted you to hear it from me first rather than secondhand."

**Why it works:** It names the pattern without blaming your boss. It treats going up a level as a courtesy, not a complaint. If you can, tell your boss beforehand that you're doing it, so nobody feels gone around.

### 3. After you've been passed over

**When to use:** Someone else got the promotion or raise you expected.

> "I'd like to understand the decision so I can plan the next six months. I'm not asking you to reverse it. I'm asking what I'd need to do differently so we're not having this same conversation next time."

**Why it works:** Asking for a reversal rarely works and puts the other person on the defensive. Asking for clear criteria gives you a target you can actually hit, and a record you can point back to later.

### 4. Raising pay during a performance review

**When to use:** You're in a review and pay might not come up on its own.

> "Before we finish the feedback part, I'd like to make sure we talk about pay. I know it's sometimes handled separately, but I'd like to connect them while the details are fresh for both of us."

**Why it works:** It claims time for the topic before the meeting ends. That heads off "we'll talk about that later," which often turns into never.

### 5. Asking when there's no review coming up

**When to use:** There's no review cycle, or the next one is far away, and you don't want to wait.

> "I don't want to wait until review time for this. It's been on my mind for a few months, and I think it deserves a proper thirty minutes with you. Could we book that for next week?"

**Why it works:** Saying it's been on your mind shows this isn't a whim. Asking for a set time shows you expect a real discussion.

### 6. Requesting the meeting by email

**When to use:** You'd like the request in writing, or your boss is hard to catch in person.

> "Hi [Manager], I'd like to schedule thirty minutes with you in the next two weeks to talk about my pay. I've put some thoughts together and would like to go through them with you. Would Tuesday the 12th at 10 work? If not, I'm flexible any time before the 22nd. Thanks, [Your name]"

**Why it works:** It's short, specific, and easy to say yes to. It also leaves a simple written record that you asked, and when.

### 7. When you have another offer

**When to use:** You have a real job offer somewhere else, and you'd rather stay if the pay can be fixed.

> "I want to be upfront with you. I've received an offer from [employer] at [number]. I'm not trying to start a bidding war, and I'm not looking for a reason to leave. I'd like to understand what's possible here before I decide anything."

**Why it works:** Telling them yourself, before they hear it elsewhere, builds trust. Saying you'd rather stay keeps it a conversation, not a threat. Only use this if the offer is real.

### 8. Asking for a promotion, not just a raise

**When to use:** You've grown into a bigger role and want the title and pay to match.

> "I'd like to talk about a promotion today, not just pay. I've been doing the work of a [next role] for about nine months now, and I'd like to make that official. If it isn't the right next step, I'd like to understand what's in the way."

**Why it works:** "I've been doing the work of" is the strongest promotion case there is. You're not asking for something new. You're asking them to recognize what's already true.

## Part 2: Naming your number

How to put a fair number on the table and keep it there.

### 9. Naming your number first

**When to use:** You've done your research and you know what you're asking for.

> "I've thought about this carefully, and the number I think is fair is [specific number]. I'm happy to walk you through how I got there. I wanted to put it on the table so we have something real to talk about."

**Why it works:** The first number in a conversation tends to shape the discussion that follows. When that number is well researched and you can explain it, naming it first is both fair and effective. Offering to explain, rather than over-explaining up front, shows you're confident in it.

### 10. When they ask, "What are you looking for?"

**When to use:** They turn the question back on you before you've named a number.

> "That's a fair question. Based on the work I've been doing and what this role pays in our area, [specific number] feels right to me. I'm open to talking it through."

**Why it works:** Dodging the question can make you seem unsure. A specific number with two clear reasons behind it gives the conversation a solid starting point.

### 11. Sharing an honest range

**When to use:** You've found a range in your research and want to start there before settling on one figure.

> "From what I've found, people in this role in our area are paid between [low end] and [high end]. Given what I've taken on, I believe I belong toward the upper part of that range. I'd like to hear how that fits with what's possible on your side."

**Why it works:** It shows your homework and invites them to share their side, which gives you useful information. Keep the range honest. Quote the real range from your research, not an inflated one.

### 12. Grounding your number in market data

**When to use:** You want the ask to be about facts, not feelings.

> "I looked at government wage data for this job in our area, current job listings with posted pay, and [our association's salary survey / the published pay scale]. The middle of that range is around [number]. I'm not asking to be paid above the market. I'm asking to be paid at it."

**Why it works:** Named sources are hard to brush aside. Asking to be paid "at market" sounds reasonable because it is. Swap in whichever sources fit your field.

### 13. Pointing out what it takes to fill your role

**When to use:** You want to show your value in practical terms, without threatening to leave.

> "I'm not planning to go anywhere. But it's worth being realistic: if this role had to be filled from scratch, it would take time to hire and train someone up to where I am now, and the work would slow down in the meantime. What I'm asking for is a fair price for keeping things running the way they are."

**Why it works:** It moves the conversation from "how much do you cost" to "what do you keep running." Saying first that you're not planning to leave keeps it honest and calm.

### 14. When you don't have a number yet

**When to use:** You know your pay is off, but you haven't settled on a figure.

> "I'll be honest, I don't have an exact number yet. What I do know is that my current pay doesn't reflect what I'm doing now. I'd like us to work out together what the right number is, and I'll come back with my research by [date]."

**Why it works:** Being open about it makes the conversation feel shared rather than tense. Promising to come back with research by a set date keeps it from drifting.

## Part 3: Handling objections

The objections you're most likely to hear, and a calm reply to each.

### 15. "It's not in the budget."

**When to use:** The most common first answer.

> "I understand. Budgets are set months ahead and they're usually tight. Can I ask two things? When is the next budget set? And if we can't change things this time, what can we agree on now so this is already decided when it opens?"

**Why it works:** It doesn't argue with the budget, which is often real. It turns "no money" into a plan with a date. In nonprofits, ask whether pay can be built into the next grant or funding request.

### 16. "Now isn't the right time."

**When to use:** A vague delay with no reason given.

> "Help me understand what the right time would look like. What would need to be true for us to move forward on this?"

**Why it works:** One simple question turns a vague delay into specific conditions. Once you know the conditions, you can work toward them.

### 17. "We need to wait for the review cycle."

**When to use:** They want to push it to the scheduled process.

> "I understand that's the usual process. I have two concerns. That's several months away, and I'd be underpaid for all of that time. And review raises are often a set percentage for everyone. I'd like to talk about the right number for my role now, rather than wait for a standard increase."

**Why it works:** It respects their process while naming its real limits. You're asking for a fair conversation, not special treatment.

### 18. "I don't have the authority to approve that."

**When to use:** Your boss says the decision sits above them.

> "That's fair. Who does make the decision? I'd like to understand how it gets approved, and whether there's anything I can give you to help make the case."

**Why it works:** It doesn't push your boss to say yes when they can't. It maps out who decides and turns your boss into your advocate rather than your obstacle.

### 19. "Others on the team are at the same level."

**When to use:** They say paying you more wouldn't be fair to others.

> "I understand that. Two thoughts. First, I'd like to know how my work compares with others in the same role. I'm not asking for special treatment, just pay that matches what I contribute. Second, if we're all doing the same work and we're all paid below market, maybe the bigger conversation is about pay for the whole team."

**Why it works:** Either your work stands out, which supports your raise, or it doesn't, which points to a team-wide pay problem. Both are fair conversations to have.

### 20. "Your performance doesn't justify that."

**When to use:** They point to your work as the reason for no.

> "I'd like to understand that better. What specifically would have made this a different conversation? I'd like to leave today with a clear list I can work on."

**Why it works:** Defending yourself on the spot rarely helps. Asking for specifics gives you something concrete to work on. If they can't name anything specific, that tells you something too.

### 21. "Let me see what I can do."

**When to use:** A friendly answer with no commitment in it.

> "Thank you, I appreciate that. Could we agree on a date when you'll come back to me with an answer? That way neither of us has to chase it."

**Why it works:** Without a date, "let me see" often goes nowhere. A date gives both of you a clear next step.

### 22. "We have to be fair across the team."

**When to use:** Fairness is offered as the reason for no.

> "I agree completely. To me, fair means people are paid for what they contribute. Could we look at what I've contributed compared with others in my role, and see whether my pay reflects that? If it does, I'll let it go. If it doesn't, I'd like to talk about it."

**Why it works:** You agree with the principle and then apply it honestly. Offering to drop it if the facts don't support you shows you're arguing in good faith.

### 23. "That's above market for this role."

**When to use:** They say your number is too high.

> "Could you share the figures you're looking at? I used [your sources] and I'm seeing a different range. I'm happy to go through mine and compare."

**Why it works:** It invites both sides to put their facts on the table. If their figures are better than yours, you've learned something. If they don't have any, the conversation goes back to yours.

### 24. "You just had a raise."

**When to use:** They point to a recent increase.

> "I appreciated that raise. What's changed is how much my role has grown since then. I've taken on [specific examples]. The raise made sense for the job I was doing then. It doesn't match the job I'm doing now."

**Why it works:** It doesn't argue with the past. It puts the focus on the bigger role, which is the real reason pay needs to move again.

### 25. "We're in a hiring freeze."

**When to use:** They point to wider money problems.

> "I understand the freeze. Can I ask whether it covers pay for current staff, or only new hires? If we can't add people, keeping the people we have seems even more important."

**Why it works:** A hiring freeze and a pay freeze are not always the same thing. A simple clarifying question often shows there's more room than it first seemed.

### 26. "Show me another offer."

**When to use:** They want proof of your value in the form of a competing job offer.

> "I'm not looking to job-hunt just to prove a point. I want to stay here, which is why I'm talking to you. What I can share is the market research I've done. What else would help you make the case?"

**Why it works:** It keeps the focus on your value and your wish to stay, not on a contest. It also offers something useful in place of what they asked for.

### 27. "You're not ready yet."

**When to use:** They say you need more time or experience.

> "Can you help me understand what ready looks like? I'd like a specific list of what I need to show. If I do those things, can we agree that this becomes a yes?"

**Why it works:** It turns a vague "not yet" into clear criteria and gets agreement on them up front. That protects you from the moving goalposts in Chapter 15.

### 28. "Can you wait until next quarter?"

**When to use:** They want to put it off for a few months.

> "I can wait if we agree on the details today. If we can settle the number now and put it in writing to start next quarter, I'm fine with that. If next quarter means starting this conversation over, I'd rather keep working on it now."

**Why it works:** It separates a real commitment with a later start date from a delay that quietly goes nowhere. Either answer tells you where you stand.

### 29. "That's more than I make."

**When to use:** Your boss is underpaid too, and says so.

> "I appreciate you telling me that, and I want to be respectful about it. My number comes from market data and the work I'm doing, not from what anyone else earns. I don't want this to be awkward, but I can't base my ask on what you're paid."

**Why it works:** It's kind about an awkward moment without backing down. Your pay and your boss's pay are separate questions.

## Part 4: Negotiating the offer

What to say once they come back with a number. This is often where the most money moves.

### 30. When they offer less than you asked

**When to use:** They've moved, but not far enough.

> "I appreciate you moving on this. That number doesn't quite get us to where I think it should be. What would it take on your side to get closer to [your number]?"

**Why it works:** It thanks them for the effort and returns calmly to your number. Asking what it would take invites them to help solve the problem rather than defend their offer.

### 31. When they offer exactly what you asked

**When to use:** They said yes to your number.

> "Thank you, I appreciate that. Before we wrap up, are there other parts of the package we should cover while we're here? Title, timing, schedule, anything we haven't touched?"

**Why it works:** Once the main number is settled, it's a natural moment to sort out the rest. Asking openly is fair and saves a second round later.

### 32. When they offer more than you asked

**When to use:** It doesn't happen often, but it does happen.

> "Thank you, that's generous and I appreciate it. Could you help me understand how the number was set? I'd like to know where I fit in the range so I know what to aim for next year."

**Why it works:** A simple thank-you is the right response. Asking how the number was set helps you plan the next conversation.

### 33. Looking beyond base pay

**When to use:** The base pay is capped, but other things may be flexible.

> "If the base rate is tight, could we look at the whole package? Things like a bonus, overtime or shift pay, extra paid time off, a training or certification budget, or help with commuting costs. Those sometimes come from different budgets. What's flexible on your side?"

**Why it works:** Base pay is often fixed while other items have more room. Naming the options makes it easier for the other person to find a yes. Pick the ones that fit your job.

### 34. Asking for training, certification, or a skills raise

**When to use:** Your employer pays more for certain skills or licenses, or you want to grow into a better-paid role.

> "If a raise isn't possible right now, I'd like to ask for support with [certification / license / course]. It would let me take on [specific work], and I'd like us to agree that once I finish it, my pay moves to [number or the next level]."

**Why it works:** It turns "not now" into a path with a clear finish line. In healthcare, the trades, and many public jobs, certifications are tied directly to higher pay.

### 35. Asking for a one-time bonus

**When to use:** The ongoing pay is capped, but a one-time payment might be possible.

> "If the base pay is where it has to be for now, would a one-time bonus be possible? It could be tied to staying through a set date, or to [specific result]. That might work for both of us."

**Why it works:** One-time payments often come from a different budget than ongoing pay. Tying it to staying or to a result gives the employer something too.

### 36. Asking for a title change

**When to use:** You want your title to match the work, whether or not the pay moves today.

> "Separate from pay, I'd like to ask about my title. I've been doing [higher-level] work for [timeframe], and I'd like the title to reflect that. Can we settle that today, or is it a separate process?"

**Why it works:** Titles are often easier to change than pay. A better title also makes your next pay conversation, here or elsewhere, easier.

### 37. Asking for a better schedule or flexible work

**When to use:** Your schedule, shifts, or location matter to you as much as money.

> "There's something besides money I'd like to raise. I'd like to [work from home two days a week / move to day shifts / have a fixed schedule / have every other weekend off]. If the full pay increase isn't possible, this would mean almost as much to me. How much flexibility is there?"

**Why it works:** Putting it in the same conversation means it gets real weight, rather than being treated as a small favor to ask about later.

### 38. Asking for a later start date

**When to use:** You're accepting a new job and need more time before you start.

> "I'll need four weeks before I can start, rather than two. I want to leave my current job properly and take a short break in between. I wanted to make sure that works before we set the date."

**Why it works:** It states the need plainly, gives an honest reason, and checks it works for them. Most employers respect someone who leaves a job properly.

### 39. Getting it in writing

**When to use:** They've agreed out loud, and you want it written down.

> "Thank you for this. Could you send me a short email today or tomorrow with what we agreed, so we both have the details?"

**Why it works:** A written note protects both of you from misremembering. It isn't about distrust. Details get lost when people get busy. Chapter 25 has more on making agreements stick.

## Part 5: Follow-up emails

What to write after the conversation. Short is almost always better.

### 40. Same-day summary

**When to use:** Right after the conversation, while it's fresh.

> "Hi [Manager], thanks for your time today. Just to confirm what we discussed: you'll [action] by [date], and I'll [action]. Let me know if I've missed anything. [Your name]"

**Why it works:** It's short and specific, and it records who agreed to do what. It reads as organized, not suspicious.

### 41. No reply after a few days

**When to use:** They said they'd get back to you, and they haven't yet.

> "Hi [Manager], just following up on our conversation last Tuesday. I know these things take time to work through. I wanted to check where things stand. Happy to talk any time this week. [Your name]"

**Why it works:** It's low pressure and respects their process. It also shows, politely, that you haven't dropped the subject.

### 42. No reply after a week

**When to use:** A full week has gone by.

> "Hi [Manager], it's been a week since we talked and I haven't heard back yet. I wanted to make sure this hasn't slipped through the cracks. What does the timeline look like from here? [Your name]"

**Why it works:** It's a little more direct, but it still gives them an easy explanation (it got busy) rather than accusing them of ignoring you.

### 43. Thank-you after a yes

**When to use:** They agreed.

> "Hi [Manager], thank you for making this happen. I appreciate it. Just to confirm, my pay will move to [number] starting [date]. I'm looking forward to [next project or goal]. [Your name]"

**Why it works:** Thank them sincerely and confirm the details in writing. That's all it needs. A clear record now saves confusion when the first new paycheck arrives.

### 44. After a no, keeping the door open

**When to use:** They said no, and you're staying for now.

> "Hi [Manager], thanks for being straight with me about where things stand. I'd like to keep this conversation open for the next review. In the meantime, I'd like to agree on what you'd want to see from me between now and then, so we both know what we're working toward. Could we find thirty minutes next week to go through that? [Your name]"

**Why it works:** No sulking and no threats. It moves straight to what needs to happen next time, and it puts the goals in writing.

## Part 6: Special cases

Conversations that don't follow the usual pattern.

### 45. A promotion without a raise

**When to use:** You got the new title or responsibilities, but not the pay.

> "Thank you for the promotion. I want to raise something directly. Right now the new role comes with more responsibility but the same pay. I'd like to understand when the pay will catch up, because that matters a lot to me."

**Why it works:** It names the gap clearly and calmly. Asking when, not whether, assumes the pay should follow, which is fair.

### 46. When you're well below market

**When to use:** You've done the research and you know you're underpaid.

> "I want to raise something that's been on my mind. Based on the research I've done, my pay is about [X percent] below what this role pays in our area. I'm not asking to be paid at the top. I'm asking to be brought up to at least the middle of the range. What's the path to get there?"

**Why it works:** A specific, researched figure is hard to dismiss. Asking for the middle of the range is modest and fair. "What's the path" invites them to help.

### 47. When they try to keep you after you've accepted another job

**When to use:** You've told them you're leaving, and now they're offering more.

> "I appreciate this, and I'll be honest with you. If this had been on the table a few months ago, I wouldn't have been looking. I need a couple of days to think it through properly. I'll come back to you by Friday."

**Why it works:** It's honest about the timing without blaming anyone. It gives you time to compare properly, and a set date shows you're taking it seriously.

### 48. When your boss asks, "What would make you stay?"

**When to use:** They ask you directly.

> "I appreciate you asking. I'll be honest: I'd rather not have to be close to leaving before pay gets discussed. So there are two parts. The pay part is [specific number]. And I'd like us to agree on a regular pay review, so we don't end up here again."

**Why it works:** It gives a clear number and fixes the pattern that led here. Many offers to keep someone fix the number but not the pattern.

### 49. Asking for a higher hourly rate

**When to use:** You're paid by the hour, in retail, food service, warehouse work, care work, the trades, or similar.

> "I'd like to talk about my hourly rate. I've been here [time], I'm [training new staff / covering extra shifts / opening and closing / handling the register and the stockroom], and I'd like my rate to reflect that. Based on what similar jobs around here are paying, I'm asking for [rate] an hour. Could we talk about that?"

**Why it works:** It ties the request to specific, visible extras that a manager can see for themselves. Local job listings for similar roles are often the easiest proof of the going rate.

### 50. Union contracts and fixed pay scales

**When to use:** Your pay is set by a union contract or a public pay scale, so the hourly rate or salary for each step can't be negotiated.

> "I know the pay scale is fixed, so I'm not asking to change it. I'd like to ask about the things that can move. Am I on the right step for my experience? Is there a higher classification or title that fits the work I'm doing now? And could I be considered for [a schedule change / training / a lead or acting role / an open position]?"

**Why it works:** Fixed scales still leave real choices: which step you're placed on, how your job is classified, acting or lead pay, schedule, and training. Ask your union representative or HR what's possible under the contract, and check that your step and classification are correct, because mistakes do happen.

### 51. A small business with a hands-on owner

**When to use:** You work closely with the owner, and pay talks feel personal.

> "I really like working here, and I want to keep growing with the business. I'd like to talk about my pay. Over the past year I've [specific contributions], and I'd like to ask for [number]. I know every dollar matters in a business this size, so if the full amount isn't possible right now, could we agree on a step now and the rest by a set date?"

**Why it works:** With small business owners, it's often their own money. Showing you understand that, and offering a step-by-step path, makes a yes easier without giving up your number.

### 52. Freelancers raising rates

**When to use:** You work for yourself and it's time to charge more.

> "I wanted to let you know well in advance that starting [month], my rate for this kind of work will be [new rate]. I've really valued working with you, and I'm glad to keep everything booked before then at the current rate."

**Why it works:** Plenty of notice is fair and gives clients time to plan. Stating the new rate, rather than asking whether it's okay, shows you're running a business. It's also easiest to start new clients at the new rate first.

## One last note

A pay conversation is not a performance. It's a conversation in which you're the only person speaking up for your interests. These scripts are there to help you do that clearly, fairly, and without a fight.

The most common mistake isn't saying the wrong line. It's giving up too early. Staying in the conversation, past the first no, past the vague "let me see what I can do," is often what decides whether anything changes.

You'll forget most of these words in the moment. That's fine. If you remember only three things, remember these: ask a question instead of defending yourself, tie every agreement to a date, and confirm it in writing within a day.
