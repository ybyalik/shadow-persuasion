## Email 3 (Day 2)

**Subject:** The fastest tell to fix
**Preview:** It takes three seconds.

Hi {{first_name}},

Poker players call the small signals that give away their cards "tells."

People who get taken advantage of have tells too. The loudest one is the **instant yes**. Someone asks, and you agree before they finish the sentence.

The speed is the message. It tells people your agreement doesn't need thought, so your time doesn't need consideration. People learn to bring their requests to whoever answers fastest.

Here's a small experiment for tomorrow. Don't agree to anything on the spot. Instead, say:

> "Let me check and get back to you."

You can still say yes later, to everything if you want. You're only changing the speed.

Most people find nobody minds. A few find that some requests quietly disappear once they don't get an instant yes. That tells you something too.

This is Day 1 of the 3-day quick start in the book.

## Email 6 (Day 7)

**Subject:** What to say to a guilt trip
**Preview:** Acknowledge, then hold.

Hi {{first_name}},

Guilt is the most common pressure people like us face. "After everything I've done for you." "I'll just do it myself, like always." "I thought I could count on you."

Each one takes a request you could easily decline and attaches a moral weight to it. Saying no stops being about your schedule and starts being about your character.

The reply has a simple shape: **acknowledge the feeling, then hold the decision.**

> "You've done a lot for me, and I'm grateful. I'm still not able to do this."

> "I know it feels like a lot. I can't help this weekend."

You don't argue about whether they really do everything. You don't defend your record. You just don't let the guilt change the answer.

One test helps: when you feel guilty, ask whether you actually broke a promise or caused harm. If not, the guilt isn't telling you about right and wrong. It's just pressure.

## Email 8 (Day 11)

**Subject:** Don't argue about what happened
**Preview:** You'll rarely win. You don't need to.

Hi {{first_name}},

"That never happened." "You're too sensitive." "It was just a joke." "You agreed to this, don't you remember?"

When someone keeps rewriting the past, the natural instinct is to prove them wrong. Reconstruct exactly what was said, in what order, in what tone.

It almost never works. Someone determined to win that argument can simply keep denying.

So don't fight over the facts. State your experience, and make your decision:

> "I remember it differently. Either way, here's what I need going forward."

> "Maybe it was a joke. It didn't land that way for me, and I'd like you to stop."

And if it happens a lot with one person, write things down after important conversations: the date, what was said, what was agreed. Not to build a case. Just to give yourself something solid to stand on when your own memory starts to feel shaky.

## Email 10 (Day 17)

**Subject:** When someone goes silent on you
**Preview:** Don't chase. Don't retaliate.

Hi {{first_name}},

Some of the strongest pressure involves no words at all. A partner who goes quiet for two days after you disagree. A parent who answers the phone in a flat voice and says "I'm fine." A friend who stops replying after you said no.

Nobody has made a demand. But you know exactly what would make the silence stop: give in, apologize, make it right.

Here's the reply. Name it once, without accusation, and open a door:

> "I can tell you're upset. When you're ready to talk about it, I'm here."

Then carry on with your life. Don't follow them around, send a stream of messages, or apologize for something you didn't do. And don't go silent back. That just turns it into a contest.

Silence tends to last much longer when it's chased, and much shorter when it isn't.

## Email 12 (Day 24)

**Subject:** Waiting to be noticed
**Preview:** The clause that keeps you waiting.

Hi {{first_name}},

One clause of the Good Person Contract keeps people waiting for years: *if you're good enough, you'll be treated fairly.*

Work hard, don't complain, and eventually someone will notice. The raise will come. Your partner will see everything you do. Your family will finally get it.

Sometimes that happens. Often it doesn't, and not because you don't deserve it. People notice what's in front of them, and someone who never asks is very easy to overlook.

Fair treatment is mostly negotiated, not earned in silence. That doesn't mean being pushy. It means making your contribution visible and asking directly:

> "I'd like to set up thirty minutes to talk about my compensation."

> "I'd like us to list everything it takes to run the house and look at who does what."

## Email 13 (Day 28)

**Subject:** Caring without carrying
**Preview:** Warmth with a spine.

Hi {{first_name}},

If you can tell from one text whether someone is upset, if you feel other people's bad news in your own stomach, you probably have a lot of empathy.

It's one of the best things about you. It's also the most exploited, because anyone who wants you to act only has to look unhappy.

The fix isn't to care less. It's to notice the difference between caring *about* someone and *carrying* them. Three questions help:

1. **Whose feeling is this?** Did it start with me, or did I pick it up?
2. **Whose problem is this to solve?**
3. **What would help most?** Ask them, before you start fixing.

And when you do help, give it edges: "I can talk for twenty minutes before I start dinner."

The calmest person in a crisis is rarely the one crying alongside you. They care deeply, and because they're steady, they can actually help.

That's Chapter 5.

A book called *Shadow Persuasion* that teaches influence. It's fair to ask whether it's just teaching manipulation.

Here's the line I draw. Influence is honest, leaves the other person free to say no, and aims for an outcome that's fair to both of you. Manipulation uses deception, makes refusing too painful, or serves one person at the other's expense.

Four quick tests:

- Would I be comfortable if they knew exactly what I was doing?
- Would I think it was fair if someone did it to me?
- Can they say no without being punished?
- Is the outcome fair to both of us?

## Email 15 (Day 36)

**Subject:** How you got here
**Preview:** Nobody agrees to the whole thing.

Hi {{first_name}},

You agree to help a colleague with one report. Six months later, you're doing all her reports. You lend your brother a little money. A year later he owes you thousands. You host one family holiday. Now you host all of them.

Nobody ever agreed to the whole thing. It arrived one reasonable step at a time.

Here's a quick way to spot it. Make a list of the ongoing things you do for other people, at work, in your family, with friends. Next to each, ask:

> If someone proposed this whole arrangement to me today, would I say yes?

If the answer is no, you've found what I call a slow squeeze. The way out is the same as the way in: go back to the original agreement, name the gap, and reset.

> "When you moved in, we said a few weeks. It's been five months. I'd like you to find your own place by the end of next month."

Chapter 15 has the full audit and six versions of the squeeze.

## Email 16 (Day 40)

**Subject:** A code word for your family
**Preview:** Five minutes that could save a lot.

Hi {{first_name}},

This one isn't about the people you know. It's about strangers.

Scammers now use AI to copy the voices of people you love. A call comes from a "grandchild" in trouble, or a "bank" reporting fraud, and you're told to act immediately.

A few rules stop almost all of these:

1. **Hang up and call back** on a number you find yourself.
2. **Never pay with gift cards, wire transfers, or crypto** to someone who contacted you.
3. **Never share codes** sent to your phone.
4. **Treat "don't tell anyone" as a warning sign.**
5. **Slow down.** A real emergency can survive a ten-minute check.

And one more: agree on a family code word that a real relative in trouble would know.

Forward this to someone you love. It takes five minutes.

## Email 18 (Day 50)

**Subject:** Calm is not the same as weak
**Preview:** When someone raises their voice.

Hi {{first_name}},

When someone gets angry, the alarm goes off in your body before your mind can think. Heart racing, voice thin. And the fastest way to make the alarm stop is to give in.

A few things help you stay steady:

- **Breathe out slowly**, longer than you breathe in, two or three times.
- **Lower and slow your own voice.** It calms you, and often them.
- **Have an exit line ready:** "I'm going to step out. We can pick this up tomorrow."
- **Remember you don't have to resolve anything** while someone is shouting.

One important note. If someone has hurt you, threatened you, or makes you afraid, this isn't about better replies. It's about getting safe and getting support. In the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788.

## Email 19 (Day 55)

**Subject:** What usually changes
**Preview:** Not dramatic. Better than dramatic.

Hi {{first_name}},

People often imagine that getting better at this means a big confrontation: finally telling someone exactly what you think.

It rarely looks like that. The changes the reset is built for are quieter, and they last longer.

The instant yes turns into "let me check," and that becomes a habit within a couple of weeks. The guilt after saying no doesn't vanish, but it shrinks from days to minutes. Some people test the new you, usually two or three times, and then adjust. And the 3 a.m. replays come less often, because more of the conversations go the way you meant them to.

Then one day you notice you said no, held it, and forgot about it by lunch.

That's the finish line. Not a dramatic victory. Just a no that no longer costs you anything.
