## Email 2 (Day 1)

**Subject:** The question most people skip
**Preview:** What will you do if the answer is no?

Hi {{first_name}},

If you tried the four questions from Chapter 1, the last one was probably the hardest:

**What will I do if the answer is no?**

Most people skip it. They plan what to say, and they hope. But if you have no plan for a no, you will accept almost anything to avoid hearing one.

You don't need a dramatic answer. "I'll ask someone else." "I'll stop covering the extra shift." "I'll get the estimate in writing before I agree to pay." Any real option you could live with changes how the next conversation feels.

The book calls this the No-Agreement Plan: what you will do if the answer is still no. It's introduced in Chapter 18 and covered in full in Chapter 28.

## Email 3 (Day 2)

**Subject:** The fastest habit to change
**Preview:** It takes about three seconds.

Hi {{first_name}},

The habit that gives away the most is the **instant yes**. Someone asks, and you agree before they've finished the sentence.

The speed sends a message. It tells people your time needs no thought. Over time, requests drift toward whoever answers fastest.

Here's a small experiment for tomorrow. When someone asks for something you'd usually accept without thinking, say:

> "Let me check and get back to you."

You can still say yes later, to everything if you want. You're only changing the speed.

Many people find nobody reacts at all. Some find a request quietly disappears once there's no instant yes. That tells you something too.

## Email 6 (Day 7)

**Subject:** Duty, favor or agreement?
**Preview:** One word before you decide.

Hi {{first_name}},

Guilt is the pressure kind people feel most. "After everything I've done for you." "I'll just do it all myself, like always." "I thought I could count on you."

Real guilt points at something: a broken promise, harm you caused, something unfair. Guilt pressure attaches that same feeling to a fair decision.

Chapter 7 gives you a simple check. The next time you feel that guilty pang about a request, name it in one word:

- **Duty:** it comes from a role or a promise.
- **Favor:** a gift you can decline.
- **Agreement:** something you both said yes to.

Then decide. If it's a favor, you can answer kindly and still say no:

> "You've done a lot for me, and I'm grateful. I still can't do this."

You don't have to argue about whether they really do everything, or defend your record as a good person.

## Email 8 (Day 11)

**Subject:** Don't argue about what happened
**Preview:** You don't need to win it.

Hi {{first_name}},

"That never happened." "You're too sensitive." "We agreed you'd handle it."

When someone keeps changing the story, the natural instinct is to prove them wrong: rebuild exactly what was said, in what order, in what tone.

Someone who wants to win that argument can simply keep denying. So you don't need to win it to make a clear decision about what happens next:

> "I remember it differently. Let's agree on what happens from here."

And a small habit from Chapter 8 helps a lot. After a plan you make by phone or in person, send a one-line message confirming it, and ask them to reply. Keep it somewhere only you can see. It gives you something solid to stand on when your memory starts to feel shaky.

## Email 10 (Day 17)

**Subject:** When someone goes quiet on you
**Preview:** Don't chase. Don't go quiet back.

Hi {{first_name}},

Some of the strongest pressure has no words at all. A partner who goes quiet for two days after you disagree. A friend who stops replying after you said no. "I'm fine," in a voice that says otherwise.

Healthy space is temporary and leads back to talking. Silence used as pressure tends to start after a no and end when you give in.

The reply is short. Say it once, without accusation:

> "I'm here when you want to talk."

Then do one ordinary thing you had already planned. Notice the urge to fix it, and let the urge pass. Don't send a stream of messages, and don't go silent back.

## Email 13 (Day 28)

**Subject:** Say what you want, then leave room
**Preview:** The difference between an ask and a demand.

Hi {{first_name}},

Kind people often ask for things in a way that makes a no almost certain. A long run-up. Three apologies. A question so vague the other person can't tell what you want.

The book's tool for this is the Clean Ask: say what you want, give the relevant reason, and leave room for a real answer.

> "I'd like us to split the hosting this year. I can't take on all of it again. Which part would you be willing to take?"

One true reason is usually enough. Your own needs count as a reason.

Leaving room matters. If you'd punish a no, you're making a demand, not an ask. And if they do say no, that's information: for a better ask, for someone else to ask, or for your No-Agreement Plan.

That's Chapter 17, Ask for More and Negotiate Well.

## Email 15 (Day 36)

**Subject:** How you got here
**Preview:** Nobody agreed to the whole thing.

Hi {{first_name}},

You agree to help a colleague with one report. Months later, you're doing all of them. You lend a friend a little money. Then a little more. You host one family holiday. Now it's "your" holiday.

Nobody agreed to the whole thing. It arrived one reasonable step at a time. Chapter 10 calls it the slow squeeze.

Here's a quick check. List the ongoing things you do for other people. Next to each one, ask:

> If someone proposed this whole arrangement to me today, would I say yes?

Where the answer is no, write down what you first agreed to and what it is now. Then raise it plainly:

> "This started as a temporary arrangement. Let's talk about what makes sense from here."

Anything "temporary" can have an end date.

## Email 16 (Day 40)

**Subject:** When it's a scam, not a sale
**Preview:** Four rules that stop most of them.

Hi {{first_name}},

This one isn't about the people you know. It's about strangers.

Scams use the same pressure as a pushy salesperson, with the honesty removed: a call from "your bank", a relative in trouble who needs money now, an online romance that suddenly hits a crisis.

A few rules from Chapter 24 stop most of them:

1. **Hang up and call back** on a number you find yourself, like the one on the back of your card.
2. **Never pay with gift cards, wire transfers or cryptocurrency** to someone who contacted you.
3. **Never share a code** sent to your phone.
4. **Treat "don't tell anyone" as a warning sign.** Tell someone.

If you've been caught, don't be embarrassed. Call your bank right away. In the US you can also report it to the Federal Trade Commission at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

Forward this to someone you love. It takes a minute to read.

Nate

## Email 18 (Day 50)

**Subject:** When a better line is not the answer
**Preview:** A hard conversation and a dangerous one are different.

Hi {{first_name}},

Most of what I've sent you is about ordinary pressure: guilt, a sulk, a pushy request. In those situations, a calm reply helps, because you can say no and still be safe.

Some situations are different. If someone threatens you, frightens you, checks your phone, tracks where you go, or controls your money, a better line is not the answer. No phrase makes an unsafe person safe, and confronting them is not a safety plan.

What helps is real support from people trained for it. In the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is open at any hour at 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788. If someone may check your phone or computer, use a device they can't access. If you're in immediate danger, call 911.

## Email 19 (Day 55)

**Subject:** What to do when they still say no
**Preview:** Their answer and your decision are different things.

Hi {{first_name}},

Sometimes you do everything well. You pause, you ask clearly, you stay calm. And they still say no.

Chapter 28 makes a distinction that helps: their answer is theirs, and your decision is yours. A no tells you where they stand. It doesn't decide what you do next.

The book lays out four paths, each with a cost:

- **Accept it,** and truly stop waiting.
- **Escalate,** if someone has the power to act.
- **Step back,** so less of their behavior reaches you.
- **Leave,** which is usually slow and needs a plan.

None of them is right for everyone. The point is to choose one on purpose, instead of drifting. That choice, written down ahead of time, is your No-Agreement Plan.
