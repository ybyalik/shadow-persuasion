# Chapter 15: The Slow Squeeze

The tactics in this chapter are the hardest to see, because no single moment looks like a tactic at all.

You agree to help a colleague with one report. Six months later, you're doing all of her reports. You lend your brother a small amount of money. A year later, he owes you several thousand dollars and treats each new request as normal. You agree to host one family holiday. Now you host every holiday, and nobody remembers it was ever a choice. You take on a "temporary" responsibility at work while someone is on leave. They came back eight months ago. You still have it.

None of these began with anything unreasonable. Each step was small, and each step built on the last. That's the slow squeeze. It doesn't take much from you at once. It takes a little more, then a little more, until the new normal is something you'd never have agreed to if it had been proposed all at once.

The replies in this chapter share one idea. **Go back to the original agreement, name the gap, and reset.**

## Moving goalposts

**What it sounds like.**

- "This is great, but could you also..."
- "Now that you've finished that, the real priority is..."
- "That's not quite what I meant. I was hoping for more like..."
- "If you can hit this number, we'll talk about the raise." Then, once you hit it: "Let's see if you can keep it up for another quarter."

**Where you'll meet it.** At work, constantly, with managers and clients. In families, with parents whose approval always depends on one more achievement. With partners who say they'll be happy once you change one more thing.

**What it's for.** Moving goalposts keep you working while the reward stays out of reach. Each time you meet the standard, a new one appears. You never get to the point where you've done enough.

**The calm reply.** Name the original agreement, and ask whether it's changed.

> "When we talked in March, the goal was ten new accounts, and I've brought in twelve. I'd like to talk about the raise we discussed."

> "I've done what we agreed. If the scope has changed, let's talk about what that means for the timeline."

**What not to do.** Don't silently chase the new goal. Every time you do, you teach the other person that the goalposts can move without cost.

## The ladder of small yeses

**What it looks like.** It starts with something tiny. Then each new request is only a little bigger than the last. A friend borrows twenty dollars, then fifty, then two hundred. A coworker asks you to cover one call, then a morning, then a week. A partner asks you to skip one evening with friends, then most evenings, then asks why you need friends at all.

**What it's for.** Each yes makes the next one easier, because you want to be consistent and each step is only slightly bigger than the last. By the time the requests are large, you've already agreed to so many smaller ones that refusing feels like a sudden change in character.

**The calm reply.** Look at the whole ladder, not just the next rung. Then stop at a clear point.

> "I've been happy to help with the last few loans. I'm not going to lend any more until the earlier ones are paid back."

> "I've covered several calls for you this month. I can't keep doing that. Let's figure out another plan."

**What not to do.** Don't judge each request in isolation. Ask yourself where this is heading, and whether you'd have agreed to the destination on day one.

## Scope creep

**What it looks like.** A task or role that keeps expanding without any formal decision. You were hired to manage social media, and now you also handle customer service, event planning, and the office kitchen. You agreed to drive your mother to her Tuesday appointments, and now you manage her medications, her bills, and her entire calendar. You offered to set up a friend's website, and now you're his permanent technical support.

**Where you'll meet it.** Work is the classic setting, but it's just as common with aging parents, adult siblings, and friends.

**What it's for.** Usually it isn't deliberate. Someone notices you're capable and willing, and hands you more. Nobody stops to ask whether this is fair, because you never say it isn't.

**The calm reply.** List what you're actually doing, and ask for a decision about it.

At work:

> "My role has grown quite a bit. I'm now covering customer service and events as well as social media. I'd like to talk about either adjusting my responsibilities or adjusting my title and pay."

In family:

> "I've been handling more and more of Mom's care. I'm happy to keep doing the appointments, but I need you to take over her bills and pharmacy."

**What not to do.** Don't wait until you're exhausted and resentful to raise it. The longer scope creep goes unaddressed, the more permanent it feels.

## Stolen credit

**What it looks like.** Your idea is repeated in a meeting by someone else, and they get the praise. Your work is presented by your manager as their own. A family member takes the thanks for a holiday you organized. A friend tells everyone how they "helped" with your project, when they barely took part.

**What it's for.** Sometimes it's simple carelessness. Often it's opportunism: you're unlikely to object, so your contributions become available for others to claim. Over time, others build reputations on your work while you stay invisible. Clause four of the contract from Chapter 2, "if you're good enough, you'll be treated fairly," makes this especially easy, because you're waiting to be noticed instead of making sure you are.

**The calm reply.** Reclaim the credit calmly, in the moment if you can, without accusing anyone.

> "Thanks, Sam, I'm glad you picked up on the idea I raised earlier. I'd love to walk everyone through how I'd put it into practice."

> "Just to add some detail, since I put the analysis together: the key finding was..."

Afterward, protect your work by making it visible. Send a short email summarizing your contributions. Present your own work where possible. Keep a running list of your achievements for your next review.

**What not to do.** Don't stay silent and seethe. The 3 a.m. replay was built for moments like this. A short, calm comment at the time is worth far more than a perfect speech you never deliver.

## "Temporary" that never ends

**What it sounds like.**

- "Just until I find a new place."
- "Only until we hire someone."
- "Just for a few weeks while things are crazy."
- "You can stop once I'm back on my feet."

**What it's for.** Temporary arrangements are easy to say yes to, because they have an end. The problem is that the end often never comes, and nobody ever formally extends the arrangement. It simply continues, and asking for it to stop starts to feel like breaking a promise.

**The calm reply.** Put a date on it at the start. If it's already gone on too long, set one now.

At the start:

> "I'm happy to help for a few weeks. Let's say until the end of March, and then we'll check in."

Later:

> "When you moved in, we said it would be a few weeks. It's been five months. I'd like you to find your own place by the end of next month."

**What not to do.** Don't wait for the other person to notice that "temporary" has expired. They usually won't.

## The unreturned loan

**What it looks like.** Money, belongings, or favors lent and never returned. The tools borrowed last summer. The money "just until payday." The favor you did that was supposed to be traded back. Each time you think about raising it, it feels awkward, so you don't. And the person keeps asking for more.

**What it's for.** Sometimes people genuinely forget. Sometimes they've learned that you won't follow up, so the loan quietly becomes a gift. Either way, the pattern drains you while teaching the other person that your resources are always available.

**The calm reply.** Follow up specifically, without apology, as you practiced on day 18 of the reset.

> "I wanted to check on the five hundred dollars from January. When will you be able to pay that back?"

> "Can you bring back the ladder this weekend? I need it."

If new requests come while old ones are outstanding:

> "I'm not able to lend more until the earlier loan is sorted out."

**What not to do.** Don't keep lending in the hope that the other person will one day repay you without being asked.

## Seeing the squeeze

Because the slow squeeze happens over months or years, it's hard to see from inside a single day. Each request seems reasonable. The pattern only becomes visible when you step back and look at the whole picture.

A useful habit is a quarterly review. Every few months, look at your work, your family obligations, and your friendships, and ask three questions:

1. What am I doing now that I wasn't doing a year ago?
2. Did I actually agree to it, or did it just accumulate?
3. If someone proposed this whole arrangement to me today, would I say yes?

If the answer to the third question is no, you've found a squeeze. You can then go back to the original agreement, name the gap, and reset.

## This week's exercise: the creep audit

Open your journal.

**Step one.** Make three columns: work, family, and friends.

**Step two.** In each column, list the ongoing things you do for other people. Be thorough. Include the small, invisible tasks.

**Step three.** Next to each item, mark whether you originally agreed to it as it is now (**A**), whether it grew from something smaller (**G**), or whether it was meant to be temporary (**T**).

**Step four.** Choose one **G** or **T** item. Write down the original agreement, what it has become, and the calm reset you'll propose. Then, this week, have that conversation.

When someone sends you a request that sounds small but feels like part of a larger pattern, paste it into the Decoder. It can help you see whether this is one favor or another rung on the ladder.

> *Field note: Nobody ever agrees to the whole squeeze. They agree to the first small step, then the next one, and then one day they look up and wonder how they got here. The way out is the same way in: one clear step at a time, in the other direction.*
