# Chapter 13: Withdrawal

Some of the most effective pressure involves no words at all.

A partner goes quiet for two days after you disagree. A friend stops replying to your messages after you decline an invitation. A parent answers the phone in a flat voice and says "I'm fine" in a way that makes it clear they aren't. A manager who used to stop by your desk now walks past without looking up.

Nobody has made a demand. Nobody has said what's wrong. And yet you can feel the pressure building, and you know exactly what would make it stop: give in, apologize, make it right. For many accommodating people, withdrawal is harder to resist than anger, because there's nothing to push back against. Just silence, and the growing sense that you've done something terrible.

This chapter covers the main forms of withdrawal and how to respond to them without either chasing or retaliating.

## Why withdrawal works

To understand why silence is so powerful, go back to the openings from Chapter 3. Withdrawal presses on several at once. It triggers fear of loss: maybe they're pulling away for good. It triggers guilt: you must have done something wrong. It triggers the need for approval: the warmth you rely on has been switched off, and you want it back.

It also leaves you guessing. When someone tells you directly why they're upset, you can respond to it. When they withdraw, you have to work out what's wrong yourself. Many accommodating people fill that gap with the worst possible explanation and then work very hard to fix a problem that may not even exist.

The deliberate version is sometimes called the silent treatment, and it's a well-recognized form of control in relationships. But plenty of people withdraw without much strategy. They're hurt, they don't know how to say so, and they go quiet because it's the only way they learned to show it. The replies below work for both.

## The silent treatment

**What it looks like.** Hours or days of not speaking, or speaking only in short, cold answers. Ignoring messages. Being physically present but emotionally absent. It often follows a disagreement, a boundary, or a no.

**Where you'll meet it.** Most often with partners and family. Sometimes with friends, roommates, and even coworkers.

**What it's for.** It makes the cost of displeasing them so uncomfortable that you'll do almost anything to end it. Often the silence only breaks when you apologize, even if you weren't the one who did something wrong. Over time, you learn to avoid anything that might trigger it.

**The calm reply.** Name it once, without accusation. Open a door. Then carry on with your life.

> "I can tell you're upset. When you're ready to talk about it, I'm here."

That's all. You don't need to say it again in an hour. You don't need to follow them from room to room, send a stream of messages, or apologize for something you didn't do.

If the silent treatment is a recurring pattern, raise it at a calm time, when the silence isn't happening:

> "When we disagree, you sometimes stop talking to me for a day or two. I'd rather we talk about what's wrong, even if it's hard. Going silent makes it impossible for me to fix anything."

**What not to do.** Don't chase. Every attempt to break through the silence by pleading, over-apologizing, or doing extra favors teaches the other person that silence gets results. Don't retaliate with your own silence, either. That turns it into a contest of who can hold out longer.

## "I'm fine"

**What it looks like.** You ask what's wrong. They say "Nothing" or "I'm fine," in a tone that makes it clear they are not fine, and that you should know why.

**What it's for.** It puts the work of figuring out the problem on you. If you guess wrong, you've failed. If you don't guess at all, you're insensitive. Either way, you end up apologizing and trying harder.

**The calm reply.** Take them at their word, kindly.

> "Okay. If that changes and you want to talk, let me know."

Then continue as normal. Don't keep asking, don't guess, and don't start apologizing for things they haven't mentioned.

This can feel cold the first few times you do it. It isn't. You're treating the other person as an adult who is able to say what they need. If they want something from you, they're capable of telling you.

## Going cold

**What it looks like.** It's less dramatic than full silence. The person is still talking to you, but the warmth is gone. Shorter messages. No "love you" at the end of the call. Fewer questions about your day. A colleague who used to chat now keeps everything strictly professional. A friend who used to share everything now gives one-word answers.

**What it's for.** It applies pressure gently and deniably. If you ask about it, they can say nothing has changed. Meanwhile, you feel the loss of warmth and start wondering what you did.

**The calm reply.** Mention what you've noticed, without accusation, and ask directly.

> "You seem a bit distant lately. Is something going on between us?"

If they say no, accept that for now. Then watch. If the coldness lifts when you comply with something and returns when you don't, you've learned what it's for.

**What not to do.** Don't increase your own warmth to win theirs back. Extra compliments, favors, and attention in response to coldness teach the same lesson as chasing the silent treatment.

## Withholding affection

**What it looks like.** In close relationships, affection is switched off as a consequence. No physical closeness, no kind words, no intimacy, until you do what they want or apologize for what they're upset about. Sometimes it's explicit: "I'm not in the mood to be nice to you right now." More often it's simply understood.

**What it's for.** It ties affection to compliance. Over time, you learn that love is something you earn by keeping the other person happy, and lose by disagreeing with them.

**The calm reply.** Name the pattern at a calm moment.

> "I've noticed that when we disagree, you pull away physically and emotionally until I apologize. I don't want our closeness to depend on me always agreeing with you."

If the pattern continues despite a calm conversation, it's worth taking seriously. Affection used consistently as a reward and punishment is one of the clearer signs of a controlling relationship. Talking with a counselor, either alone or together, can help.

## Ghosting and fading

**What it looks like.** A friend stops replying altogether. A date who seemed interested disappears. A relative stops inviting you to things. A colleague stops including you in meetings. There's no conversation, no explanation, just absence.

**What it's for.** Sometimes ghosting is simply avoidance. The person doesn't want to have a difficult conversation, so they disappear. Sometimes it's punishment. They want you to feel the loss and come back on their terms.

**The calm reply.** Send one clear, warm message. Then let them choose.

> "I've noticed we haven't been in touch lately. I value our friendship and I'd like to see you. If something's bothering you, I'm open to talking about it."

If there's no answer, let it go. You don't need to send a second, third, or fourth message. One clear door is enough.

At work, where absence affects your job, be more direct:

> "I noticed I wasn't included in the planning meetings this month. Can we talk about how I can stay involved?"

## The slow reply

**What it looks like.** A milder version, common in families and friendships, and very common at work. The person who usually replies quickly now takes days. Your questions go unanswered while they reply promptly to everyone else.

**What it's for.** Sometimes it's nothing. People get busy. But when it starts right after a disagreement or a no, and ends when you give in, it's a quiet form of withdrawal.

**The calm reply.** Don't chase. Wait a reasonable time, then follow up once, factually.

> "Just checking you saw my message about Saturday."

If it's work, set a clear deadline:

> "I'll need your answer by Thursday to keep the project on schedule."

## The deeper pattern

All forms of withdrawal share one feature. They make the relationship conditional. Warmth, contact, and affection are offered when you comply, and removed when you don't.

Healthy relationships have quiet periods too. People need space after an argument. People get busy. People have bad weeks. The difference is in the pattern. In healthy relationships, space is temporary and eventually followed by talking. In controlling ones, silence is a tool that keeps returning, especially after you set a boundary, and only ends when you give way.

Your job isn't to make the other person stop withdrawing. That's not in your control. Your job is to stop letting withdrawal change your decisions. When silence stops working, many people stop using it. When it keeps happening regardless, you've learned something important about the relationship, and you can decide what to do with that information.

## This week's exercise: the silence log

Open your journal.

**Step one.** List the people who withdraw from you most often. Next to each name, write how they usually do it: silent treatment, "I'm fine," going cold, withholding affection, ghosting, or slow replies.

**Step two.** For each person, write what usually ends the withdrawal. Do you apologize? Give in? Do something extra? Wait it out?

**Step three.** Write the calm reply you'll use next time, from this chapter.

**Step four.** The next time someone withdraws, use the reply once, then carry on with your normal routine. Each day, write a short note about how you felt and what happened. Pay attention to how long the withdrawal lasts when you don't chase it.

Many people find that withdrawal lasts much longer when they chase it and much shorter when they don't. Seeing that on paper is often the moment the pattern loses its grip.

> *Field note: Silence is only pressure if you treat it as a question you're required to answer. Once you stop rushing to fill it, it goes back to being what it really is: someone else choosing not to talk.*
